1. What the bond lecture covers

This is Lecture 3, Valuation of Bonds, in Prof. Dr. Thorsten Feix’s Valuing Securities and Companies course. The stated theme is applying the net present value (NPV) idea to debt securities.

The lecturer’s explicit objectives are to calculate bond values, connect prices to interest rates and yields, explain duration and volatility, understand the term structure and its competing explanations, distinguish real from nominal rates using Fisher’s relation, and evaluate default risk and ratings.

Opening breakout (slide 3)
  1. What is a bond?
  2. In what ways is it similar to or different from a loan?
  3. What payments does the investor receive after buying a bond?

These are questions posed by the slides. No spoken student answers or lecturer discussion was supplied.

Visual reference: slide 1 shows Wall Street signage; slide 2 pairs objectives with a footprint image; slide 3 uses financial trading imagery beside the three breakout questions. These images are contextual, not numerical evidence.

2. French government bond: cash flows and present value

In the French government bond case, an investor receives recurring coupon interest and then recovers principal at maturity together with the last coupon. The diagram explicitly separates the coupon stream from the principal repayment.

Here the face value is €100, the annual coupon is €5 and the maturity is five coupon periods. At the slide’s discount rate of 2.4%, the present-value expression is:

PV = 5/1.024 + 5/(1.024)² + 5/(1.024)³ + 5/(1.024)⁴ + 105/(1.024)⁵ ≈ €112.11.

Slide 5 rewrites the same calculation as two components: the present value of the coupon annuity (€23.30) plus the discounted €100 principal (€88.82). That is the central structural idea for ordinary fixed-coupon bonds.

PV = Coupon × [1/r − 1/(r(1+r)N)] + FV/(1+r)N
Original spreadsheet expression (slide 5): =PRICE(DATE(2024,1,1),DATE(2029,1,1),0.05,0.024,100,1). It is recorded here as source syntax, not verified as a fully specified current Excel workflow.

3. Yield to maturity: solving in reverse

Once a bond’s market price is known, slide 6 proposes reversing the present-value calculation to solve for yield to maturity (YTM), denoted y. Instead of inserting a required rate to find price, insert the observed price and find the single yield that makes the discounted payments add up.

€112.11 = 5/(1+y) + 5/(1+y)² + 5/(1+y)³ + 5/(1+y)⁴ + 105/(1+y)⁵

For this example, the underlying discount rate is about 2.4%. The source describes YTM as combining coupon receipts with the bond’s declining value over its remaining life, phrasing particular to this premium-price French example. Slide 6 highlights the separate discounted components using a series of arrows.

4. General bond valuation formulas

The following three slides state the general formula in expanded, summation and annuity-shortcut forms. They are different layouts of the same present-value logic.

PV = C₁/(1+r) + C₂/(1+r)² + … + (FV+CN)/(1+r)N
PV = Σt=1…N Ct/(1+r)t + FV/(1+r)N

Source notation: Ct is the coupon interest payment (given as coupon rate × face value), FV is face value, r is yield to maturity, and N is maturity. The summation spans coupon periods t=1 through N.

For level coupon payments, slide 9 gives the annuity shortcut:

PV(bond) = Coupon × [1/r − 1/(r(1+r)N)] + FV/(1+r)N

Visual reference: slides 7–9 place these formulas in large highlighted equation panels against a light-bulb motif. The panels do not introduce further empirical data.

Slide 16 introduces the inverse price–yield relationship through a screenshot of a U.S. Treasury yield-curve comparison. The legend distinguishes latest, 1 week ago and 1 month ago; maturities run from 1 month, 3 months and 6 months through 2, 5, 10 and 30 years. The vertical axis shows yields in percent, rising approximately from 4.0% to 6.0% in this historical chart. PV = 56.25/1.04 + 56.25/(1.04)² + 56.25/(1.04)³ + 56.25/(1.04)⁴ + 56.25/(1.04)⁵ + 1,056.25/(1.04)⁶

Duration and modified duration: definitions

Slide 19 links the inverse bond-price/yield curve to duration and volatility. Its illustration places bond price on the vertical axis (80–115) and interest rate on the horizontal axis (0%–10%), with a downward-sloping line; a dashed guide marks an example near a price of 98 and a yield of 5%.

The lecturer defines duration as a present-value-weighted average of payment times, with t running from 1 to N; the price is the sum of the present values of the entire bond cash-flow stream, including principal in the final payment.

Duration = Σt=1…N[t × PV(CFt)] / PV(bond)

Slide 21 expands this weighted average as [1 × PV(C₁) + 2 × PV(C₂) + 3 × PV(C₃) + … + T × PV(CT)] / PV. The same slide labels modified duration as volatility (%):

Modified duration = Volatility = Duration / (1 + yield)

Slide 19 also prints “Change in bond price = (Volatility) × (Change in interest rate)”. This is retained as slide notation; because the slide simultaneously asserts an inverse relation, the usual signed price-change expression requires a negative sign and a distinction between percentage price change and absolute price change.

Notation caveat: The slides' “volatility” denotes modified duration, a first-order interest-rate sensitivity; it is not a measured statistical standard deviation of returns. A later audit should preserve the lecturer's terminology while explaining the distinction.

Term structure, spot rates and the law of one price

Slide 26 defines the term structure of interest rates or yields as the relationship between interest rates and different maturities. The horizontal axis of its figure is bond maturity (years), about 0–30; the vertical axis is yield (%), about 3.5%–7.5%. Two historical U.S. examples are shown: the September 1992 curve slopes upward from shorter to longer maturities, while the April 2000 curve gently slopes downward, illustrating that short- and long-term rates are not always parallel. The slide states that in April 2000 short-term rates rose sharply while long-term rates declined.

Slide 27 introduces spot rates for separate maturity dates: r₁ is the one-year spot rate and r₂ the two-year spot rate, with PV discount factors 1/(1+r₁) and 1/(1+r₂)². The sequence r₁, r₂, r₃, …, rt traces the spot-rate term structure.

PV(1 at year 1) = 1/(1+r₁);   PV(1 at year 2) = 1/(1+r₂)²

For one dollar at each of the end of years 1 and 2 and r₁ = 3%, r₂ = 4%, the slide calculates:

PV = $1/1.03 + $1/(1.04)² ≈ $1.895

After the price is fixed, a single discount rate y may be solved from PV = 1/(1+y) + 1/(1+y)², giving a stated YTM of 3.66%.

Slide 28 explains the law of one price: the same commodity should have the same market price in a functioning market. It asserts that prices of interest-bearing instruments must fit the prevailing term structure, achieved through changes in asset prices, which then produce corresponding yields to maturity.

Spot-rate curves and competing term-structure theories

Slide 31 emphasizes that the spot rate rt is the interest rate for a zero-coupon security delivering one single payment at time t. The slide's historical example is titled Spot Rate on US Treasury TIPS, 02/2012. Its graph plots spot rate (%) vertically, about 0%–4%, against maturity (years) horizontally, roughly 1–29. Rates rise from near zero at the very shortest terms to approximately 3.4% at the far end, with progressively flatter slope. The date 02/2012 labels a historical picture, not today's market.

Slide 32 asks why an investor might prefer short-term Treasuries even when long-term bonds offer higher stated interest rates. It lists (1) expectations hypothesis: a belief short-term rates will be higher in future; (2) risk averseness: fear of the greater price exposure of long bonds when rates change; and (3) inflation-premium theory: concern about higher future inflation. These are three distinct classroom explanations for the yield curve.

Slide 33 revisits these theories: under the unbiased expectations theory, forward/long-term yields reflect expected subsequent short-term rates under the assumptions of that theory. If investors care about uncertainty in future real rates, they may choose maturities that match their investment horizons, so risk preferences could twist the term structure either way. If inflation is the key uncertainty, investors may demand additional compensation to hold long bonds, consistent with an inflation premium.

Case study: lock in two years or roll over after one year?

Slide 34 gives a one-year spot rate r₁ = 5% and a two-year spot rate r₂ = 7% per annum. Investing $100 for one year produces $105, while locking in two years produces $114.49.

Year-1 payoff = $100 × 1.05 = $105;   Year-2 payoff = $100 × (1.07)² = $114.49
Implied second-year forward rate = (1.07)² / 1.05 − 1 = 9.038...% ≈ 9%

The slide asks whether the investor would be happy earning the extra 9% in the second year of a two-year investment. This is a breakout question, not a statement of investor preferences. The 9% is an implied one-year forward rate, not a guaranteed future one-year spot rate.

Slide 35 states the equilibrium logic of expectations theory: investing in a sequence of shorter-maturity bonds must offer the same expected return as one longer-maturity bond, under the relevant simplifying assumptions. Its slide visual is a series of lightbulbs; no additional quantitative evidence is supplied there.

Real versus nominal cash flows and the Fisher relation

Slide 38 converts a future cash flow expressed in nominal currency into purchasing-power-adjusted real currency, under a constant inflation rate π over t periods. The printed formula is:

CFtreal = CFtnominal / (1 + π)t

It then relates the real rate of interest to nominal interest and inflation:

1 + rreal = (1 + rnominal) / (1 + π)

Slide 39 calls this the interest-rate formula and “Fischer effect”, and states that a change in expected inflation induces the corresponding proportional change in nominal required interest rates, without changing the required real interest rate. This is presented as a theoretical effect under its assumptions, not proof that real rates can never change in practice.

The slide explicitly distinguishes an accurate interest-rate transition formula from the rule-of-thumb Fisher equation. It prints the exact gross-return conversion above and the approximation below:

rreal ≈ rnominal − π

In the original, the approximation is displayed with an equals sign. It is recorded as a rule of thumb, whereas the fractional equation is the exact statement. Slides 2 and 39 use the spelling “Fischer”; slide 41 refers to the economist as Irving Fisher.
