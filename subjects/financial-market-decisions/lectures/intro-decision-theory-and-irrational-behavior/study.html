Source slides 1–7 · L001–L007 · compact teaching

Identify the rational economic decision-maker

If two choices serve your goal differently, what would a perfectly rational person choose?

1. Why start with self-interest?

Before explaining complicated markets, economists need a simple picture of the person making the choice. On slide 6, John Stuart Mill describes seeking the greatest amount of necessities, comforts and luxuries with the least labour and physical self-denial. Adam Smith points to the butcher, brewer and baker: we expect our dinner from their pursuit of their own interest, not simply their kindness. Both examples emphasize purposeful choices, although their original claims are not identical.

Worked example · ILLUSTRATIVE, not a lecturer calculation

A baker wants to maximize net money earned on one sale. Assume a customer will definitely buy at either listed price and that all other considerations are identical.

  1. Step 1. At a price of €5 and a cost of €3, earnings are €5 − €3 = €2.
  2. Step 2. At a price of €4 and the same €3 cost, earnings are €4 − €3 = €1.
  3. Step 3. Under this stated goal and these assumptions, choose the €5 price: €2 is €1 more net earnings than €1.

The slides give Smith’s baker, but no prices or costs. These numbers only illustrate his self-interest idea.

Quick check: Which people feature in Smith’s slide 6 example?

Answer without JavaScript

Butcher, brewer and baker. Smith points to self-interest, not charity, as the motive in this exchange.

2. The rational-agent assumption

Homo economicus (“economic man”) is the lecture’s model of a human as a decision-making agent who is always rational, self-interested, and motivated by subjective individual goals that they try to optimize. In plain terms, this person compares available actions and picks the one that best fits their own goals. “Self-interested” does not mean every person wants the same thing: someone might value free time more than extra earnings. Nor does the model prove that real people always choose consistently.

What the assumption says (slide 7)

Conceptual decision flow: the objective is individual; the choice follows the objective, not the crowd.

Quick check: Must two rational people want the same outcome?

Answer without JavaScript

No. The model says individual goals are subjective, but each person tries to optimize their own.

3. What the lecture sets out to compare

Slide 5 previews rational decision approaches, then four heuristic biases (mental shortcuts that can distort probability judgments) and four cognitive biases (systematic distortions of judgment). The opening chapter is called Introduction to Decision Theory and Irrational Behavior. Slide 4 invites students to two classroom experiments through pingo.coactum.de, session 937090, but the supplied PDF contains neither their questions nor their results. They cannot be reconstructed from this source.

Quick check: Can the results of the two opening experiments be inferred from the PDF?

Answer without JavaScript

No. Only the participation instructions and session code appear on the slide.

Remember
  • Homo economicus is an idealized model, not an observed description of everyone.
  • People try to optimize their own subjective goals under this assumption.
  • Mill emphasizes material benefit and effort; Smith illustrates exchange motivated by self-interest.
  • Watch out: rational choice does not mean everyone wants the same outcome.

Build a one-step decision model

You reach a fork in the forest. Which path is better if the weather could change?

1. List your actions and decide what matters

You cannot make a genuine choice unless there are at least two alternative actions. Slide 8 offers left or right at a forest fork. The activity (action) space is the set of all actions you could take. It can be finite (a limited list), countably infinite (options you could number one by one), or uncountably infinite (a continuum of possible values).

Next choose at least one target variable: the feature by which you judge results. The lecturer proposes ease of walking, shade or temperature. An easy walk and a cool walk need not be the same route. Different goals can make different actions attractive.

Quick check: In the forest example, which is a target variable?

Answer without JavaScript

Amount of shade. It measures what you care about. “Go left” is an action, while rain is an external scenario.

2. Connect actions, scenarios and results

Choosing a route does not guarantee its outcome. External scenarios, such as the weather or changes in the economic, ecological or social environment, can alter the result. The sample space describes the possible results. Slide 10’s one-step decision model begins with an action aᵢ at t = 0 and ends at t = T, when scenario zⱼ helps determine the result eᵢⱼ.

  2. Step 2. Scenarios: z₁ = dry weather; z₂ = rainy weather. Each path has a different time under each condition.
  3. Step 3. Assume e₁₁ = 20 min, e₁₂ = 40 min, e₂₁ = 25 min, e₂₂ = 30 min. Read one result from each row-column intersection in the table below.
  4. Step 4. If dry, left is 25 − 20 = 5 min faster. If rainy, right is 40 − 30 = 10 min faster. Therefore no path wins in both scenarios.

Quick check: In the illustrative matrix, what is e₁₂?

Answer without JavaScript

40 minutes. First subscript 1 means left path; second subscript 2 means rainy weather.

3. Classify what you know about the scenario

The lecturer distinguishes decisions under certainty (you know which result will occur), risk in the narrower sense (possible scenarios have known probabilities), and uncertainty (reliable probabilities are not available). The very same action-result matrix can appear in all three cases: what changes is your information about which scenario will occur. Under risk, probabilities help evaluate choices; under uncertainty, you cannot honestly supply them.

Quick check: You know rain has a 40% probability and dry weather 60%. What situation is this?

Answer without JavaScript

Risk in the narrower sense. The 40% and 60% probabilities are illustrative, not lecturer data; their availability makes this a risk situation.

Remember
  • A decision needs at least two actions and at least one target variable.
  • The action + scenario pair determines a possible result in the outcome matrix.
  • The model follows the choice from t = 0 to t = T; scenarios can be environmental, economic or social.
  • Watch out: no known probabilities means uncertainty, not automatically a 50/50 gamble.

Trace stock decisions over time

If you do nothing with a stock today, have you avoided making a decision?

1. Every trading choice affects the next one

Stock investing is a sequence of successive decisions, not necessarily one buy-and-finish event. At each date you may buy, sell or wait. The lecturer explicitly says that not buying or not selling is also a decision. An earlier action changes your later options: someone who bought a share can sell it, whereas someone who never bought has no share to sell, assuming short-selling is not allowed. A multi-step decision process captures this dependence across time rather than pretending every choice starts from scratch.

Quick check: You hold a share instead of selling it today. Does that count as a decision?

Answer without JavaScript

Yes. Not selling is explicitly listed as a decision on slide 11.

2. Follow the decisions through time

Slide 12’s diagram follows the activity space (available actions) through Step 1, Step 2, …, Step k. An action chosen at t₁ determines which actions you can take at t₂; a later choice at tₖ also contributes to the result at T. The arrows represent a path of choices. Relevant scenarios may affect the result at the decision dates t₁, …, tₖ and potentially throughout [0, T]. This is why one good decision cannot guarantee a good final result.

Slide 12: multi-step decision process, simplified

The original diagram has no numerical scale. The result depends on selected activities and relevant scenarios over time.

Worked example · ILLUSTRATIVE (not lecturer prices)

Start with €100 cash. One share costs €40 at t₁. Assume no short-selling, fees, dividends or interest.

  1. Step 1. At t₁, buy one share or keep your cash. Buying leaves €100 − €40 = €60 cash plus one share.
  2. Step 2. At t₂, suppose the share trades at €50 in a favorable scenario or €30 in an unfavorable one. If you bought, you can now sell or hold.
  3. Step 3. Buying then selling at €50 gives €60 + €50 = €110. Selling at €30 gives €60 + €30 = €90.
  4. Step 4. If you never bought, you still have €100 cash, but you cannot sell a share you do not own. Assume cash remains unchanged until T.

Quick check: After buying at €40 and selling at €30, how much cash do you have?

Answer without JavaScript

€90. €100 − €40 + €30 = €90, a €10 loss versus the start.

3. Markets add scenarios and competing goals

Future stock-price developments are uncertain. The lecturer names global and local economic conditions, political decisions at home and abroad, exchange rates, interest rates and the labour market as influences. A probability distribution assigns probabilities to possible future scenarios; the slides mention one but supply no actual figures. Investors may also track several target variables, not just one. The next topic’s magic triangle organizes three common financial goals.

Quick check: Why is the final outcome not controlled by your trades alone?

Answer without JavaScript

Outside scenarios also shape future prices. Actions and relevant future developments together determine the result.

Remember
  • Investment decisions unfold as a sequence.
  • Doing nothing is also a decision.
  • Final results depend on chosen actions and relevant scenarios.
  • Watch out: the actions available later can depend on earlier decisions.

Balance liquidity, return and risk

Can an investment maximize easy access to cash, high return and low risk all at once?

What the slide establishes

The lecture’s magic triangle connects three target variables: liquidity, return and risk. The slide says fixed cash flows make meeting obligations more predictable but limit benefits from favorable market-price changes, while variable cash flows can fluctuate. It also notes that, for efficient portfolios, higher expected returns come with higher market risk. The diagram is a qualitative trade-off, not a numeric optimization rule.

Compare risky choices using preference axioms

Can your preferences be consistent enough to turn into a formula?

What the slides establish

The lecture writes A > B for preference, A = B for indifference, and A ≥ B for weak preference. Its four axioms are completeness, transitivity, continuity and independence. Continuity compares an intermediate option B to a lottery mixing A and C; independence says adding the same lottery component to two choices should not reverse their order. If the four axioms hold, the von Neumann–Morgenstern result represents preferences with an individual utility function u(·), and choices can be compared using E[u(A)] and E[u(B)]. Utility also encodes risk appetite.

Four axioms (slide 16)
CompletenessCompare any A and B
TransitivityIf A > B and B > C, then A > C
ContinuityA suitable lottery between A and C can match B
IndependenceA common lottery component preserves preference ordering

Distinguish cooperative games and minimax choices

What changes when your payoff depends on somebody else’s move?

What the slides establish

Game theory studies interdependent choices. The slides distinguish co-operative from non-cooperative games and introduce John von Neumann (1903–1957), his 1928 minimax theorem, and the 1944 book Theory of Games and Economic Behavior with Oskar Morgenstern. The lecture states the minimax idea as choosing strategies that minimize maximum losses in zero-sum games and notes extensions to imperfect information and more than two players. The precise scope of the stated theorem is flagged for later fact checking.

Source slides 18 · L021 · structured draft

Explain Nash equilibrium in non-cooperative games

Why might nobody change strategy, even if everybody dislikes the outcome?

What the slides establish

John F. Nash Jr. (1928–2015), jointly awarded the 1994 Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences with Reinhard Selten and John C. Harsanyi, is associated in the lecture with analysis of equilibria in non-cooperative games. In a Nash equilibrium, no player has an incentive to change strategy unilaterally while the other players keep their strategies. The slide also contrasts games that allow binding agreements with those that do not. The prisoner’s dilemma appears as a later discussion question, not an explained numerical example.

Source slides 19–20 · L022, L023 · structured draft

Distinguish three forms of market efficiency

If prices already reflect information, what edge is left to a trader?

What the slides establish

The efficient market hypothesis (EMH) treats asset prices as reflecting information. The slide distinguishes weak-form efficiency (past trading information), semi-strong efficiency (other public information too) and strong-form efficiency (even insider information). It concludes that traders cannot systematically outperform the market. Eugene Fama (born 1939) received the 2013 Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences jointly with Lars Peter Hansen and Robert C. Shiller for empirical analysis of asset prices. Later teaching should distinguish these forms and qualify the outperformance claim.

Part 3 · Shortcuts in probability judgments

Learn why quick mental estimates can misread stereotypes, vivid news, initial numbers and random streaks.

Source slides 21–23 · L024, L025, L026 · structured draft

Explain why finance needs psychology

Why can clever people still make predictable money mistakes?

What the slides establish

The lecture pivots from rational models to psychology through Daniel Kahneman (1934–2024) and Amos Tversky (1937–1996). Kahneman shared the 2002 Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences with Vernon L. Smith for integrating psychological insights into economics, particularly uncertainty. Richard Thaler (born 1945), the 2017 laureate, studied limited rationality, perceived fairness or reasonableness, and weak self-control. Their role here is to explain patterns the idealized rational-agent model misses.

Source slides 24–26 · L027, L028, L029 · structured draft

Spot representativeness and ignored base rates

Does someone who sounds like a librarian actually have to be one?

What the slides establish

A heuristic is a shortcut for estimating uncertain outcomes. The lecture names four: representativeness, availability, anchoring and gambler’s fallacy. In its classic person-description problem, the subject is shy, tidy, meek and order-loving; the choices are farmer, librarian, physician, salesperson and airline pilot. Representativeness means judging likelihood by resemblance to a stereotypical category or process. The danger is neglecting base rates, the underlying frequency of each category. A description that resembles a librarian is not, by itself, evidence that librarian is the likeliest profession.

Source slides 27 · L030 · structured draft

Recognize the availability shortcut

If a stock crash dominates the news, does that make another crash more probable?

What the slides establish

The availability heuristic estimates event likelihood from how easily similar episodes come to mind. The lecture warns that current or recently prominent information gets overweighted. Ease of recall and the actual probability of an event are different things.

Source slides 28 · L031 · structured draft

Catch anchoring in prices and estimates

Can a random number shift your financial estimate?

What the slides establish

With anchoring, an initial piece of information becomes a reference point, and later judgments remain linked to it. The slide uses the opening asking price for a used car and even an entirely random wheel-of-fortune outcome as examples. The number can affect a later judgment without deserving any evidential weight.

Source slides 29 · L032 · structured draft

Check whether a streak changes the next odds

After five losses, are you finally due a win?

What the slides establish

The gambler’s fallacy is the mistaken expectation that an unusually frequent outcome must become less frequent next (or vice versa). The slide’s crucial condition is that trials are random and independent. Under that condition a process does not correct its past streaks by changing the probability of the next trial.

Part 4 · Investor biases and consequences

Link judgment errors and emotions to trading decisions, market movements and the lecture’s final discussion prompts.

Source slides 30–31 · L033, L034 · structured draft

Identify three forms of overconfidence

Are you as accurate as you feel?

What the slides establish

A cognitive bias is a systematic departure from a rationality or judgment benchmark. The lecture lists overconfidence, herd effect, overreaction and regret aversion. Overconfidence makes subjective certainty higher than objective accuracy. The slide distinguishes overestimation of own performance, overplacement relative to others and overprecision in how certain one feels about a belief.

Source slides 32 · L035 · structured draft

Explain how herding amplifies market moves

Would you buy the same stock if nobody else was buying it?

What the slides establish

Herd behavior means decisions move with peers, often for emotional reasons rather than independent analysis. The slide also calls this mob, pack or gang mentality. It links collective greed and fear to the potential worsening of asset bubbles and market crashes. A herd-influenced investor may make a choice unlike the one they would make alone.

Source slides 33 · L036 · structured draft

Recognize overreaction to market news

Can investors react so strongly to news that prices overshoot?

What the slides establish

Overreaction bias is an emotional response to new information, such as company news, driven by greed or fear. The slide describes securities becoming overbought or oversold relative to their intrinsic value and later moving back toward that value. This is the lecturer’s suggested mechanism; it is not a guarantee that a particular asset will revert on schedule.

Source slides 34 · L037 · structured draft

Distinguish regret from action and inaction

Is the regret of buying a loser different from missing a winner?

What the slides establish

Regret aversion is fear of learning later that a decision was wrong. The slides contrast an error of omission (not buying an asset that turns out to be right) with an error of commission (buying the wrong asset). Both can change investment behavior before the outcome is known.

Source slides 35–38 · L038, L039, L040, L041 · structured draft

Connect investor biases to market outcomes

Can a market follow mathematical rules when its participants have emotions?

What the slides establish

The lecturer concludes that probability judgments can be seriously distorted and that greed and fear can influence investing more strongly than formal models suggest. According to the slide, these patterns can amplify market movements and crises. The final three slides pose discussion prompts on one-step and multi-step models, the magic triangle, endogenous scenarios, mathematical versus psychological theory, the prisoner’s dilemma and all eight named biases. The slides pose these questions but do not supply worked answers.
