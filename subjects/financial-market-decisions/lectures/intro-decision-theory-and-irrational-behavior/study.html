Identify the rational economic decision-maker

If two choices serve your goal differently, what would a perfectly rational person choose?

1. Why start with self-interest?

Before explaining complicated markets, economists need a simple picture of the person making the choice. On slide 6, John Stuart Mill describes seeking the greatest amount of necessities, comforts and luxuries with the least labour and physical self-denial. Adam Smith points to the butcher, brewer and baker: we expect our dinner from their pursuit of their own interest, not simply their kindness. Both examples emphasize purposeful choices, although their original claims are not identical.

Worked example · ILLUSTRATIVE, not a lecturer calculation

A baker wants to maximize net money earned on one sale. Assume a customer will definitely buy at either listed price and that all other considerations are identical.

  1. Step 1. At a price of €5 and a cost of €3, earnings are €5 − €3 = €2.
  2. Step 2. At a price of €4 and the same €3 cost, earnings are €4 − €3 = €1.
  3. Step 3. Under this stated goal and these assumptions, choose the €5 price: €2 is €1 more net earnings than €1.

The slides give Smith’s baker, but no prices or costs. These numbers only illustrate his self-interest idea.

Quick check: Which people feature in Smith’s slide 6 example?

Answer without JavaScript

Butcher, brewer and baker. Smith points to self-interest, not charity, as the motive in this exchange.

2. The rational-agent assumption

Homo economicus (“economic man”) is the lecture’s model of a human as a decision-making agent who is always rational, self-interested, and motivated by subjective individual goals that they try to optimize. In plain terms, this person compares available actions and picks the one that best fits their own goals. “Self-interested” does not mean every person wants the same thing: someone might value free time more than extra earnings. Nor does the model prove that real people always choose consistently.

What the assumption says (slide 7)

Conceptual decision flow: the objective is individual; the choice follows the objective, not the crowd.

Quick check: Must two rational people want the same outcome?

Answer without JavaScript

No. The model says individual goals are subjective, but each person tries to optimize their own.

Slide 5 previews rational decision approaches, then four heuristic biases (mental shortcuts that can distort probability judgments) and four cognitive biases (systematic distortions of judgment). The opening chapter is called Introduction to Decision Theory and Irrational Behavior. Slide 4 invites students to two classroom experiments through pingo.coactum.de, session 937090, but the supplied PDF contains neither their questions nor their results. They cannot be reconstructed from this source.

Quick check: Can the results of the two opening experiments be inferred from the PDF?

Answer without JavaScript

No. Only the participation instructions and session code appear on the slide.

Remember
  • Homo economicus is an idealized model, not an observed description of everyone.
  • People try to optimize their own subjective goals under this assumption.
  • Mill emphasizes material benefit and effort; Smith illustrates exchange motivated by self-interest.
  • Watch out: rational choice does not mean everyone wants the same outcome.

Build a one-step decision model

You reach a fork in the forest. Which path is better if the weather could change?

1. List your actions and decide what matters

You cannot make a genuine choice unless there are at least two alternative actions. Slide 8 offers left or right at a forest fork. The activity (action) space is the set of all actions you could take. It can be finite (a limited list), countably infinite (options you could number one by one), or uncountably infinite (a continuum of possible values).

Next choose at least one target variable: the feature by which you judge results. The lecturer proposes ease of walking, shade or temperature. An easy walk and a cool walk need not be the same route. Different goals can make different actions attractive.

Quick check: In the forest example, which is a target variable?

Answer without JavaScript

Amount of shade. It measures what you care about. “Go left” is an action, while rain is an external scenario.

2. Connect actions, scenarios and results

Choosing a route does not guarantee its outcome. External scenarios, such as the weather or changes in the economic, ecological or social environment, can alter the result. The sample space describes the possible results. Slide 10’s one-step decision model begins with an action aᵢ at t = 0 and ends at t = T, when scenario zⱼ helps determine the result eᵢⱼ.

Quick check: In the illustrative matrix, what is e₁₂?

3. Classify what you know about the scenario

The lecturer distinguishes decisions under certainty (you know which result will occur), risk in the narrower sense (possible scenarios have known probabilities), and uncertainty (reliable probabilities are not available). The very same action-result matrix can appear in all three cases: what changes is your information about which scenario will occur. Under risk, probabilities help evaluate choices; under uncertainty, you cannot honestly supply them.

Quick check: You know rain has a 40% probability and dry weather 60%. What situation is this?

Answer without JavaScript

Risk in the narrower sense. The 40% and 60% probabilities are illustrative, not lecturer data; their availability makes this a risk situation.

Remember
  • A decision needs at least two actions and at least one target variable.
  • The action + scenario pair determines a possible result in the outcome matrix.
  • The model follows the choice from t = 0 to t = T; scenarios can be environmental, economic or social.
  • Watch out: no known probabilities means uncertainty, not automatically a 50/50 gamble.

Slides 11–12 · L012, L013, L014 · compact teaching

Trace stock decisions over time

If you do nothing with a stock today, have you avoided making a decision?

1. Every trading choice affects the next one

Stock investing is a sequence of successive decisions, not necessarily one buy-and-finish event. At each date you may buy, sell or wait. The lecturer explicitly says that not buying or not selling is also a decision. An earlier action changes your later options: someone who bought a share can sell it, whereas someone who never bought has no share to sell, assuming short-selling is not allowed. A multi-step decision process captures this dependence across time rather than pretending every choice starts from scratch.

Quick check: You hold a share instead of selling it today. Does that count as a decision?

Answer without JavaScript

Yes. Not selling is explicitly listed as a decision on slide 11.

2. Follow the decisions through time

Slide 12’s diagram follows the activity space (available actions) through Step 1, Step 2, …, Step k. An action chosen at t₁ determines which actions you can take at t₂; a later choice at tₖ also contributes to the result at T. The arrows represent a path of choices. Relevant scenarios may affect the result at the decision dates t₁, …, tₖ and potentially throughout [0, T]. This is why one good decision cannot guarantee a good final result.

Slide 12: multi-step decision process, simplified

The original diagram has no numerical scale. The result depends on selected activities and relevant scenarios over time.

Worked example · ILLUSTRATIVE (not lecturer prices)

Start with €100 cash. One share costs €40 at t₁. Assume no short-selling, fees, dividends or interest.

  1. Step 1. At t₁, buy one share or keep your cash. Buying leaves €100 − €40 = €60 cash plus one share.
  2. Step 2. At t₂, suppose the share trades at €50 in a favorable scenario or €30 in an unfavorable one. If you bought, you can now sell or hold.
  3. Step 3. Buying then selling at €50 gives €60 + €50 = €110. Selling at €30 gives €60 + €30 = €90.
  4. Step 4. If you never bought, you still have €100 cash, but you cannot sell a share you do not own. Assume cash remains unchanged until T.

Quick check: After buying at €40 and selling at €30, how much cash do you have?

Answer without JavaScript

€90. €100 − €40 + €30 = €90, a €10 loss versus the start.

3. Markets add scenarios and competing goals

Future stock-price developments are uncertain. The lecturer names global and local economic conditions, political decisions at home and abroad, exchange rates, interest rates and the labour market as influences. A probability distribution assigns probabilities to possible future scenarios; the slides mention one but supply no actual figures. Investors may also track several target variables, not just one. The next topic’s magic triangle organizes three common financial goals.

Quick check: Why is the final outcome not controlled by your trades alone?

Answer without JavaScript

Outside scenarios also shape future prices. Actions and relevant future developments together determine the result.

Remember
  • Investment decisions unfold as a sequence.
  • Doing nothing is also a decision.
  • Final results depend on chosen actions and relevant scenarios.
  • Watch out: the actions available later can depend on earlier decisions.

Slide 13 · L015 · compact teaching

Balance liquidity, return and risk

Would you choose an investment with high returns if the cash arrives after your bills are due?

1. Why there are three goals, not just profit

The slide’s magic triangle of financial management links three target variables for an investment decision. Liquidity means having cash available when payments are due; return means the financial gain from investing; risk means the possibility that results differ unfavorably from expectations. An attractive return is not very helpful when you cannot pay an obligation on time. The triangle’s double arrows mean these objectives interact. It is a qualitative decision aid, not a formula claiming every investment follows a fixed trade-off.

Slide 13, redrawn: the magic triangle

No numerical axes, units or promised correlations appear on the original triangle; arrows mark interacting aims.

Quick check: Which triangle objective matters most for paying a bill tomorrow?

Answer without JavaScript

Liquidity. Having enough cash on the payment date matters, regardless of future potential gains.

2. Why predictable cash flows affect liquidity

On the liquidity–return side, the lecturer states that fixed cash flows help fulfill financial obligations but mean you may miss positive market-price developments. On the liquidity–risk side, he contrasts this with variable cash flows, which make financial planning harder because payments fluctuate. The timing of cash receipts matters alongside their total amount; predictable receipts are easier to match to expenses. Fixed future payments, however, do not by themselves guarantee immediately available cash.

Worked example · ILLUSTRATIVE, not lecturer cash-flow data

You start with €0 cash. You owe €400 in each of two periods. Compare receipts from two arrangements; ignore borrowing fees and interest.

  1. Step 1. Fixed receipts: €500 in period 1 and €500 in period 2. After paying €400 each time, the cumulative cash balances are €100 and €200.
  2. Step 2. Variable receipts: €300 then €700. After period 1, €300 − €400 = −€100: there is a €100 shortfall that needs funding.
  3. Step 3. Total receipts for either plan are €1,000. Both would finish with €1,000 − €800 = €200 after two periods if the temporary shortfall is financed at zero cost.
  4. Step 4. The difference is when cash arrives. Equal total receipts do not imply equal ability to pay on time.

*Period 2 assumes temporary financing of the first shortfall with no interest or fees. The original slide supplies no amounts.

Quick check: Why is the variable plan harder to manage in the example?

Answer without JavaScript

The first-period shortfall. Both plans receive €1,000 overall, but €300 is insufficient to cover the first €400 bill.

3. Why return and risk are linked

The bottom edge of slide 13 says that, with efficient portfolios (portfolios offering the best available expected return for a given level of risk), a higher expected return comes at the price of higher market risk. That describes the lecturer’s investment-choice trade-off, not a guarantee that taking more risk always earns more money. Compare expected outcomes and risk together rather than treating an ambitious return forecast as certain.

Fact check on the original slide: It also claims variable cash-flow instruments “do not face any market risk (at least in theory).” That is not generally correct: variable cash flows do not eliminate market-price or valuation risk. The claim is preserved here as the lecturer’s wording, not as a financial rule.

Quick check: Does accepting more market risk guarantee a higher realized return?

Answer without JavaScript

No. Higher expected return at higher risk is not a promise about what will actually happen.

Remember
  • The three goals are liquidity, return and risk.
  • Cash-flow timing affects whether you can meet obligations.
  • The slide links higher expected returns of efficient portfolios with higher market risk.
  • Watch out: neither fixed cash flows nor variable cash flows automatically remove all risk.

Compare risky choices using preference axioms

Would you choose a certain €36 or a 60% chance of €100?

1. First, make preferences consistent

Suppose two investments appeal to you differently. Can you always compare them, and do your comparisons make sense together? The lecturer writes A > B when you prefer A, A = B when you are indifferent, and A ≥ B when you prefer A or are indifferent to B. These symbols describe preferences, not necessarily numerical comparisons of euros. Four axioms (conditions imposed on preference rankings) make them logically consistent.

Slide 16: four preference axioms and original notation
AxiomSlide’s relationshipIn plain words
Completeness∀ A, B: (A = B) ∨ (A > B) ∨ (B > A)For any pair, you can compare them.
Transitivity(A > B) ∧ (B > C) ⇒ A > C
(A = B) ∧ (B = C) ⇒ A = C
(A ≥ B) ∧ (B ≥ C) ⇒ A ≥ C		Preference, indifference and weak-preference rankings stay consistent.
ContinuityA ≥ B ≥ C ⇒ ∃ p ∈ (0,1):
p·A + (1−p)·C = B		A suitable lottery between best and worst can feel equivalent to the middle choice.
IndependenceA ≥ B ⇒ ∀ C, p ∈ (0,1):
p·A + (1−p)·C ≥ p·B + (1−p)·C		Mixing the same alternative into both choices preserves their ordering.

Here ∀ means “for all”; ∃ means “there exists”; ∧ means “and”; ∨ means “or”; ⇒ means “implies”. The plus signs in lotteries denote probability mixtures, not ordinary addition of payouts.

Quick check: You prefer A to B and B to C. Which axiom requires A > C?

Answer without JavaScript

Transitivity: A > B and B > C imply A > C.

2. Treat a lottery as a choice, not an arithmetic average

For continuity, if A is at least as good as B, and B at least as good as C, there is a probability p strictly between 0 and 1 at which the lottery p·A + (1−p)·C is indifferent to B. Slide 16 writes “= B”: it means equally preferred, not the same cash payout. For independence, mixing the same C into A and B with the same probability must not reverse their ordering.

Quick check: In p·A + (1−p)·C = B, what does “=” mean?

Answer without JavaScript

Indifference. The mixture and B can have different outcomes yet receive the same preference ranking.

3. Turn those preferences into expected utility

The von Neumann–Morgenstern theorem on slide 17 says that if all four axioms hold, an individual utility function u(·) can represent that preference order. It maps possible results, whether one-dimensional or multidimensional, to real numbers and implicitly captures risk appetite. For a risky choice, compare expected utility rather than merely expected euros:

Lecture: A > B ⇔ E(u(A)) > E(u(B)). For discrete outcomes: E[u(A)] = ∑ pⱼ·u(xⱼ).

xⱼ is an outcome (such as a euro payout); pⱼ is its probability (unitless, summing to 1); u(xⱼ) is the individual’s utility score; E means probability-weighted expectation; ⇔ means both directions. The summation is an explanatory rewrite of the lecturer’s expected-utility notation.

Worked example · ILLUSTRATIVE, not lecturer numbers

Assume A = €100 for sure, B = €36 for sure, C = €0 for sure, and your utility is u(x) = √x with x measured in euros. The utility scores are invented for illustration.

  1. Step 1. Calculate utilities: u(A) = √100 = 10, u(B) = √36 = 6, u(C) = √0 = 0. Therefore A > B > C.
  2. Step 2. Mix A with probability p = 0.60 and C with probability 0.40: E[u] = 0.60 × 10 + 0.40 × 0 = 6. That equals u(B) = 6, illustrating continuity as indifference.
  3. Step 3. For independence, mix A and B separately with the same C at p = 0.50. The utilities are 0.50 × 10 + 0.50 × 0 = 5 and 0.50 × 6 + 0.50 × 0 = 3. The ordering still favors A.
  4. Step 4. A 50% chance of €100 and 50% of €0 gives expected utility 5; certain €36 gives 6. This particular utility function therefore favors the certain €36.

Quick check: With u(x) = √x, what is expected utility for a 50% chance of €100 and 50% of €0?

Answer without JavaScript

5 utility points. 0.50 × √100 + 0.50 × √0 = 0.50 × 10 + 0 = 5.

Remember
  • Completeness and transitivity make rankings comparable and consistent.
  • Continuity and independence discipline preferences involving lotteries.
  • Under all four axioms, expected utility can represent individual preferences and risk appetite.
  • Watch out: equality of utility or indifference is not equality of possible cash payouts.

Slides 14–15 · L016, L017 · compact teaching

Distinguish cooperative games and minimax choices

If your opponent gets to react, which choice keeps your worst possible loss under control?

1. Why another person's choices matter

Choosing an investment can depend on what someone else does. Game theory studies choices where each participant's result depends partly on others' actions. Slide 14 separates co-operative games from non-cooperative games. The practical distinction developed later in the lecture is whether players can form binding agreements or instead must choose strategies without enforceable deals. Non-cooperative does not mean people can never help one another; it means their strategic choices are analyzed without binding coordination. See the next Nash equilibrium topic for how such choices can settle.

Quick check: Which distinction matters for classifying these games?

Answer without JavaScript

Binding agreements. A non-cooperative game can still involve helpful actions, but not enforceable coordination in the model.

2. Protect against the worst case: minimax

Slide 15 introduces a zero-sum game: one player's gain is the other's loss. It summarizes the minimax theorem as choosing strategies that “minimize maximum losses” and describes a two-player game with perfect information, meaning participants know relevant earlier moves. The useful decision rule is to look at your worst result under each available strategy. A player maximizing their guaranteed payoff follows a maximin approach; the opponent minimizes the largest payoff they might allow you. In the illustrative matrix below, these choices coincide at one outcome.

ILLUSTRATIVE zero-sum matrix: Player A's payoff (points)
A's strategy ↓ / B's strategy →B: LeftB: RightWorst for A
A: Safe+2+1+1
A: Bold+4−3−3
Largest A payoff in column+4+1

A's payoff is in points, not euro returns; B receives the opposite payoff. The lecture gives no numerical matrix. Best guaranteed payoff for A: +1; least maximum payoff B can permit: +1.

Worked example · ILLUSTRATIVE (not a lecturer calculation)
  1. Step 1. Read A's worst payoff in each row: Safe gives min(+2, +1) = +1; Bold gives min(+4, −3) = −3.
  2. Step 2. A chooses Safe, maximizing the worst-case outcome: max(+1, −3) = +1.
  3. Step 3. B checks A's best payoff in each column: Left allows max(+2, +4) = +4; Right allows max(+1, −3) = +1. B chooses Right.
  4. Step 4. The resulting pair is (Safe, Right). A earns +1 point and B loses 1 point. Neither can improve by changing strategy alone in this example.

Quick check: Which strategy has A's better worst-case outcome?

Answer without JavaScript

Safe. Compare the smaller number in each row: +1 is higher than −3.

3. What von Neumann contributed

John von Neumann (1903–1957) proved his celebrated minimax result in 1928. With Oskar Morgenstern, he published Theory of Games and Economic Behavior in 1944, a foundational mathematical treatment of game theory. The lecture says the approach was later improved or extended to games with imperfect information and games involving more than two players. Those are different settings, and the straightforward two-player minimax conclusion should not be copied into every game.

Fact check: Slide 15's wording compresses several developments. The classical minimax theorem concerns two-player zero-sum games and can use mixed strategies; it is not restricted to perfect-information games. General games involving more than two players do not automatically satisfy the same minimax equality.

Quick check: Does the same two-player minimax rule automatically solve any game with three players?

Answer without JavaScript

No. Different game structures need separate analysis. The classical result is for two-player zero-sum games.

Remember
  • Game theory studies decisions whose outcomes depend on other players.
  • Co-operative and non-cooperative models differ in their treatment of binding agreements.
  • Minimax protects against a worst-case opponent in a zero-sum setting.
  • Watch out: the original minimax theorem does not automatically solve every multiplayer game.

Explain Nash equilibrium in non-cooperative games

Two firms could both earn more by keeping prices high. Why might both cut prices anyway?

1. Your best move depends on the other player

In non-cooperative game theory, each player chooses a strategy without relying on a binding agreement with the others. Unlike an investment whose outcome depends only on outside scenarios, here another decision-maker can react strategically. Slide 18 contrasts games with binding agreements and those without. A Nash equilibrium is a combination of strategies where no player gains by changing their own strategy alone, given what everyone else is doing. That says the choices are stable against unilateral changes, not that the outcome is ideal for society or even for the players together.

Quick check: What makes an outcome a Nash equilibrium?

Answer without JavaScript

No profitable unilateral change. Others' strategies are held fixed when checking each player's incentive.

2. Find an equilibrium in the payoff matrix

Read the payoffs for both players, one player at a time. A best response is the choice that gives a player the highest payoff when the other player's move is fixed. An equilibrium occurs where the players' best responses meet. The lecture does not give a numerical game on slide 18; the following pricing example is ILLUSTRATIVE. It has the same incentive pattern often discussed in a prisoner's dilemma, which the lecturer asks about on slide 36.

ILLUSTRATIVE price-competition game: payoff points (Firm A, Firm B)
Firm A ↓ / Firm B →High priceLow price
High price(4, 4)(0, 6)
Low price(6, 0)(2, 2) · Nash equilibrium

Each pair is (A's payoff, B's payoff), in illustrative points, not euros or probabilities. Larger is better. The original slide has no payoff matrix.

Worked example · ILLUSTRATIVE, not a lecturer calculation
  1. Step 1. If B keeps a high price, A earns 4 from High versus 6 from Low. A chooses Low.
  2. Step 2. If B cuts to Low, A earns 0 from High versus 2 from Low. A still chooses Low.
  3. Step 3. By symmetry, B also chooses Low whether A picks High (6 > 4) or Low (2 > 0).
  4. Step 4. Both choose Low and earn (2, 2). Switching alone would reduce either player's payoff from 2 to 0. Both choosing High gives (4, 4), but either player could improve alone from 4 to 6 by cutting price, so it is not an equilibrium.

Quick check: If B chooses a low price, what is A's best response?

Answer without JavaScript

Low price. Comparing A's payoffs in the Low column: 2 is better than 0.

3. Stable does not mean mutually best

Notice the uncomfortable result: (4, 4) gives each firm more than (2, 2), yet it invites either firm to cut its own price. The Nash condition checks individual incentives, not whether both would be happier elsewhere. This is why binding agreements and their absence matter in classifying strategic situations, and why the prisoner’s dilemma is useful to discuss. Do not confuse Nash equilibrium with the two-player zero-sum minimax case: this matrix is not zero-sum.

John F. Nash Jr. (1928–2015) pioneered equilibrium analysis of non-cooperative games. He shared the 1994 Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences with Reinhard Selten and John C. Harsanyi, as the lecture records.

Quick check: Why is (4, 4) not an equilibrium in the illustrative matrix?

Answer without JavaScript

Profitable unilateral deviation. A move from High to Low when the other stays High raises the mover's payoff from 4 to 6.

Remember
  • A Nash equilibrium has no profitable unilateral deviation.
  • Find it by checking each player's best response.
  • A stable equilibrium may be worse for both players than another outcome.
  • Watch out: Nash equilibrium does not mean maximum total payoff or a binding agreement.

Distinguish three forms of market efficiency

If prices already reflect information, what edge is left to a trader?

What the slides establish

The efficient market hypothesis (EMH) treats asset prices as reflecting information. The slide distinguishes weak-form efficiency (past trading information), semi-strong efficiency (other public information too) and strong-form efficiency (even insider information). Shiller for empirical analysis of asset prices. Later teaching should distinguish these forms and qualify the outperformance claim.

Spot representativeness and ignored base rates

Does someone who sounds like a librarian actually have to be one?

What the slides establish

A heuristic is a shortcut for estimating uncertain outcomes. The lecture names four: representativeness, availability, anchoring and gambler’s fallacy. In its classic person-description problem, the subject is shy, tidy, meek and order-loving; the choices are farmer, librarian, physician, salesperson and airline pilot. Representativeness means judging likelihood by resemblance to a stereotypical category or process. The danger is neglecting base rates, the underlying frequency of each category. A description that resembles a librarian is not, by itself, evidence that librarian is the likeliest profession.

Recognize the availability shortcut

If a stock crash dominates the news, does that make another crash more probable?

What the slides establish

The availability heuristic estimates event likelihood from how easily similar episodes come to mind. The lecture warns that current or recently prominent information gets overweighted. Ease of recall and the actual probability of an event are different things.

Catch anchoring in prices and estimates

Can a random number shift your financial estimate?

What the slides establish

With anchoring, an initial piece of information becomes a reference point, and later judgments remain linked to it. The slide uses the opening asking price for a used car and even an entirely random wheel-of-fortune outcome as examples. The number can affect a later judgment without deserving any evidential weight.

Source slides 29 · L032 · structured draft

Check whether a streak changes the next odds

After five losses, are you finally due a win?

What the slides establish

The gambler’s fallacy is the mistaken expectation that an unusually frequent outcome must become less frequent next (or vice versa). The slide’s crucial condition is that trials are random and independent. Under that condition a process does not correct its past streaks by changing the probability of the next trial.

Identify three forms of overconfidence

Are you as accurate as you feel?

What the slides establish

A cognitive bias is a systematic departure from a rationality or judgment benchmark. The lecture lists overconfidence, herd effect, overreaction and regret aversion. Overconfidence makes subjective certainty higher than objective accuracy. The slide distinguishes overestimation of own performance, overplacement relative to others and overprecision in how certain one feels about a belief.

Source slides 32 · L035 · structured draft

Explain how herding amplifies market moves

Would you buy the same stock if nobody else was buying it?

What the slides establish

Herd behavior means decisions move with peers, often for emotional reasons rather than independent analysis. The slide also calls this mob, pack or gang mentality. It links collective greed and fear to the potential worsening of asset bubbles and market crashes. A herd-influenced investor may make a choice unlike the one they would make alone.

Source slides 33 · L036 · structured draft

Recognize overreaction to market news

Can investors react so strongly to news that prices overshoot?

What the slides establish

Overreaction bias is an emotional response to new information, such as company news, driven by greed or fear. The slide describes securities becoming overbought or oversold relative to their intrinsic value and later moving back toward that value. This is the lecturer’s suggested mechanism; it is not a guarantee that a particular asset will revert on schedule.

Source slides 34 · L037 · structured draft

Distinguish regret from action and inaction

Is the regret of buying a loser different from missing a winner?

What the slides establish

Regret aversion is fear of learning later that a decision was wrong. The slides contrast an error of omission (not buying an asset that turns out to be right) with an error of commission (buying the wrong asset). Both can change investment behavior before the outcome is known.
