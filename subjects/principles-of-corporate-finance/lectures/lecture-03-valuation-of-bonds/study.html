What the bond lecture covers

The problem: Suppose you are offered a security that promises money several years from now. How much should you pay for it today? You cannot simply add up its future payments, because receiving €100 today and receiving €100 years later are not economically equivalent. Nor can you treat every issuer's promise as equally certain. The starting point of bond valuation is to identify precisely who owes whom, what is promised, when it is due, and what return the investor requires for waiting and bearing risk.

This is Lecture 3, Valuation of Bonds, in Prof. Dr. Thorsten Feix's Valuing Securities and Companies course for the first term of the IBF master's programme (slide 1, L001). The lecturer's stated organising idea is applying the net present value (NPV) concept to debt securities (slide 2, L002). What follows in this opening topic is a teaching explanation responding to the printed slides, not a purported transcript of spoken answers.

What does an investor buy when buying a bond?

A bond is a debt security: a financial claim under which an issuer (the borrower, such as a government or company) undertakes to make payments to the bondholder (a creditor or lender). When you buy a standard fixed-coupon bond, you are not buying a share of the issuer's ownership. You are buying the right to receive contractual interest payments and, normally, repayment of a stated principal amount at maturity. The word promised matters: the payment schedule is a contract, whereas actual receipt still depends on the issuer meeting its obligations.

Three amounts must be kept distinct from the beginning. The face value, also called principal or par value, is the amount normally repaid at maturity. The coupon is the contractual interest cash payment; its annual percentage of face value is the coupon rate for a conventional fixed-rate bond. The market price is what an investor pays to purchase the security today. It can be above, equal to or below face value. A fourth percentage, yield to maturity (YTM), describes the discount rate that makes the present value of the promised bond cash flows equal the observed price, under its stated payment and compounding convention. Coupon rate and YTM are therefore not interchangeable.

Bond versus loan: the economic similarity and the practical difference

Slide 3 (L003) asks how a bond differs from a loan. At the level of economics, both are ways to borrow money: one party receives financing and commits to pay interest and repay principal according to an agreed schedule. In each case the provider of funds is a creditor, not automatically an owner. Both can have repayment risk, and their contracts may contain rights or restrictions. What changes is usually the form of the claim and how it is distributed. A conventional bank loan is often negotiated directly between the borrower and one lender or a syndicate, with terms and transfer procedures specific to that agreement. A bond is issued in securities form, often in standard denominations, and may be held and traded by many investors in a securities market.

These are tendencies, not absolute legal boundaries: some loans are traded, and some bonds are privately placed or relatively illiquid. A crucial cash-flow consequence is that when an investor buys an already-issued bond from another investor, the purchase price generally goes to the selling investor, not to the issuer. The issuer's contractual obligations ordinarily continue under the bond's terms. The bond's market price can change between holders even when its face value and fixed coupon payments do not.

Which cash flows belong to the bondholder?

Imagine a timeline. At the purchase date, t = 0, a buyer pays the bond's price; this is a cash outflow for that investor. At stated future dates, the issuer pays coupons. At the maturity date, the issuer generally repays the face value as well as any final coupon due. In an annual fixed-coupon bond, that gives coupon, coupon, …, final coupon plus principal. The first payment does not need to equal the final payment. For the issuer, the directions are reversed: financing is received at issuance and promised payments go out later.

Payment frequency matters. “5% annual coupon rate” on €100 face value means €5 of contractual coupon per year for a straightforward annual-pay bond. If that same 5% nominal annual coupon is paid in two equal half-year payments under the usual convention, each payment is €2.50, not €5. Other bond structures may have zero coupons, floating coupons, early repayment options or different conventions; those require careful reading of their terms. Throughout the basic fixed-coupon model, the quoted cash flows are gross, promised amounts, before personal taxes, trading costs and any default loss. This is a simplifying model, not a guarantee.

Why present value, rather than simply adding the payments?

Money received later has an opportunity cost: an investor forgoes other uses of the purchase price while waiting. To compare payments arriving on different dates, finance converts them into today's money using a discount rate. If an amount today could earn a rate y for one year, its value after one year would be multiplied by (1 + y). Working in reverse, a certain future payment due in one year is divided by (1 + y) to express the equivalent value today. For a payment due in two years, divide by (1 + y)², and so on. Discounting each receipt separately and adding the results produces its present value (PV).

As a preview of the full bond formula developed in the next sections, if CFt is the cash inflow at the end of period t, y is a required return per corresponding period, and T is the number of remaining periods, then:

V₀ = CF₁ / (1 + y) + CF₂ / (1 + y)² + … + CFT / (1 + y)T

V₀ is the theoretical value at the purchase date in the same currency as the cash flows. CFt is a payment amount in euros, dollars or another currency; t is a count of payment periods, not automatically a count of calendar years; y is a decimal interest rate per that period, so 5% enters the calculation as 0.05. This introductory expression assumes end-of-period receipts and a common constant discount rate. It is not the right pricing expression if individual dates require different spot rates, a topic addressed later in the lecture.

To decide whether a given purchase price P₀ is attractive under the assumed discount rate and promised cash flows, compare value with cost: NPV = V₀ − P₀. Paying less than estimated value gives positive estimated NPV; paying more gives negative estimated NPV. A positive calculation is not proof of a risk-free bargain: the quality of the cash-flow forecasts and discount rate matters.

Illustrative teaching calculation, not supplied as a slide example. Take a deliberately small two-year annual-coupon bond with €100 face value, a 5% annual coupon, and a required return of 5% per year. It pays €5 at the end of year 1 and €105 at the end of year 2 (€5 coupon plus €100 principal). Discount each payment to today:

Year 1: €5 / 1.05 = €4.7619
Year 2: €105 / 1.05² = €95.2381
Value today: €4.7619 + €95.2381 = €100.00

The price equals face value in this example because its coupon rate matches the required return, not because bond prices must equal face value. If the same promised €5 and €105 payments are now discounted at 7% annually, the value falls to €5/1.07 + €105/1.07² = €96.38. The issuer still owes the same contractual euros. It is the investor's required compensation for time and risk that has changed. This previews the later price–yield and duration sections; the lecturer's actual French and U.S. bond numbers remain in their own original study topics.

Common errors at this starting point are to count the €100 principal as a coupon, to forget to add principal in the last period, to discount every receipt as though it were paid today, or to put an annual interest rate into a calculation whose periods are six months. Another error is to assume the coupon percentage always equals the investor's yield. The coupon is a contractual feature of the cash-flow stream; the yield also depends on the price at which the bond is bought. Finally, nominal payment certainty and purchasing-power certainty are different: inflation can erode the real value of a payment even if its stated euro amount is received.

French government bond: cash flows and present value

The opening breakout asks what the buyer actually receives. The French government bond on slides 4–5 supplies the first complete timeline and turns those promised payments into a price today.

The decision: how much is a fixed stream of payments worth now?

Imagine that you are considering a French government bond which promises €5 each year and returns €100 of principal at the end of its life. Simply adding the payments produces €125, but paying €125 today to receive that sum only over the next five years would ignore the time value of money. An investor could otherwise use the purchase money elsewhere, and must wait to receive each euro. Therefore we first lay out the precise dates and amounts, then discount each amount to the valuation date. This is the application of net present value that the lecturer names as “A short trip to… Paris to value a government bond” (slide 4, L004).

The contractual face value (FV or F) is €100. The fixed annual coupon is €5, so the annual coupon rate is €5/€100 = 5%. A coupon rate tells us how much the issuer promises to pay relative to face value; it is not the return an investor necessarily earns when purchasing the bond at an above-par or below-par market price. At maturity the issuer owes both its final €5 interest coupon and the €100 principal. The final investor receipt is consequently €105, not just €100 and not €205.

Step one: discount every dated payment

The lecturer assumes a 2.4% annual discount rate, denoted r in slide 5. In decimals this is r = 0.024 per year. The core principle is that receiving €1 a year from now has a present value of €1/(1.024). Receiving €1 after two full years has present value €1/(1.024)², and so on. For a future cash flow CFt measured in euros at the end of year t, the corresponding value today is PV(CFt) = CFt/(1+r)t. The denominator grows with delay, so a euro arriving later is worth less today when r is positive.

To read the notation precisely: PV and CFt are euro amounts; r is the annual interest rate as a decimal, 0.024; t is the number of annual payment periods after the pricing date. A positive cash flow means a receipt for the bondholder. The purchase price, if paid, is instead the bondholder's initial cash outflow at t = 0. Do not discount €5 at 5% just because the bond has a 5% coupon: the cash coupon is €5, whereas the valuation discount rate supplied by the slide is 2.4%.

Step two: see why the bond is a coupon annuity plus one principal payment

Slide 5 (L006) rearranges the same five terms into a cleaner structure. There are five equal coupon payments of €5, so their present values form an ordinary annuity, meaning equal periodic amounts paid at the ends of successive periods. The principal is a single lump sum of €100 due in period 5. This decomposition changes only the grouping of the cash flows, not which payments are owed or the valuation:

PV(bond) = PV(annuity of coupon payments) + PV(final payment of principal)

The present-value annuity factor sums the five discount factors for one euro paid per year. For an equal coupon C, discount rate r and number of periods N, write the coupon stream as C/(1+r) + C/(1+r)² + … + C/(1+r)N. This is a finite geometric sum. Multiplying that sum by (1+r), then subtracting the original sum, cancels the middle powers and leaves the familiar expression C × [1 − (1+r)−N]/r for r ≠ 0. Thus the lecturer's displayed formula is:

PV(bond) = Coupon × [1/r − 1/(r(1+r)N)] + FV/(1+r)N

Here Coupon (or C) is the fixed euro payment per period; r is the required yield per matching period; N is the count of remaining periods, with payments assumed at period ends; and FV or F is the euro face value repaid in period N. The bracketed quantity, [1 − (1+r)−N]/r, is dimensionless; multiplying it by a euro coupon gives the coupon stream's value in euros. The principal formula discounts a single final euro amount through all N periods. If r were zero, the corresponding undiscounted limit would be C × N + FV; the bracket form has a division by r and should not be used by direct substitution at r = 0.

Apply the slide's C = €5, FV = €100, r = 0.024, N = 5. The five-year coupon annuity factor is approximately 4.6592325, so the coupons alone are worth €5 × 4.6592325 = €23.29616 ≈ €23.30. The principal is worth €100/(1.024)⁵ = €88.81784 ≈ €88.82. Add the unrounded contributions:

PV(bond) = €23.29616… + €88.81784… = €112.11400… ≈ €112.11

Slide 5 literally prints “€23.30 + €88.82 = €112.11” (L006). Taken as separately rounded numbers, the first two terms would sum to €112.12. This is only a rounding-presentation inconsistency: the correct two-decimal answer from the unrounded cash-flow calculation is €112.11. The lecturer's displayed amounts and answer are both preserved here, with the precision difference made explicit.

What does the €112.11 price mean?

The five annual coupons total €25 in nominal currency, but the buyer cannot receive them all at once. At a 2.4% discount rate, the investor's estimated value of the bond's promised payments is approximately €112.11 per €100 face value. The bond therefore trades at a premium to par in this valuation: its value is roughly €12.11 more than the €100 principal. The reason is intuitive. The contract pays €5 interest each year, or a 5% coupon on face value, while the investor's required yield for this particular set of cash flows is only 2.4%. A high fixed coupon is attractive relative to that lower rate, so a buyer can rationally pay more than the principal ultimately repaid.

Paying €112.11 does not entitle the holder to receive €112.11 in principal in 2029; only €100 of principal is contractually scheduled for repayment, along with the €5 final coupon. Nor is the 5% coupon the same as the 2.4% purchase yield. For a bond bought above par, the later repayment at face value creates a capital-value decline from the purchase price if held through maturity. The periodic coupons and that decline must be considered together when describing YTM. Topic t03 takes up the reverse problem: given a bond's observed price, solve for the yield that makes discounted payments add to it.

The sensitivity has a clear direction: holding the five payments unchanged, a higher required discount rate makes every discounted receipt smaller, so the bond's price falls. For the same €5 annual coupons and €100 final principal, a 3% annual rate would value these payments at approximately €109.16, while at a 5% annual rate the value would equal exactly €100. These are teaching recalculations of the same slide cash flows, not additional lecturer quotes or external-market data. They demonstrate why the rate belongs in the discount denominator and why coupon and yield only produce a par-value bond when the remaining contract and compounding conventions line up.

General bond valuation formulas

The French government example priced a specific bond. These three lecture slides turn that calculation into a general method that works for any stated schedule of coupon and principal payments under a consistent discount-rate assumption.

One practical question, three equivalent ways to write the answer

Suppose a finance analyst is comparing bonds with different coupon amounts, maturity dates and quoted prices. Is a separate valuation rule needed for every bond? No. The common idea is to put all future promised payments on the same time basis: calculate what each future receipt is worth at the valuation date, then add the present values. The three displays in slides 7–9 are not competing models. Slide 7 shows every payment term, slide 8 collects coupon terms with a summation symbol, and slide 9 compresses those terms into an annuity factor when all coupon payments are equal. Which form is convenient depends on how regular the bond's cash flows are.

The issuer promises two possible types of cash flow to the holder of a standard coupon bond: periodic coupon interest and principal repaid at maturity. We value them together even though the amounts may arrive on different dates. Price is determined at time t = 0; periods are counted forward from that valuation date. The basic lecture convention is end-of-period payments, a fixed per-period discount rate, and full payment of the contractual cash flows. Credit risk, calendar conventions and maturity-specific spot rates complicate actual market pricing, but do not change the principle that only the value of future cash flows justifies the price.

First form, slide 7: write the complete cash-flow timeline

PV = C₁/(1+r)¹ + C₂/(1+r)² + … + (FV + CN)/(1+r)N

This is the expanded present-value formula printed on slide 7 (L010). The first coupon C₁ arrives at the end of the first period; the second C₂ arrives one period later; and CN arrives together with the bond's face value FV at maturity. Each additional period adds another factor (1+r) to the denominator. The final term deliberately contains FV + CN: omitting either principal or the last coupon understates the payment, but adding FV a second time would overstate it.

PV is the value today, in currency units such as euros; Ct is the coupon paid at the end of period t, also in euros; FV is the euro amount of principal repaid at the end of period N; N is the number of payment periods until maturity; and r is the yield to maturity per payment period used to discount the cash flows in this simple constant-yield valuation. A rate of 4% is entered as 0.04, never 4. These period and unit conventions must match: for annual payments use annual periods and an annual effective rate appropriate for those periods, while a semiannual bond requires half-year cash flows and a correspondingly defined half-year rate.

In an investor's timeline, the future coupons and principal are positive receipts, whereas the acquisition price is a cash outflow at t = 0. The PV formula calculates the value of future receipts; it does not subtract a price until an investment comparison is being made. With a quoted purchase price P₀, the investor's value-minus-cost calculation is NPV = PV − P₀, provided the assumed discount rate reflects the investor's required return for those cash flows.

Second form, slide 8: use a summation so no coupon is forgotten

PV(bond) = Σt=1N Ct/(1+r)t + FV/(1+r)N

Slide 8 (L011) states PV(bond) = PV(coupon payments) + PV(final payment) and gives the formula above. The symbol Σ means “add the expression for each integer period t from 1 through N.” It is only a compact way to write the same sequence that appears in slide 7. The last coupon is already part of the coupon sum because the sum includes t=N; the additional FV term is principal only. This explains why the expanded formula ends in (FV + CN)/(1+r)N and the summation formula can instead place FV after the summation without double-counting anything.

Slide 8's notation explicitly says Ct = (coupon rate) × (face value), FV = Face Value, r = Yield To Maturity and N = Maturity. For the straightforward fixed-coupon, annual-payment setting shown, that coupon-rate relationship produces the same coupon C in every year: 5% of €100 face value means €5 per annual payment. If the contractual coupon changes by date, keep a separate Ct in each term rather than pretending all payments are equal. When there are two coupons a year, convert the stated annual coupon rate into the contractual payment per half-year using the relevant convention before applying the formula.

Illustrative calculation, not a separate lecturer example: Consider a three-period security with principal €100, coupons of €4, €6 and €8 respectively, and a required rate r = 5% per period. The coupon amounts here intentionally change, so a level-coupon shortcut would be inappropriate. Apply the summation directly:

PV = 4/1.05 + 6/(1.05)² + (8+100)/(1.05)³
= €3.8095 + €5.4422 + €93.2945 = €102.5462 ≈ €102.55

The interpretation is precise: at the given discount rate, three promised receipts totalling €118 in nominal euro terms have a present value of approximately €102.55. The final €108 includes the third coupon and €100 principal, and the sum correctly includes the principal just once. This small, explicitly illustrative changing-coupon calculation demonstrates the purpose of the general formula; it is not a replacement for the lecturer's original French, IBM or Japanese examples elsewhere in the chapter.

Third form, slide 9: the annuity shortcut for equal coupons

Many conventional fixed-rate bonds have the same amount of coupon interest every period. Instead of calculating N coupon present values separately, sum the geometric series once. Slide 9 (L012) describes the bond as “PV(annuity of coupon payments) + PV(final payment of principal)” and, in its intermediate wording, the coupon amount multiplied by a t-years annuity factor plus the final face-value payment multiplied by a discount factor. Here annuity means equal payments at equal intervals at the end of each interval; it does not mean a different kind of security.

PV(bond) = Coupon × [1/r − 1/(r(1+r)N)] + FV/(1+r)N

This is the lecturer's exact structural shortcut, with Coupon = constant payment per period in euros, r = yield per period as a decimal, N = number of remaining periods and FV = face value paid at maturity. It can also be written as:

PV(bond) = C × [1 − (1+r)−N]/r + FV × (1+r)−N

The bracketed annuity factor has no currency units. Multiplying it by the €-per-period coupon yields an amount in euros. The second factor, (1+r)−N, discounts the one principal repayment back N periods. The two factors are different because coupons arrive repeatedly while principal arrives only at maturity.

Derive the annuity factor rather than memorising it blindly

Let q = 1/(1+r) be the one-period discount factor. One euro at each period-end has a present value of A = q + q² + … + qN. Multiply the whole sum by q, obtaining qA = q² + q³ + … + qN+1. Now subtract: A − qA = q − qN+1, because every middle term cancels. Hence A(1−q) = q(1−qN). Substituting q = 1/(1+r) and simplifying gives:

A = q(1−qN)/(1−q) = [1−(1+r)−N]/r
= 1/r − 1/[r(1+r)N]

That final line is exactly the bracket on slide 9. For r = 0, the displayed division by r must not be evaluated directly; its correct limiting factor is A=N, since an undiscounted euro arrives at each of N periods. Thus PV = N×C + FV when the rate is zero. If coupons are unequal, the geometric cancellation no longer works with a single C: return to slide 8's coupon-by-coupon sum.

How to interpret a model price: par, premium, discount and interest-rate changes

A price computed from future cash flows and a required rate is a conditional valuation, not a cash payment the issuer owes today. With a standard fixed-coupon bond and a positive yield, there is a useful exact decomposition. Denote its annuity factor by A. Because rA = 1−(1+r)−N, it follows that FV = r×FV×A + FV/(1+r)N. Subtracting this expression for FV from the bond-price formula gives:

PV(bond) − FV = (C − r×FV) × A

This small identity explains the familiar premium–par–discount rule. If the coupon C is greater than the required coupon-equivalent amount r×FV, the price exceeds face value (a premium). If they are equal, the price equals face value (par); if C is less than r×FV, the price is below par (a discount). The statement assumes ordinary fixed coupon dates, a single positive per-period yield, full face-value repayment and the same compounding convention throughout. The fact that a bond is priced above par does not increase the amount of principal ultimately due.

Because each positive future payment is divided by a larger power of (1+r) when r rises, bond price declines as required yield increases, holding promised cash flows fixed. Receipts further in the future are discounted more times, making the price especially sensitive to the required rate. This is the mechanism behind the later duration lesson, not a claim that the issuer reduces its contractual coupon whenever yields rise. For professional decisions the distinction matters: a portfolio manager may judge a quoted price against a required return, while a treasury issuer considers how market yields change the proceeds from issuing new fixed-rate debt. Those choices require checking actual payment dates, tax and cost conventions, credit risk, and whether a flat rate r adequately describes the yield curve.

Choosing the correct expression and avoiding traps

Use the expanded formula when only a handful of cash flows are involved and you want every date visible. Use the summation for any explicitly known coupon schedule, including changing amounts. Use the annuity shortcut only when every coupon is equal and paid at regular intervals, and when the same per-period discount rate is being applied. They must agree numerically whenever the shortcut's assumptions hold. With zero coupons, set Ct=0 and the value is simply FV/(1+r)N. With a different spot rate for each maturity, replace the common-discount-rate assumption with date-specific discounting, as taught in the term-structure topic.

Typical errors are counting the last coupon twice; forgetting principal; using N as calendar years when there are two payments per year; treating a quoted annual rate as automatically the correct semiannual rate; entering “5” instead of “0.05”; using a coupon percentage directly as the required yield; treating a premium purchase price as additional principal repayment; or applying slide 9's fixed-annuity factor to irregular payments. A further conceptual error is to confuse the direction of the problem: when r is given, calculate PV; when a market price is given, solve r as the YTM. Topic t03 develops that reverse calculation and its limitations.

Yield to maturity: solving in reverse

The preceding sections treated the required interest rate as an input and computed the bond's value. Slide 6 turns the same equation around: once an investor knows the bond's price, what single annual return makes the promised payments worth exactly that amount?

Why the bond's quoted price does not tell you its yield by itself

A bond's market price is the amount someone must pay to acquire its remaining contractual receipts. But prices of bonds with different coupon rates, maturities and repayment patterns cannot be compared meaningfully by looking at the price alone. An investor buying the French bond on the lecture slide pays more than its €100 face value, yet still receives €5 each year and €100 principal at maturity. The investor therefore enjoys the coupon payments and faces the economic effect of paying a premium that will not be returned as principal. The question is how to combine those cash flows into one rate comparable with other investments.

The yield to maturity (YTM) answers that question in the lecture's simplified fixed-coupon model. It is the single discount rate that makes the present value of all remaining promised coupons and principal equal the price paid today. The same rate is also the internal rate of return (IRR) of the dated cash-flow stream, provided the buyer pays that price and receives all the scheduled payments on time. This is a rate implied by the quoted price and promised payments, not an independent contractual coupon rate and not a guarantee of the return actually realized in the future.

Why there is a negative relationship between price and yield

The important mechanism is already visible in the equation. If you keep all five promised cash flows fixed and raise y, each factor (1+y)t becomes larger, so each present value becomes smaller. Hence an increase in observed market price implies a decrease in YTM, and a lower price implies a higher YTM, all else equal. In the model the price sensitivity is formally described by the derivative:

dP/dy = − Σt=1…5 [t × CFt/(1+y)t+1] < 0

Each CFt is positive, so every component inside the sum is positive and the overall slope is negative. Here dP/dy measures currency-unit change in theoretical price per 1.0 change in the decimal yield, not euros per one percentage point. This slope leads naturally to the later concept of modified duration, which expresses approximate percentage price sensitivity. The larger powers for later payments explain why cash-flow timing matters.

What slide 6 means by “coupon return” and a “declining value”

The lecturer's exact wording (L008) is: “The Yield To Maturity blends the return from the coupon payment with the declining value of the bond over its remaining life.” This describes the particular premium bond pictured, not a universal rule that every bond falls in price. The French bond is worth more than €100 because its fixed 5% coupon is high compared with the roughly 2.4% yield implied by the price. An investor who buys it for €112.11 receives €25 of total coupons over five years, but at maturity the issuer repays only €100 of principal, not the €112.11 purchase price. The premium paid initially is not returned at redemption.

Under the unchanged-yield thought experiment, the theoretical ex-coupon price of a plain fixed-coupon premium bond generally moves toward its €100 face value as maturity approaches. This is called pull to par. Coupons are still received along the way; a downward drift in the ex-coupon premium price does not by itself imply the bondholder is losing money overall. A bond purchased at a discount, by contrast, has a price that would generally move upward toward par under unchanged required yield and full repayment. In actual trading, shifts in required yields or credit risk can overwhelm either simplified price path.

A second crucial distinction is between a yield based on promised contractual payments and a realized return. If the buyer holds the bond to maturity and every promised payment occurs, the schedule's realized cash-flow IRR equals the purchase-date YTM, measured under the same rate convention. But default, early sale, transactions costs and taxation can change the actual investor outcome. The frequently quoted interpretation of YTM as a continuously earned or reinvested annual compound return may additionally depend on how intermediate coupons are invested. The basic IRR calculation itself does not require assuming the coupons were reinvested at exactly the YTM; such an assumption matters when projecting a single terminal wealth figure. Keep those two uses of “return” separate.

How an analyst should use the result, and what to avoid

When comparing two bonds, an analyst might take each bond's actual market price and promised payments, solve the IRR, then ask whether the apparent extra yield compensates for differences in maturity, payment frequency, creditworthiness, embedded rights and liquidity. A higher YTM is not automatically the better investment: it may also reflect a larger chance that some of the quoted payments will not occur. A single YTM is a convenient summary of one bond's price, whereas the term-structure chapter later distinguishes the maturity-specific spot rates appropriate to cash flows of different dates.

Common calculation mistakes are to enter the 5% coupon as the unknown yield, to omit the final €100 principal, to discount the €105 final receipt and also discount another €100 principal, to use €100 face value in place of the €112.11 purchase price, to treat the annual 2.4% yield as 2.4 rather than 0.024, or to confuse a five-year maturity with five semiannual payment periods. Another common misconception is that the coupon-to-price ratio, €5/€112.11 ≈ 4.46% (current yield), already equals YTM. It does not: it ignores the principal repayment and the fact that price exceeded face value. Nor should one force false precision from the slide's rounded €112.11 price.

The problem: how do you value a three-year bond that pays twice a year?

Why should the payment frequency matter? An investor does not receive all three years of coupons at the end, nor one coupon at each annual anniversary. Bond yields, price sensitivity and maturity

The valuation cases calculated prices from a specified required yield. Now change that yield while keeping the bond's promised payments fixed. This reveals both the inverse price–yield relationship and why longer cash-flow schedules are usually more exposed to interest-rate movements.

Why can a fixed-coupon bond lose value when market yields rise?

Consider a bond that promises the same interest payment every period plus repayment of principal at maturity. When investors suddenly require a higher return on comparable debt, those old contractual payments have not improved. To make the old payments offer the higher required return, buyers will offer a lower purchase price. Conversely, if comparable yields fall, those unchanged coupons become more attractive relative to new investment opportunities and the bond's market price rises. This is the first lesson of slides 16–17: yield and price move in opposite directions, holding the cash-flow contract unchanged (L022–L023).

Remember the difference between the two percentages: a fixed coupon rate determines the contractual interest amount based on face value, while the yield to maturity (YTM) is the return implied by discounting all promised remaining cash flows at the price paid. Higher market yields do not automatically change coupons already fixed in the bond contract. The price adjusts instead. In practice, changes in credit risk, liquidity, taxes and embedded options may also affect the price, so the lecture isolates only the interest-rate mechanism.

Derive the inverse-price result from the discounting formula

For N positive promised future payments CFt made at the ends of equal intervals, with one yield y per interval, the bond value is:

P(y) = Σt=1N CFt/(1+y)t

P is the present price in currency units; CFt is the euro or dollar payment in period t; t counts payment periods from today; N is the number of periods remaining; and y is a decimal yield per the same period (4% = 0.04). Coupons and maturity principal are included in the dated cash flows; the price paid at time zero is an initial cash outflow for the investor. An annual yield should not be put directly into a semiannual model without checking the yield convention and period conversion.

Every future positive payment is divided by a bigger number as y rises. Formally, the rate derivative is:

dP/dy = − Σt=1N [t × CFt/(1+y)t+1] < 0

Because the bracketed quantities are positive for these ordinary fixed-coupon bonds, the slope is negative. The derivative expresses a price change in currency units per 1.0 change in the decimal rate; one percentage point corresponds to a rate movement of 0.01, not 1. The factor t also hints at the importance of cash-flow timing: more distant payments tend to amplify price sensitivity. The later duration formulas make this practical.

How to use the result in financial decisions

A fund manager's portfolio can experience mark-to-market losses after a yield rise even if issuers continue paying all promised coupons. Holding more long conventional fixed-rate bonds generally creates more exposure to parallel yield changes than holding otherwise comparable short bonds. For a borrowing company, higher market-required yields mean that a new fixed coupon obligation would support less issuance proceeds at the old coupon rate. These conclusions come from present-value logic; they are not statements about any particular current issuer or market.

Duration and modified duration: definitions

The price–yield charts showed that bond prices move inversely to required yields. Duration now measures how sensitive a bond's price is, by looking at when the money is scheduled to arrive, not merely at its final maturity date.

The financial decision: which bond is more exposed to a change in yields?

Suppose two bonds have similar maturity dates and credit quality, but one pays large coupons early while the other saves most of its value for the final repayment. A portfolio manager expecting interest rates to change needs more than the statement “prices fall when yields rise.” The important question is how much the price will change for a specified movement in yield. A bond whose economic value is concentrated in payments far into the future will generally be more exposed to discount-rate changes. Duration makes this timing of value measurable.

Ordinary maturity tells us when the last principal payment occurs. It does not tell us when the bondholder receives all the intermediate coupons. Duration, in the sense used in slides 19 and 21, is the present-value-weighted average payment time. It assigns a greater weight to a cash flow that contributes more to the bond's current price. The result is a time measure, such as 4.57 years for an annual-payment example, even if the contractual maturity is five years. It is not simply the arithmetic average of the calendar years on a timeline.

First concept, Macaulay duration: the weighted time until cash arrives

For an ordinary fixed-coupon bond, write its present price as P = Σt=1N PV(CFt), where CFt is the entire promised investor payment at the end of period t. At maturity that payment includes both the final coupon and repayment of principal. If the same per-period yield y discounts every promised payment, then PV(CFt) = CFt/(1+y)t. The weight of any period's payment is its present value divided by total bond price, wt = PV(CFt)/P. For positive cash flows the weights add up to 1, since their numerators add to P.

Slides 19 and 21 express the corresponding Macaulay duration as:

DMac = Σt=1N [t × PV(CFt)] / P = Σt=1N t × wt

The symbol t is the number of discount periods until receipt; N is the remaining number of periods; PV(CFt) is a currency amount today; and P is the present value of all future cash flows in the same currency. Thus the ratio PV(CFt)/P is a dimensionless share of price, and multiplying it by t produces a time-weighted contribution. If periods are years, DMac is measured in years; if periods are half-years, the raw answer is in half-year periods and should be divided by two to express years.

Why does this weighting make sense? If a final principal payment accounts for 80% of today's bond value, then the timing of that payment must have more influence on the bond's overall interest-rate exposure than a small coupon which accounts for 4%. A positive-cash-flow zero-coupon bond makes the intuition especially clear: since the entire present value arrives in one maturity payment, its Macaulay duration is exactly its maturity in the same time units. Conventional coupon bonds typically have duration shorter than maturity because some value arrives in earlier coupon payments.

Second concept, modified duration: translate time weighting into price sensitivity

Macaulay duration is measured in time, but the risk question concerns how much price changes when yield changes. Slides 19 and 21 give the conversion, using their terms “Volatility (%)” and “Modified duration” for the same formula (L026, L031):

Modified duration = “Volatility (%)” = Duration / (1 + yield)

Under the lecture's single-yield, equal-period convention, the precise notation is:

Dmod = DMac / (1+y)

Here y is the yield per the same period used for the duration weights, entered as a decimal, and DMac and Dmod have the same period units. For the annual French case, Dmod = 4.574566/(1.024) = 4.467350. This number describes approximately a 4.467% price change in the opposite direction for a one-percentage-point change in annual yield near the starting yield, not a 4.467% statistical standard deviation of daily returns.

Why divide by 1+y? Differentiate P(y) = Σ CFt/(1+y)t with respect to the decimal period yield. Each term contributes −t × CFt/(1+y)t+1, which is −t × PV(CFt)/(1+y). Factor out −1/(1+y), divide by P to express the result as a fraction of price, and the weighted-time sum that remains is Macaulay duration:

(1/P) × (dP/dy) = − [1/(1+y)] × Σt=1N [t × PV(CFt)/P] = −Dmod

For a small change in the period yield, this derivative gives the first-order approximation:

ΔP/P ≈ −Dmod × Δy
ΔP ≈ −P × Dmod × Δy

The first equation predicts a decimal fractional price movement; multiplying by 100 reports it as a percentage. The second predicts an absolute price change in the bond's currency. Δy must be a decimal change in yield: a rise of 0.5 percentage points is +0.005, not +0.5; a fall of 0.5 percentage points is −0.005. The negative sign is vital: as yield rises, the price falls for these conventional positive-payment bonds.

Payment frequency, professional interpretation and limits

The simplest slide 21 formula assumes the discount periods and yield periods match. With annual coupon payments and an annual yield, the raw duration can be read in years and Dmod=DMac/(1+y) measures sensitivity to that annual decimal yield. With semiannual payments, first compute cash-flow timing in half-year periods and use the half-year yield in the denominator. If you want duration in years, divide the raw half-year Macaulay duration by two. For a conventional nominal annual bond yield Y quoted with two compounding periods per year, the annualized sensitivity convention becomes Dmod,years = DMac,years/(1+Y/2); one must not confuse a one-percentage-point annual quoted yield change with a one-percentage-point semiannual period yield change. The slide 21 formula itself leaves those quoting conventions implicit, so they are clarified here rather than attributed to an unrecorded lecture explanation.

For professional portfolio decisions, modified duration provides a quick estimate of interest-rate price risk. A manager can compare sensitivity between bonds or estimate the impact of a small, approximately parallel shift in relevant yields before recomputing every bond. But duration is not a forecast of where yields will go, a probability of loss, or proof that promised principal will be repaid. Its first-order approximation can be inaccurate for large rate changes, callable or otherwise option-embedded bonds, and shifts in the yield curve that are not uniform across maturities. Credit spreads and liquidity can also move prices independently of the chosen risk-free yield.

The most frequent errors are using maturity instead of the weighted average; forgetting to include principal inside the final-period cash flow; multiplying coupon cash amounts by time without first computing their present-value weights; treating the unit of duration as dollars; interpreting the slide's “volatility” as a standard deviation; omitting the negative sign in a yield-rise scenario; or entering 0.5 for a half-percentage-point yield change rather than 0.005. The financial story, formula units and sign must all agree.

Why would two seven-year bonds respond differently to the same yield change?

An investor can own two conventional bonds that both repay $1,000 after seven years but do not expose that investor to identical interest-rate price risk. The difference is when the cash flows arrive. A low-coupon bond returns relatively little money before maturity, so the final principal accounts for more of the present value. A high-coupon bond returns more money along the way, giving earlier periods greater weight. When the required discount rate changes, the value concentrated in later dates is generally more sensitive. This is the point of slide 20's headline: the 3% coupon bond has a longer average time span to each cash flow because the last cash flow has a higher impact (L028–L030).

The source fixes both bonds' face value at $1,000 and maturity at seven years, and uses annual coupons and an annual yield to maturity (YTM). The low-coupon bond pays 3% × $1,000 = $30 after each year; the high-coupon bond pays 9% × $1,000 = $90 each year. They repay the same face value at year seven, so their last receipts are $1,030 and $1,090 respectively. These are coupon-bond cash flows, not zero-coupon or amortizing bonds. The investor pays the bond price at time zero (an outflow), then receives positive future payments at year ends.

What this means in portfolio decisions and where the model stops

When a portfolio manager wants to understand interest-rate risk, coupon size matters as well as maturity. The 3%-coupon seven-year security in the lecture behaves more like a later-payment claim and has a higher Macaulay duration than the 9%-coupon security. A lower-coupon bond will therefore generally register a larger percentage price change for the same small parallel shift in required yield when other relevant terms are comparable. A high-coupon bond typically returns a greater share of its value earlier, reducing duration. This is not a claim that its market price must be lower or that a higher coupon is necessarily the better investment.

Duration itself assumes a particular cash-flow schedule and a yield convention. The 5.47 figure is a local slope, not a guaranteed realised loss or a probability; for bigger moves it misses convexity, and for defaultable or callable securities the cash flows may change. More generally, shifts in spot rates, credit spreads and liquidity are not necessarily captured by changing one bond-wide YTM. The source compares plain fixed-coupon bonds with matched maturity to isolate coupon-related timing effects. Those qualifications matter when using the method outside a teaching example.

Common mistakes include using the 3% or 9% coupon rate as the discount yield even though the question says 4% or 3%; discounting principal twice; placing $1,000 in each year's cash-flow row instead of only at maturity; comparing dollar gains instead of percentage gains based on each original bond price; using final maturity as if it were Macaulay duration; forgetting to weight by present value; and replacing a half-percentage-point change by 0.5 rather than 0.005. In the volatility example, copying both +2.687% and −2.687% without separately repricing the bonds hides the curvature in the true price–yield function.

Why the price–yield relationship is curved, rather than a straight line

With the source's yearly-effective discount convention and its fractional-year times, the value at any annual decimal rate y is P(y)=Σ CFi/(1+y)ti. Differentiate with respect to y, holding the contractual cash-flow sequence fixed:

dP/dy = −Σi=112 tiCFi/(1+y)ti+1 < 0
d²P/dy² = Σi=112 ti(ti+1)CFi/(1+y)ti+2 > 0

The first derivative is negative, because higher discount rates reduce each positive future receipt's present value. The second derivative is positive, giving the curve its upward convexity. Both expressions apply to a simple fixed-payment schedule with a common rate and no rate-dependent cash flows. The derivative is defined for changes in a decimal effective annual yield, so a one-percentage-point change corresponds to Δy = 0.01, not 1.

The pricing problem: €1 next year and €1 in ten years are not the same financial claim

An investor who will receive a fixed sum next year does not face the same waiting time as someone due to receive that sum ten years from now. Market compensation for those different horizons can vary. Consequently, a bank or portfolio analyst should not automatically value every future euro using one identical annual rate. The term structure of interest rates describes how rates differ according to the time until payment or maturity. A line drawn through those maturity-specific rates is a yield curve. The lecture's slide 26 specifically defines the topic as the relationship between short-term and long-term interest rates or yields (L040).

To make the comparison meaningful, one must hold relevant features such as currency, credit and payment conventions comparable. An observed difference between two bond yields could reflect maturity, but also different credit risk, liquidity, taxes or coupons. Slide 26 presents government-yield illustrations to introduce maturity patterns, while slide 27 moves to the cleaner underlying concept of spot rates. A spot rate discounts one receipt at one specified future maturity rather than summarising many receipts in one coupon bond.

In an annual-compounding convention, let r₁ be the one-year spot rate and r₂ the two-year spot rate. The slide displays the corresponding values of one unit of currency as:

Each rt is an annual spot rate to maturity t stated as a decimal, not automatically the interest rate earned solely during year t. The exponent t counts the total years from the valuation date to the single payoff. A two-year spot rate of 4% means that the two-year discount factor is 1/(1.04)², not one factor of 1.03 followed by 1.04. The successive rates r₁, r₂, r₃, …, rt form the spot-rate term structure (L042).

Why does the exponent work that way? If the annualised two-year spot rate is 4% under this compounding convention, then a payment invested at that constant-equivalent return grows from P₀ to P₀×(1.04)² over two years. To find the value today of a promised single unit paid in two years, work backwards by dividing by (1.04)². The 4% is an equivalent two-year investment rate, not an assertion that every one-year period in a changing market must itself offer exactly 4%.

Spot rates and YTM answer different questions

A spot curve tells us the discount factor appropriate to each payment date. A coupon bond's YTM tells us which single rate reproduces the bond's overall price from its whole promised stream. Their relationship is one of valuation and summarisation, not equivalence of the underlying rates. In this two-payment example the single yield lies between 3% and 4%; the exact result depends on the timing and amounts of both receipts. A different payment pattern, even with the same spot curve, could have a different YTM. A single YTM cannot fully reproduce the entire term structure for every other bond.

The lecturer's phrase “spot rates come first” is best read as the logical order of this valuation method: use dated spot discounts to obtain a consistent bond price; then solve YTM as that price's internal rate of return. It need not mean that in actual financial markets every spot rate is observed before every bond price. In practice, bond and discount-factor information can be inferred jointly from traded securities. The relevant learning point on the slide is the distinction between a set of discount factors and a single implied YTM, not a universal chronological claim.

Why the law of one price prevents two inconsistent valuations

Slide 28's headline is “Law of One Price: The same commodity must sell at the same price in a well functioning market” (L045). For bonds, the deeper idea is that two portfolios promising precisely the same future cash flows, on the same dates and with matching risks and conditions, cannot sustainably command different prices without an arbitrage opportunity. Otherwise a trader could sell the expensive set of claims and acquire the identical cheaper set, receiving the same net cash flows later while keeping a difference today. This assumes sufficiently frictionless trading, enforceable payments and the ability to transact in both positions; transaction costs, credit differences and market frictions may prevent an exact textbook equality.

Return to the lecturer's two payments. A claim paying $1 in year 1 has the spot-based price $0.970874; a separate claim paying $1 in year 2 costs $0.924556. Purchasing both would replicate the same $1 in year 1 plus $1 in year 2 promised by the two-payment security. The combined replication cost is therefore $1.89543. If a perfectly matching security were offered at, for example, $1.92, that would be an ILLUSTRATIVE hypothetical discrepancy of about $0.02457 above the cost of replication. Selling the expensive combination and purchasing the matching dated claims would lock in that day-zero difference in the simplified no-friction case, with offsetting future promises. The $1.92 is a teaching assumption, not a lecturer-supplied bond quote or observed market fact.

Slide 28 expresses the adjustment mechanism in four short statements, preserved here in substance: “All interest-bearing instruments priced to fit term structure”; “Accomplished by modifying asset price”; “Modified price creates new yield, which fits term structure”; and “New yield called Yield To Maturity (YTM).” The sense is that when the price of a fixed promised cash-flow stream is adjusted, its internally implied YTM changes too. The contractual coupon amounts do not need to change for the price and yield to move into consistency with the current opportunity set.

From one example to a general bond-pricing rule

If a conventional bond promises a coupon Ct at each annual date t=1,…,N and repays F at the end of year N, the spot-rate version of the earlier present-value formula becomes:

P₀ = Σt=1N Ct/(1+rt)t + F/(1+rN)N

Here P₀, the coupons and the principal are expressed in the same currency; t counts whole years to each date; and rt is the corresponding annual-compounded t-year spot discount rate as a decimal. The final coupon belongs to the sum, and the face value is added exactly once at maturity. The expression assumes fixed contractual cash flows and a consistent set of discount factors for their risks, currencies and dates. With semiannual payments, actual fractional maturities and matching spot-rate conventions must be used instead of blindly applying integer-year powers.

If the entire curve happened to be flat at one common y, so that every rt=y, then the formula reduces to the familiar single-YTM present-value formula from the earlier topics. But when r₁, r₂ and other rates differ, a single common-yield discounting assumption generally would not correctly value each individual receipt. Once the spot-priced total P₀ is known, one can still solve the separate equation P₀=ΣCt/(1+y)t+F/(1+y)N to report a bond's YTM, under its stated conventions.

Practical judgement and frequent confusions

For a treasury or investment analyst, this distinction matters in two directions. When valuing dated obligations, use the discount factors associated with the dates on which money is actually due; otherwise long and short future payments can be mispriced. When comparing market bond quotes, YTM is a helpful common-looking summary but must be interpreted alongside maturity, coupon distribution, and credit risk. A steep or inverted term structure can make two securities' YTMs differ despite otherwise similar risk, and changing spot rates at only one maturity can affect bonds differently according to where their cash flows occur.

Typical mistakes are to read a two-year spot rate as merely the second year's one-year interest rate; discount the second payment with 1.03×1.04 although the source specifies a two-year spot rate of 4%; put $2 in the year-two numerator instead of treating the two $1 dates separately; treat the lecturer's $1.895 rounded quote as infinitely precise; mistake YTM for an average of spot rates; or suppose two securities with different cash-flow dates must have identical prices just because each has face value $100. The law of one price requires matched cash flows and economically equivalent claims, not merely similar labels. Four distinct meanings of “interest rate” on slide 30

The lecture ends this slide with four brief definitions (L051). They describe different objects and should not be merged:

A spot rate is defined in the source as the “actual interest rate today (t = 0)”. The careful maturity-specific reading, consistent with slides 27 and 29, is that a rate observed today applies to lending or receiving a single cash flow up to a stated future maturity. Today's one-year spot rate and today's four-year spot rate can be different; “today” describes when the rate is observed, not when the investment matures.

A forward rate is a rate fixed today for a loan that will begin at a specified future time. It is a rate attached to a future borrowing/lending interval, not automatically an analyst's prediction. The slide's wording is “interest rate, fixed today, on future loan at fixed time.” An example can be inferred from the spot rates in slide 29: a one-year loan running from the end of year 1 to the end of year 2 would have a no-arbitrage implied forward rate satisfying:

(1+r₂)² = (1+r₁)(1+f1,2)
f1,2 = (1.04)²/(1.03) − 1 ≈ 0.0500971 = 5.0097%

This is a derived illustrative calculation using the lecturer's 3% and 4% spot rates, not a forward-rate numerical answer printed on slide 30. Both sides represent the same two-year growth factor under matched annual compounding: investing for two years at the two-year spot rate versus investing for the first year at the one-year spot rate and contracting today for the rate during the following year. Under ideal no-arbitrage conditions, inconsistent guaranteed rates for otherwise equivalent risk and dates would invite trading to exploit the discrepancy.

A future rate is described on the slide as the “spot rate expected in future”. It is a forecast of what the market's prevailing spot rate may be when that later date arrives. It can differ from today's contractually fixed forward rate because forecasts are uncertain and a forward rate can include risk or liquidity compensation. The lecturer's definition alone does not assert that the derived 5.0097% forward rate will become the observed year-two borrowing rate.

Yield to maturity (YTM) is the slide's “IRR on interest-bearing instrument.” It is the single rate at which the present value of a bond's remaining promised cash flows equals its current price, under the payment and compounding convention. For slide 29, it is the 3.96%, 4.90% or 5.81% calculated after the respective spot-rate bond price. That IRR is based on the promised payments and need not equal the return actually realised if a bond is sold early or fails to pay as promised.

The economic meaning and limits of the law of one price

The government-bond table is an application of the preceding slide's law of one price. A year-one coupon of 80 should carry the same discount factor 0.9709 across bonds A, B and C if it is truly the same risk-adjusted dated claim. Likewise, the year-two 80-unit coupon in B and C receives the same 0.9246 factor. Prices of different whole bonds need not be equal, because their payment patterns differ. The constraint is that identical portfolios of dated cash flows should have consistent prices when currency, credit and other contractual rights match and markets can trade the claims freely.

For an investment analyst this makes spot-rate pricing useful for valuing a stream of promised future payments, while YTM is useful for summarising each resulting bond price as one number for communication or comparison. A finance team planning dated obligations should avoid replacing a maturity-specific spot curve with a single quoted YTM just because the latter is easier to remember. It can distort the relative value of short and long payments. The illustrated rates are stated, stylised exercise assumptions rather than current government-bond market observations, and the model abstracts from credit spreads, bid–ask costs, coupon-date accrual and time-varying risk.

Common errors are to discount every payment using the bond's 8% coupon rate; use the year-four 6% spot rate for all four payments of Bond C; use r₂ to mean the rate only during year 2; add the 1000 principal twice to the final 1080; treat the YTM as a mechanical average of the spot rates; compare whole bond prices without checking maturity and coupon cash flows; put 1076.19 rather than 107.619 into an Excel function expecting a quote per 100; or mistake slide 30's forward rate for a known future spot rate. The way to keep them straight is to ask what the rate applies to: one dated payment, one forward time interval, a future forecast or one bond's total cash-flow stream.

Spot-rate curves and competing term-structure theories

Having defined spot rates, real rates and inflation, return to the alternative theories of yield-curve shape.

Slide 31 emphasizes that the spot rate rt is the interest rate for a zero-coupon security delivering one single payment at time t. The slide's historical example is titled Spot Rate on US Treasury TIPS, 02/2012. Its graph plots spot rate (%) vertically, about 0%–4%, against maturity (years) horizontally, roughly 1–29. Rates rise from near zero at the very shortest terms to approximately 3.4% at the far end, with progressively flatter slope. The date 02/2012 labels a historical picture, not today's market.

Slide 32 asks why an investor might prefer short-term Treasuries even when long-term bonds offer higher stated interest rates. It lists (1) expectations hypothesis: a belief short-term rates will be higher in future; (2) risk averseness: fear of the greater price exposure of long bonds when rates change; and (3) inflation-premium theory: concern about higher future inflation. These are three distinct classroom explanations for the yield curve.

Slide 33 revisits these theories: under the unbiased expectations theory, forward/long-term yields reflect expected subsequent short-term rates under the assumptions of that theory. If investors care about uncertainty in future real rates, they may choose maturities that match their investment horizons, so risk preferences could twist the term structure either way. If inflation is the key uncertainty, investors may demand additional compensation to hold long bonds, consistent with an inflation premium.

Case study: lock in two years or roll over after one year?

Slide 34 gives a one-year spot rate r₁ = 5% and a two-year spot rate r₂ = 7% per annum. Investing $100 for one year produces $105, while locking in two years produces $114.49.

Year-1 payoff = $100 × 1.05 = $105;   Year-2 payoff = $100 × (1.07)² = $114.49
Implied second-year forward rate = (1.07)² / 1.05 − 1 = 9.038...% ≈ 9%

The slide asks whether the investor would be happy earning the extra 9% in the second year of a two-year investment. This is a breakout question, not a statement of investor preferences. The 9% is an implied one-year forward rate, not a guaranteed future one-year spot rate.

The discussion broadens from corporations to sovereign issuers, where currency denomination and monetary authority matter.
