I. Price a bond

Start with the promised cash flows, then discount them with the right rate and payment frequency.

Identify bond cash flows and the investor’s claim

Would you rather lend money to a government or buy a piece of it? A bond is the lending deal.

What are you actually buying?

A bond is a debt security: the issuer borrows money from investors and promises payments on specified dates. The bondholder is a creditor, not an owner of the issuer. A government can issue bonds to raise funds, just as a company can borrow to finance its activities.

A bond and a loan both create an obligation to repay borrowed money, generally with interest. A conventional bank loan is usually negotiated with a lender, whereas a bond is typically issued as a standardized security that can be traded between investors. Selling a bond normally changes who receives its future payments, not the issuer’s promised payment schedule. This is the teaching explanation of the breakout questions in slide 3, not a recorded lecturer answer.

Quick check: What does purchasing an ordinary bond make you?

Answer and why

A creditor. The issuer owes the promised debt payments; a bond does not normally confer ownership.

Follow the cash flows

For the fixed-payment, non-defaulting bond used in this lecture, a coupon payment is the periodic interest paid to the investor; the face value (principal) is the amount promised back at maturity, the final repayment date. The coupon rate determines interest relative to face value, not relative to the market purchase price.

Worked example · preview of the French government bond (slide 4)
  1. GIVEN: principal €100; €5 annual coupon; five payment dates, 2025–2029, from the lecturer’s schedule.
  2. Separate the last payment into its parts: €5 interest + €100 principal = €105.
  3. Teaching bridge: undiscounted receipts = 5 × €5 + €100 = €125.
  4. Interpretation: €25 is coupon income and €100 is repayment of principal. €125 is total promised cash received over time, not today’s bond price. Discounting is covered in the next topic.

Quick check: What is the total promised payment in the final year of this schedule?

Answer and why

€105: the final €5 coupon plus €100 principal. €100 omits interest; €125 is the total over all five years.

  • A bond is a debt security, not an ownership share.
  • Coupons are periodic interest; principal is returned at maturity.
  • Watch out: total promised cash over time is not the price today.

Price annual coupons and principal

The French bond promises €125 in total. Why is its value today only €112.11?

Discount every promised payment

Present value (PV) is what a future payment is worth today at the required return. A bond’s value is the sum of those discounted payments. If the question supplies a discount rate and future cash flows, solve for price; if it supplies price and asks for the rate, use the same equation in reverse (next topic).

PV = C1/(1+r) + C2/(1+r)2 + … + (FV+CN)/(1+r)N

PV = Σt=1N [Ct/(1+r)t] + FV/(1+r)N

Ct = coupon in currency at period t; FV = face value in currency; r = required yield per period (decimal); N = number of periods; t = payment period. For fixed annual coupons, C = coupon rate × FV. Assume payments at period ends, a common discount rate r > −1 and repayment as promised.

Worked example A · French government bond
  1. GIVEN: valuation at the beginning of 2024–2029 five-year interval as in the slide’s Excel dates (1 Jan 2024 to 1 Jan 2029); payment years 2025–2029, FV = €100, annual coupon €5, annual required rate 2.4%.
  2. Normalize: r = 0.024, N = 5, C = €5. Final payment €5 + €100 = €105.
  3. Substitute: 5/1.024 + 5/1.0242 + 5/1.0243 + 5/1.0244 + 105/1.0245.
  4. Discounted components (€): 4.882813 + 4.768372 + 4.656613 + 4.547474 + 93.258734.
  5. Sum before rounding: €112.11400453 ≈ €112.11, matching the lecture.
  6. Sanity check: above €100 face value because the 5% coupon rate exceeds 2.4%; below €125 total promised receipts because the discount rate is positive.

Quick check: Which amount belongs in the numerator for year five?

Answer and why

€105. The last coupon and principal arrive together; €100 omits the coupon and €125 counts earlier coupons twice.

Use the shortcut when coupons are equal

An annuity is a series of equal payments at regular intervals. For a fixed coupon, price the annuity and principal separately; when coupons vary, use the individual cash-flow method above.

PV(bond) = PV(coupon annuity) + PV(final principal)

PV = C[1/r − 1/(r(1+r)N)] + FV/(1+r)N

C is the constant coupon; the bracket is the N-period annuity factor. Use r ≠ 0; teaching bridge: at r = 0 the value is simply N × C + FV.

  1. Coupon PV = 5[1/0.024 − 1/(0.024 × 1.0245)] = €23.29616256.
  2. Principal PV = 100/1.0245 = €88.81784197.
  3. Total = €112.11400453 ≈ €112.11. Slide 5 displays €23.30 + €88.82; these individually rounded components add to €112.12, so only round the total at the end.

Excel (lecture): =PRICE(DATE(2024,1,1),DATE(2029,1,1),0.05,0.024,100,1)

Settlement, maturity, annual coupon rate, annual yield, redemption per 100 face and annual payment frequency. PRICE quotes per €100 face; here that equals the bond value in euros.

Quick check: A bond has different coupon amounts each year. Which method fits?

Answer and why

Use the general sum. The annuity shortcut assumes a constant coupon.

Your turn · ILLUSTRATIVE: Same French bond, but the annual required return increases to 6%. What is its price to two decimal places?

Hint and worked answer

Keep the €5 coupons and five years unchanged; use r = 0.06. PV = 5[1 − 1.06−5]/0.06 + 100/1.065 = €95.78763621 ≈ €95.79. The yield exceeds the 5% coupon rate, so the result is below face value.

Remember

  • Price = PV of coupons + PV of principal.
  • Use cash-flow-by-cash-flow discounting for irregular coupons; use the annuity shortcut for level coupons.
  • Watch out: never omit or double-count the final coupon or round intermediate values too soon.

Find the yield to maturity from a price

You pay €112.11 for a bond with a €5 annual coupon. Are you actually earning 5%?

Reverse the bond pricing formula

Yield to maturity (YTM) is the single discount rate that makes the present value of all promised future payments equal to the current bond price. The lecture’s French bond pays regular interest but repays only €100 at maturity, less than the purchase price. Yield blends coupon income with that eventual loss relative to the amount paid.

Recognize the problem by what is missing: a given price and known future payments mean solve for yield; a given yield means solve for price (annual pricing). Do not simply divide the coupon by face value. That tells you the coupon rate, not the return on the purchase.

112.11 = 5/(1+y) + 5/(1+y)2 + 5/(1+y)3 + 5/(1+y)4 + 105/(1+y)5

y is the annual YTM in decimal form, with annual end-of-period cash flows and y > −1. All cash flows are in euros. Assume coupons and principal are paid as promised. The same y discounts every payment.

Worked example · solve rather than price
  1. GIVEN: price €112.11, face value €100, coupon €5 annually, N = 5; final receipt €105. Unknown: y.
  2. Normalize the time periods to years; use the displayed equation, with a trial decimal rate in every denominator.
  3. Teaching bridge: try 2% (y=0.02): the five discounted payments sum to €114.140379, which is above the price. Try 3% (0.03): the sum is €109.159414, below the price. The yield is between these rates.
  4. Try 2.4%: 5/1.024 + 5/1.0242 + 5/1.0243 + 5/1.0244 + 105/1.0245 = €112.114005, which rounds to €112.11. Solving exactly against the rounded price gives y ≈ 0.024007996.
  5. Answer: YTM ≈ 2.40% per year. The tiny difference from 2.4% reflects rounding of the quoted price. Verify by substituting the rate back into the price equation.
  6. Sanity check: 2.40% is below the 5% coupon rate, consistent with paying more than face value and receiving €100 principal at maturity.

For these positive promised cash flows, the implied price falls continuously as the rate rises, so searching between trial rates works. A spreadsheet or financial calculator can do the search rather than testing dozens of rates by hand. YTM is a promised cash-flow return measure, not a guarantee of the return you actually achieve.

Quick check: The trial rate produces a price above the given market price. What should you do next?

Answer and why

Increase the trial yield. Higher discount denominators lower the present value of the same promised payments.

Your turn · ILLUSTRATIVE: The same French bond now sells for exactly €100. Find its annual YTM as a percentage, to two decimals.

Hint and worked answer

Try the coupon rate: 5/100=0.05. Substitution gives 5[1−1.05−5]/0.05+100/1.055=€100. Therefore y=0.05, or 5.00%. At face value, this fixed-coupon bond's YTM equals its coupon rate.

Enter the percentage, not the decimal. Check the case where price equals face value and substitute the trial rate into all five denominators.

Remember

  • Price known, yield unknown: solve the PV equation for y.
  • Higher trial yield means lower implied price, other things equal.
  • Watch out: the coupon rate is not necessarily the YTM.

Apply annual bond pricing to IBM and Japan

Why would anyone pay $1,161.84 for a bond that repays just $1,000?

Case B: IBM's corporate bond

Separate the rate printed on the bond from the return investors require. The coupon rate determines the promised payment; the required yield determines how much that payment is worth today. The IBM case uses the Wall Street Journal AAA yield of 7.5% as the required return for an AAA-rated bond. These are historical case inputs, not current market quotes.

Worked example B · IBM
  1. GIVEN: FV = $1,000; annual coupon rate 11.5%, paid every September 30 for five years; additional $1,000 principal at maturity; annual required YTM 7.5%.
  2. Normalize: C = 0.115 × 1,000 = $115/year; r = 0.075; N = 5. Assume five full annual periods and promised payments occur.
  3. Use the cash-flow PV formula: 115/1.075 + 115/1.0752 + 115/1.0753 + 115/1.0754 + 1115/1.0755.
  4. Discounted amounts ($): 106.976744 + 99.513250 + 92.570465 + 86.112061 + 776.662875 = 1161.835396.
  5. Result: $1,161.84, matching slide 11. Sanity check: 11.5% > 7.5%, so the value is above face value, but below $1,575 undiscounted receipts.

Quick check: Which rate belongs in the IBM discount denominator?

Answer and why

7.5% is the required return. The 11.5% coupon rate determines the $115 payment.

Case C: Japanese government bond

Price bonds with semiannual payments

Three years does not always mean three cash flows. What happens when the bond pays you twice a year?

Match the payment and discount periods

Semiannual means once every six months. A bond paying twice per year divides its annual coupon into two equal instalments. Count half-year periods rather than calendar years and use the required return for each half-year. The annual bond pricing formula itself does not change; only the units of its inputs change.

C = FV × annual coupon rate / 2; N = 2 × years

PV = Σk=1N C/(1+r)k + FV/(1+r)N

C is the half-year coupon in USD, FV is principal in USD, k counts half-years, N is the total number of six-month periods, and r is the required return per half-year in decimal form (r > −1). If r is already semiannual, do not divide it again. For a nominal annual yield quoted with semiannual compounding, divide that quote by two; an effective annual yield instead requires r = √(1+annual effective yield) − 1 (teaching bridge).

Worked example D · US Treasury note
  1. GIVEN: February 2022 purchase, three years, $1,000 face value, 11.25% annual coupon, semiannual payments, required semiannual return 0.085%. Assume six full periods and payment as promised.
  2. Normalize: C = $1,000 × 0.1125/2 = $56.25; N = 3 × 2 = 6; r = 0.085/100 = 0.00085. Final payment = $1,056.25.
  3. Substitute: PV = 56.25/1.00085 + 56.25/1.000852 + 56.25/1.000853 + 56.25/1.000854 + 56.25/1.000855 + 1056.25/1.000856.
  4. Discounted amounts ($): 56.202228 + 56.154497 + 56.106806 + 56.059156 + 56.011546 + 1050.879115 = 1331.413347 (unrounded sum).
  5. Result: $1,331.41. Check: positive discounting puts it below $1,337.50 in total undiscounted cash flows; the half-year coupon rate 5.625% is higher than 0.085%, so it is above $1,000 face value.

Fact check · slide 15: printed overlay $1,334 and underlying $1,331.40 differ from the six displayed discounted terms, which give $1,331.41.

Quick check: The annual coupon is 11.25%, but the required 0.085% return is already semiannual. What goes in the denominator?

Answer and why

The yield is already stated per half-year. Only an annual nominal quote would need dividing by two.

The Treasury still pays the same coupon and principal. Investors now demand a higher return, so they pay less for that fixed schedule. Change only the discount rate in the valuation formula, not the coupon.

Read price–yield curves and identify premium or discount

Two bonds pay the same coupon rate today. Why does a change in interest rates hurt one investor much more than the other?

Read the axes before interpreting the curve

Slide 16 shows a Treasury yield curve, which compares yields at different maturities at a particular observation time. Its horizontal labels are 1M, 3M, 6M, 2Y, 5Y, 10Y and 30Y; the vertical axis shows yield in percent, with labelled ticks from 4.0% to 6.0% in half-percentage-point increments. The historical latest, week-ago and month-ago lines generally slope upwards. At the long end the week-ago line is highest; the headline about surging yields is not evidence that every maturity rose versus every earlier observation. The exact date and underlying data are not supplied.

Slide 18 is a different graph: on its horizontal axis the rate changes, while its vertical axis shows the value of a bond. Holding promised cash flows fixed, increasing yield reduces each discounted payment's value. A bond sells at par when price equals face value, at a premium when price exceeds face value, and at a discount when price is below face value.

Compare the two maturities

A longer bond exposes more distant payments to discounting. Around the coupon-rate crossing, its price generally moves more strongly than a comparable short bond. The effect also depends on the coupon and yield; the lecturer compares otherwise similar bonds. Read the steepness, not just which line is higher. For a precise measure of this exposure, see duration.

Worked teaching bridge · reconstruct the par crossing and compare prices
  1. GIVEN: slide 18 compares 3 and 30 years, face $1,000, annual coupon 11.25%. For this redraw assume two payments a year and nominal annual yield y; promised payments at period ends.
  2. Normalize: C = 1,000 × 0.1125/2 = $56.25; N = 6 or 60; r = y/2. Use the level-coupon formula, not the coupon rate as a substitute for yield: PV = 56.25[1−(1+r)−N]/r + 1000/(1+r)N (r ≠ 0; at zero use C × N + FV).
  3. At y = 11.25%, r = 0.05625: 56.25/0.05625 = 1,000. Therefore PV = 1,000[1−1.05625−N]+1,000/1.05625N = $1,000 for both maturities.
  4. Illustrative rate increase to 12% (+0.75 percentage points): r = 0.06. For N = 6, PV = 56.25[1−1.06−6]/0.06+1000/1.066 = $981.56. For N = 60, replace both powers with 60: PV = $939.39.
  5. Both are now below $1,000; the 30-year value falls further. This matches the slide's sensitivity conclusion around par.

Quick check: If the required yield falls below 11.25% in the slide 18 comparison, how do both bonds trade?

Answer and why

At a premium. The promised coupon rate is higher than the required yield under the same annual convention.

  • Bond prices and yields move in opposite directions for fixed promised payments.

  • Watch out: a yield curve plots maturity versus yield; a price–yield curve plots yield versus price.

II. Measure interest-rate risk

Move from the direction of price changes to the timing and size of exposure.

Calculate the weighted timing of bond cash flows

Both bonds mature in seven years. Why does the one with smaller coupons respond more to a yield change?

Compare coupon size and price sensitivity

Not all of a bond's value arrives at maturity. Larger coupons return more value earlier; smaller coupons leave more of the present value in the final payment. Earlier payments are less exposed to discounting over time. Slide 20 isolates this effect by holding the maturity and face value constant. The yield falls from 4% to 3% for both bonds.

Worked comparison · seven-year bonds
  1. GIVEN: face $1,000, seven annual periods, coupon rates 3% and 9%; yields 4% and 3% per year.
  2. Normalize: annual payments $30 or $90, final payments $1,030 or $1,090; decimal yields 0.04 and 0.03. Apply the level-coupon PV formula to both.
  3. At 4%: 30[1−1.04−7]/0.04+1000/1.047 = $939.98; replacing 30 with 90 gives $1300.10.
  4. At 3%: 30[1−1.03−7]/0.03+1000/1.037 = $1000.00; with 90, $1373.82.
  5. Relative price change = (new price − old price)/old price × 100%. Using unrounded values, the 3% bond rises 6.3853% and the 9% bond rises 5.6699%. Both rise when yield falls by 1 percentage point, but the low-coupon bond rises more in relative terms.

Fact check · slide 20: visible percentage increases 6.38% and 5.67% (underlying text 6.00%, 5.37%). Recalculation gives 6.3853% and 5.669878%; standard rounding is 6.39% and 5.67%.

Quick check: Which of these otherwise comparable bonds is more sensitive in relative price terms?

Answer and why

The 3% bond has more value concentrated in the distant principal payment. Its relative price rise is larger for this yield change.

Calculate the weighted average receipt time

Duration (Macaulay duration) is the average time at which promised cash flows arrive, weighted by their present-value shares. Unlike maturity, it accounts for intermediate coupons. Use this formula when asked for weighted timing, not when asked directly for a percentage price change. Think of each payment as a weight on a timeline: a large payment far in the future pulls the average to the right.

D = Σt=1T [t × PV(Ct)] / PV = 1×PV(C1)/PV + 2×PV(C2)/PV + … + T×PV(CT)/PV

t = receipt time in years; T = final year; Ct = total payment ($), including redemption at T; PV(Ct) = Ct/(1+y)t; PV = sum of discounted payments ($); y = annual yield as decimal. The weights are fractions of price and must sum to 1. Assume positive promised annual payments and y > −1. D is measured in years.

Worked example · 9% bond at 4% yield
  1. GIVEN: $90 annually for seven years, $1,000 redemption, final payment $1,090; y = 0.04.
  2. Discount each payment: year 1 is 90/1.04 = $86.538462; year 7 is 1090/1.047 = $828.310416. All seven PVs sum to $1,300.102733.
  3. Divide each PV by total price: year 1 weight = 86.538462/1300.102733 = 0.066563; year 7 weight = 828.310416/1300.102733 = 0.637112.
  4. Multiply each weight by time: 1 × 0.066563 = 0.066563 years; 7 × 0.637112 = 4.459781 years. The table shows every intermediate result.
  5. Sum using full precision: D = 5.688417 years ≈ 5.69 years. Check: weights sum to 1 and D lies between the first and last receipt dates, 1 and 7 years.

Slide 21 calls the next measure “Volatility (%)”: Dmod = D/(1+y) for annual compounding. For this case it is 5.469632. This is modified duration, used to estimate how much the price moves rather than when payments arrive. Slide 19 prints price change = volatility × interest-rate change; the signed relative price approximation requires a minus sign, ΔP/P ≈ −DmodΔy with Δy in decimals. See the next sensitivity calculation for the complete application.

Quick check: Why is the seven-year bond's duration below seven?

Answer and why

Earlier coupons carry positive PV weights. Their timing pulls the weighted average below seven years, without altering maturity.

Remember

  • Duration is the PV-weighted average cash receipt time.
  • Lower coupons generally mean longer duration at the same maturity and yield.
  • Watch out: use discounted value weights, not undiscounted cash-flow shares, and include principal in the last payment.

Calculate duration with half-year cash flows

Twelve payments do not mean twelve years. How do you calculate duration when cash flows arrive every six months?

Keep time and rates in matching units

The same Macaulay duration method works for semiannual coupons. But Case Study II measures time in years, not in the number of payments. Its first receipt comes after half a year, so the first time weight is 0.5. The second comes after one year, and the twelfth after six years.

Look at the original discount expression: 5.63/(1 + .02)0.5. It identifies 2% as an effective annual discount rate for this example. Do not halve that rate and also halve the exponent. The alternative is to convert to an equivalent six-month rate and use the integer payment index in the discount factor, while still weighting duration by years.

PVk = Ck/(1+y)k/2; P = Σk=112 PVk; D = Σk=112 [(k/2) × PVk/P]

k = payment number; t = k/2 years; Ck = cash payment (currency unspecified on slide); y = effective annual yield as decimal, y > −1; P = total present value, in the payment currency. Assume equally spaced, positive promised cash receipts. The weights are fractions, not percentages entered as whole numbers.

Worked example · Case Study II

Estimate price changes using modified duration

Rates rise by half a percentage point. Can we estimate the loss without discounting all seven payments again?

Turn timing into price sensitivity

Recall Macaulay and modified duration. The lecturer calls the latter “volatility.” It estimates the local relative price response to a small yield change. When asked for a quick approximate price change, use modified duration; when asked for the exact new price, discount the cash flows at the new yield. They are not interchangeable.

Dmod = D/(1+y); ΔP/P ≈ −Dmod × Δy; ΔP ≈ −P × Dmod × Δy.

D is duration in years; y is annual yield in decimal form (y > −1); P is the original price in dollars; Δy is the signed rate change in decimals. Dmod measures sensitivity to the annual rate. Multiply the relative change by 100 for percent, or by P for dollars. Assume fixed promised annual cash flows and a small yield movement; the minus sign captures the inverse relationship noted in slide 19.

Worked example · 9% seven-year bond, slide 23
  1. GIVEN: original annual yield 4%, face $1,000, annual coupon $90, seven years; original price $1,300.102733 and D = 5.688417 years from slide 22. Lecturer rounds price to $1,300 and duration to 5.69.
  2. Normalize: y = 0.04, Dmod = 5.688417439/1.04 = 5.469632 ≈ 5.47 (lecture: 5.69/1.04).
  3. Yield rises to 4.5%: Δy = +0.005 (0.5 percentage points). ΔP/P ≈ −5.469632 × 0.005 = −0.02734816 = −2.7348%. Estimated price = 1300.102733(1−0.02734816) ≈ $1264.55.
  4. Yield falls to 3.5%: Δy = −0.005. Estimated change = +2.7348%; price ≈ $1335.66.
  5. Exact repricing: P(y) = 90[1−(1+y)−7]/y + 1000/(1+y)7. Substitute y=0.045: $1265.171542; substitute y=0.035: $1336.299919. Relative changes versus original P: -2.6868% and +2.7842%.
  6. Sanity check: rates up means price down; both movements are near 2.7%. A 1-percentage-point change near the original yield implies about 5.47% relative price movement, not a $5.47 move.

Fact check · slide 23: the slide gives −2.687% at 4.5% ($1,265), +2.687% at 3.5% ($1,336) and adds them to 5.47%. Two 2.687% magnitudes actually sum to 5.374%; the exact changes above sum in magnitude to 5.4710%, close to the 5.47% duration estimate, but not identical.

Why the estimate changes with the yield

The curve bends: at lower rates its slope is steeper, whereas at higher rates it flattens. The straight tangent gives the local approximation, and modified duration is the magnitude of that slope relative to price (with the sign reversed). Larger rate changes are less accurately represented by this straight line. The curve's shape also explains why a rate fall produces a larger exact gain than the loss from an equal rate rise in this example.

Remember

  • Modified duration estimates relative price sensitivity, not average receipt time.
  • Use the negative sign and decimal rate change.
  • Watch out: the tangent is an approximation; reprice to obtain the exact change.

III. Understand the yield curve

Compare maturities, value cash flows with maturity-specific rates, and investigate what determines the curve.

Read the term structure and distinguish interest rates

Can a one-year loan pay more interest than a thirty-year loan? The yield curve says yes.

Compare yields across maturities

The term structure of interest rates describes the relationship between yield and maturity at a given time. It need not move in parallel: short rates can rise while long rates fall. Slide 26 contrasts September 1992 with April 2000. The former shows an upward curve, where long-term yields exceed short-term yields; the latter shows an inverted curve, where the reverse is true. A third possibility is a humped curve, with intermediate yields above both ends.

Worked chart-reading example · September 1992 vs April 2000
  1. GIVEN: approximate visual endpoints of the original chart: September 1992 near 3.8% short and 7.4% long; April 2000 near 6.65% short and 5.95% long (approximately 1–30 years, not raw observations).
  2. Teaching bridge: use long yield minus short yield to express the slope between the endpoints in percentage points. September: 7.4−3.8 ≈ +3.6 pp. April: 5.95−6.65 ≈ −0.70 pp.
  3. Interpret: positive difference means rising overall; negative means inverted. The curves cross near nine years and 6.3%. The short end moves up while the long end moves down, so a single parallel shift cannot describe the comparison.

Quick check: If intermediate maturities pay higher yields than both short and long maturities, how would you describe the shape?

Answer and why

Humped: the middle is higher than both ends; an inverted curve falls as maturity increases.

Which interest rate is the question asking for?

A spot rate rt is today's rate for a maturity t, identifiable as the yield on a bond making just one payment at t. A forward rate is fixed today for a loan starting at a specified future time. The future rate in the lecturer's terminology is the spot rate expected to prevail in the future, which is uncertain rather than necessarily locked in. Recall that YTM is the internal rate of return (IRR) of an interest-bearing instrument's promised cash flows.

Slide 31 calls its curve US Treasury TIPS spot rates, February 2012. The plotted spot rate rises from roughly 0.15% toward 3.4% and flattens over longer maturities. These are visual estimates from the historical slide, not live rates; TIPS are inflation-protected Treasury securities. For a single payment, use its corresponding spot rate; for multiple receipts, the next topic shows how to combine different maturities' rates.

Teaching bridge · ILLUSTRATIVE single-payment bond
  1. GIVEN: €100 received in two years; hypothetical two-year annual effective spot rate 3%; no intermediate payments. t=2 years, r2=0.03.
  2. Choose single-payment PV, not a coupon annuity: PV = Ct/(1+rt)t = 100/1.032 = 100/1.0609.
  3. PV = €94.25959 ≈ €94.26. Positive discounting yields less than €100; this is the price consistent with the specified spot rate and promised cash flow.

Quick check: A one-year loan will start in two years, with its interest rate agreed today. Which rate is this?

Answer and why

Forward rate: a future loan's rate is agreed now. An expected future spot rate is a forecast.

Remember

  • The curve describes yields across maturities, not a bond's price sensitivity.
  • Spot rates are maturity-specific; forward rates fix future loan terms today.
  • Watch out: expected future spot rates are not guaranteed forward rates.
