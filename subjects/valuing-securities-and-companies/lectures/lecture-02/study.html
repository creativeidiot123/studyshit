Part I · Money across time

Start with single cash flows, their timing and the conventions used to express interest rates.

Compound money into the future

Slide 3: FV(t)=PV(t₀)×(1+r)^t; PV(t₀)=FV(t)/(1+r)^t. FV is amount after earning interest (time value and risk; compounding); PV is today’s value of a future cash flow.

Slide 4: Initial $100, annual r=7%, t=2: FV=$100×1.07×1.07=$100×(1+.07)^2=$114.49; timeline today $100 → year 2 $114.49.

Slide 5: Power of compounding: initial $100; x-axis number of years 0–20 (annual ticks), y-axis FV of $100, $0–$1,800 ($200 ticks); 0%, 5%, 10%, 15% lines, all start at $100; 0% flat, others increasingly steep; at year 20 slide labels $265 (5%), $673 (10%). Recomputed endpoints: $100, $265.33, $672.75, $1,636.65 respectively.

Related topics: Reverse compounding and discount factors.

Discount a future cash flow back to today

Slide 6: Reverse compounding: FV(t)=PV(t₀)×(1+r)^t; PV(t₀)=Cₜ/(1+r)^t; PV=DF₂×C₂=[1/(1+.07)^2]×114.49=100. Discount rate r versus discount factor 1/(1+r)^t.

Slide 7: DF is PV of $1 received in t years; DF=1/(1+r)^t; PV=DF×Cₜ; discount factors can value any cash flow.

Slide 8: Higher interest rate lowers PV; safe €/$ worth more than risky one. x-axis years 0–20, annual ticks; y-axis PV of $100, 0–120 in $20 ticks. Curves 0%, 5%, 10%, 15%, same start $100; 0% flat, higher rates drop faster; end labels $38 (5%), $15 (10%); independently computed year-20 values $100, $37.69, $14.86, $6.11.

Related topics: Forward compounding example · Risk and discount-rate sensitivity.

Convert nominal and effective interest rates

Slide 36: EAR effective annual interest rate: annualized using compound interest; APR annual percentage rate: annualized using simple interest (lecture nominal-rate convention).

Slide 37: Monthly rate 1%: the slide first writes EAR=(1+.01)^12−1=r, then EAR=(1+.01)^12−1=.1268 or 12.68% (recomputed 12.682503%); APR=.01×12=.12 or 12.00%. General EAR=(1+r/m)^m−1; continuous discounting of $1= $1/e^(rt). Continuous compounding is listed among objectives/key terms. Notation flag: the first equality uses r for the effective annual rate, but the general EAR formula uses r for nominal annual APR, with m compounding periods per year. For this example the nominal r is .12 and m=12, giving .12682503 effective; the continuous discount formula uses a continuously compounded annual rate r with t in years. These rates must not be substituted interchangeably.

Related topics: Single-period compounding · Single-period discounting.

Part II · Investment decisions

Use discounting to judge a project, compare its return with its hurdle rate and value several cash flows.

Calculate NPV and decide whether to invest

Slide 9: Office building NPV, four steps (slides 9–10). Step 1 forecast cost of building C₀=$700k as cost magnitude (cash-flow sign negative), sale C₁=$800k at year 1. Step 2 opportunity cost of capital r=7% if comparably risky capital market investments earn 7%.

Slide 10: Step 3 PV=C₁/(1+r)=$800,000/1.07=$747,664 (actual $747,663.55). Step 4 proceed if PV exceeds initial investment (NPV>0): $747,664−$700,000=$47,664 (actual $47,663.55).

Slide 11: NPV is net contribution to shareholder wealth. NPV=PV−required investment=C₀+C₁/(1+r), with C₀ signed.

Slide 14: Accept positive-NPV investments. At 10% required return NPV=−$700,000+$800,000/1.1=$27,273 (actual $27,272.73) → accept.

Related topics: Risk sensitivity and hurdle-rate summary · Rate-of-return decision rule · Multiperiod DCF.

See how risk and the cost of capital change NPV

Slide 12: Riskier projects demand higher required returns; higher required returns reduce PV of positive payoff. $800,000 at 7%: PV=$800,000/(1+.07)=$747,664.

Slide 13: Source: 12%: $714,286−$700,000=$14,286; 7%: $747,664−$700,000=$47,664; 5%: $761,000−$700,000=$61,904. Recomputed: $14,285.71, $47,663.55, $61,904.76; 5% PV=$761,904.76, not printed $761,000; correct nearest-dollar NPV $61,905.

Slide 38: Shareholder interests: accept projects worth more than cost (positive NPV); hurdle/discount/opportunity cost decisive; negative initial investment C₀. NPV=C₀+C₁/(1+r)+C₂/(1+r)^2+C₃/(1+r)^3+…=C₀+PV. Market returns determine r: safe CFs use safe security returns (US government bonds example); uncertain expected CFs use equivalent-risk securities' expected return. Discount for time (today vs tomorrow) and risk (safe vs risky).

Related topics: Office-building steps and NPV rule · Multiperiod NPV equation.

Compare the project return with its hurdle rate

Slide 15: Accept projects with return above opportunity cost of capital. At benchmark 12%, one-year building return=profit/investment=($800,000−$700,000)/$700,000=.143 or 14.3% (14.2857%); accept.

Slide 16: Slide calls NPV and IRR/return decision rules generally interchangeable: accept positive NPV; accept return exceeding opportunity cost of capital. The one-period project supports equivalence; general equivalence is qualified (cash-flow patterns/ranking).

Related topics: Original office-building NPV · 12% hurdle-rate sensitivity.

Discount several cash flows with DCF

Slide 17: DCF multiperiod: PV₀=C₁/(1+r)^1+C₂/(1+r)^2+…+Cₜ/(1+r)^t=Σ(t=1…T) Cₜ/(1+r)^t. NPV₀=C₀+Σ(t=1…T) Cₜ/(1+r)^t.

Slide 18: Office rented for first two years $30,000 annually, sold end year 2 for $840,000. Timeline year 0 −$700,000; year 1 +$30,000; year 2 +$870,000. At r=12%, slide shows $30,000/1.12=$26,786; $870,000/1.12²=$693,559; NPV=$20,344. Full-precision PVs $26,785.71 and $693,558.67; NPV $20,344.39 (rounded intermediate sums differ by $1).

Slide 39: Financial markets (stocks and bonds) trade and value safe/risky future CFs; PV estimates what people pay given investment alternatives. DCF always works under suitable discounting; shortcuts efficient: perpetuity level forever, annuity level finite, growing streams also have shortcuts; rate depends on payment frequency.

Related topics: Single-period NPV · Level finite payment shortcut · Financial shortcuts summary · Interest-rate frequency.

Part III · Level cash flows

Use financial shortcuts for regular payments, loans and accumulated savings.

Value ordinary, due and deferred perpetuities

Slide 19: Perpetuity: equal annual payments starting in one year, received theoretically forever. Return=cash flow/present value, r=C/PV.

Slide 20: Ordinary perpetuity PV₀=C₁/r. Perpetuity due PV₀=C₀+C/r=(C/r)×(1+r) when C₀=C; first payment today versus year 1.

Slide 21: Bill and Melinda Gates hypothetical: $1bn each year forever at 10%; PV=$1bn/.10=$10bn.

Slide 22: Postponed perpetuity: slide says investment does not start making money for 3 years; formula PV=($1bn/.10)×(1/1.10³)=$7.51bn; slide says −25% by delaying payments 3 years. Recomputed $7.513148bn, decline 24.87%; formula represents first payment at t=4 (ordinary t=1 stream shifted three years), not t=3.

Related topics: Discount factors and time shifts.

Value a level annuity today

Slide 23: Annuity: fixed sum each year for specified number of years. Table headings Asset | Year of Payment (1, 2…t, t+1…) | PV. First perpetuity year 1: C at all dates, C(1/r). Second begins t+1: C thereafter, C(1/r)(1/(1+r)^t). Difference is C for years 1…t only, PV=C[1/r−(1/r)(1/(1+r)^t)].

Slide 24: Tiburon Autos $5,000 each year-end years 1–5, r=7%; slide year-by-year PVs $4,673, $4,367, $4,081, $3,814, $3,565; total $20,501. PV=C(1/r−(1/r)×1/(1+r)^5)=C(1/r)[1−1/(1+r)^5]. Full-precision total $20,500.99.

Slide 25: PV annuity=C[1/r−1/(r(1+r)^t)]=(C/r)[1−1/(1+r)^t]. Bracketed factors excluding C labelled annuity factor.

Slide 26: Lottery headline $365m, 30 year-end installments $12.167m, r=6%. Lottery value=12.167×[1/.06−1/(.06(1+.06)^30)] million, slide $167,500,000 (actual $167,476,700.62, rounded to ~$167.5m). Factor 13.76483115; rounded installment totals $365.01m nominal.

Related topics: Perpetuity derivation prerequisite · Slide 35 formula comparison and annuity-due correction.

Calculate loan payments and amortization

Slide 27: Mortgage PV=$250,000, 12% annual, 30 equal annual installments. PV=payment×30-year annuity factor; payment=PV/factor. Factor [1/.12−1/(.12(1+.12)^30)]=8.055. Slide $250,000/8.055=$31,037. Full precision factor 8.05518397, payment $31,035.91; rounded factor gives $31,036.62 (~$31,037).

Slide 28: Amortizing loan $1,000 electric scooter, 4 years, 10% annual, annual payment=$1,000/(4-year annuity factor)=$315.47 (actual $315.4708037). Table headings Year | Beginning of year balance | Year end interest on balance | Total year end payment | Amortization of loan | End of year balance, all USD: 1 | 1000 | 100 | 315.47 | 215.47 | 784.53; 2 | 784.53 | 78.45 | 315.47 | 237.02 | 547.51; 3 | 547.51 | 54.75 | 315.47 | 260.72 | 286.79; 4 | 286.79 | 28.68 | 315.47 | 286.79 | 0.00. Interest falls, principal rises.

Related topics: Annuity factor and PV.

Calculate the future value of an annuity

Slide 29: FV annuity=C[1/r−1/(r(1+r)^t)]×(1+r)^t=C[((1+r)^t−1)/r]; nested braces mark annuity factor, PV annuity and FV annuity.

Slide 30: $20,000 paid end of each of next 5 years, investment return 8% per year: FV=20,000×[((1+.08)^5−1)/.08]=$117,332 (actual $117,332.0192).

Related topics: Annuity present value · Slide 35 shortcut table; annuity-due vs. FV.

Part IV · Growing cash flows and formula selection

Extend the shortcuts to growing streams and compare the timing and structure of the formulas.

Value a growing perpetuity

Slide 31: Constant growth perpetuity: PV₀=C₁/(1+r)+C₂/(1+r)^2+C₃/(1+r)^3+…; source expanded numerator term 3 is C₂(1+g)^2, term 2 C₁(1+g); formula PV=C₁/(r−g). Consistent term 3 should be C₁(1+g)^2 (or C₂(1+g)); converges under ordinary r>g. Formula values a perpetuity at any date using next-period CF; corporate valuation terminal/continuing value.

Slide 32: Growing perpetuity: first year-end payment $1bn, return 10%, growth 4%; PV₀=$1bn/(.10−.04)=$16.667bn (actual $16.6666667bn).

Related topics: Level perpetuity · Growing annuity.

Value a growing annuity and select shortcuts

Slide 33: Growing annuity: golf membership $5,000 for one year, or $13,750 today for 3 years; year-end payments increase 6%, discount rate 10%. PV=C×1/(r−g)×[1−(1+g)^t/(1+r)^t]. Source substitution printed $5000×[1/(.10−.06)]×([1−(1.06)^3]/(1.10)^3)=$5,000×2.629=$13,147; parentheses in substitution conflict with correct boxed formula. Correct factor 2.629301277; $13,146.51≈$13,147 (annual option cheaper).

Slide 34: Growing annuity from two growing perpetuities. Headings Asset | Year of Payment (1,2,3,…t,t+1…) | present value. First: C,C(1+g),C(1+g)^2,…, C(1+g)^t at t+1; PV=C/(r−g). Second begins t+1 at C(1+g)^t; PV=[C/(r−g)]×(1+g)^t/(1+r)^t. Difference pays C,…,C(1+g)^(t−1) years 1…t. Slide final right-hand box shows [1/(r−g)−(1/(r−g))×(1+g)^t/(1+r)^t] without C; this is factor, multiply by C for PV.

Slide 35: Financial shortcuts, unit cash flows table: years 0,1,2,…,t−1,t,t+1; perpetuity 1 from year 1 forever, PV 1/r; t-period annuity 1 years 1…t, PV factor [1/r−1/(r(1+r)^t)]; row labelled t-period annuity due shows 1 at years 0…t−1, but formula [1/r−1/(r(1+r)^t)]×(1+r)^t=[(1+r)^t−1]/r is ordinary annuity FV factor, not due PV (which multiplies ordinary PV by 1+r); growing perpetuity 1,1(1+g),…,1(1+g)^t at t+1, factor 1/(r−g); t-period growing annuity 1,…,1(1+g)^(t−1) at t, factor [1−(1+g)^t/(1+r)^t]/(r−g).

Related topics: Level perpetuity · Level annuity · Ordinary annuity future value · Growth perpetuity derivation · Rate conventions.
