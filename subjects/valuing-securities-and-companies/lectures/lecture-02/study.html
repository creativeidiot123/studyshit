Part I · Money across time

Start with single cash flows, their timing and the conventions used to express interest rates.

Compound money into the future

Why does doubling the interest rate more than double the ending gain over twenty years?

Grow money by earning interest on interest

Imagine putting money aside today and leaving every cent of interest invested. Next year, interest is earned on the original amount and on last year’s interest. That process is compounding. The future value (FV) is what the amount grows to by a later date. The present value (PV) is its value today.

Use the forward formula when the question gives today’s amount and asks what it becomes later. If it gives a future payment and asks what it is worth today, use the inverse in the next topic instead. The rate reflects the time value of money and, in valuation, the risk of the cash flow.

FV(t) = PV(t0) × (1 + r)t
Inverse: PV(t0) = FV(t) / (1 + r)t

PV(t0) and FV(t) are money amounts at time zero and time t; r is the decimal interest rate per period and t is the number of matching periods. Here periods are years. Assume a constant annual rate, reinvested interest, no additional payments or withdrawals, and r > −1. At t = 0, the multiplier is one.

Lecturer example: $100 invested for two years

  1. Given: PV(t0) = $100; annual rate = 7%; t = 2 years.
  2. Normalize: r = 7/100 = 0.07 per year; two annual compounding periods.
  3. Substitute: FV(2) = $100 × (1 + 0.07)2.
  4. Calculate: year 1 = $100 × 1.07 = $107.00; year 2 = $107.00 × 1.07 = $114.49. Equivalently, 1.072 = 1.1449.
  5. Result: FV(2) = $114.49 (exact to cents; matches slide).
  6. Interpret/check: gain = $14.49; year-two interest = $7.49, more than year-one $7.00. Without interest on interest the ending value would be only $114.00.

All curves start at $100. A higher positive rate increases both the return on the initial investment and subsequent returns on accumulated interest. The curve gets steeper over time; at zero percent it remains flat. Notice that a rise from 5% to 10% is five percentage points, but the ending balance is much more than twice the 5% balance. Time magnifies the difference.

Quick check: You know today’s amount and want the balance in year 10. Which operation moves it forward?

Answer and why

Multiply by (1 + r)10. Dividing discounts back to today; 1 + 10r assumes simple interest and omits interest on interest.

Discount a future cash flow back to today

Would you pay $114.49 today for exactly $114.49 arriving in two years?

Reverse the direction of compounding

Suppose you can earn 7% by investing today. You do not need $114.49 now to have $114.49 in two years; you need only the amount that grows to it. Working backward from a future payment to today is discounting. Use this method when the question gives a future cash flow and asks what it is worth now. Do not multiply by the growth factor as you did for future value.

FV(t) = PV(t0) × (1 + r)t
PV(t0) = Ct / (1 + r)t = DFt × Ct
DFt = 1 / (1 + r)t

A discount rate r is the required return per period, expressed as a decimal. The discount factor DFt is the present value of one dollar received t periods from now; it has no units. Ct is the cash flow in dollars at time t. Assume a constant applicable rate, r > −1, and matching periods; here t counts years. Dividing by the same multiplier used for forward compounding cancels its growth.

Lecturer example: reverse the $114.49 investment

  1. Given: C2 = $114.49 in year 2; annual discount rate = 7%; t = 2 years.
  2. Normalize: r = 7/100 = 0.07 per year; t = 2 annual periods.
  3. Substitute: PV(t0) = $114.49 / (1 + 0.07)2 = DF2 × $114.49.
  4. Calculate: 1.072 = 1.1449; DF2 = 1/1.1449 ≈ 0.873439. Keep the unrounded factor internally.
  5. Result: $114.49 / 1.1449 = $100.00 today, matching the slide.
  6. Interpret/check: $100 × 1.1449 = $114.49; the reverse calculation returns the original investment. A positive rate makes PV smaller than the future positive payment.

Why higher rates mean lower present values

A dollar today can be invested, so receiving a dollar later has a time cost. A risky future dollar or euro is also worth less than an otherwise comparable safe one when the required return is higher. The slide’s curves keep the future payment fixed at $100 while changing the waiting time or the discount rate. More waiting at a positive rate, or a higher rate at a fixed positive time, enlarges the denominator and lowers PV. At 0%, or at t = 0, there is no discount. The risk-based choice of rate is developed in the cost-of-capital topic.

Quick check: You know a future payment and want today's value. Which method applies?

Answer and why

Multiply by the discount factor 1/(1 + r)t. The growth factor moves money forward, and subtracting r once ignores periodic compounding and the size of the cash flow.

Convert nominal and effective interest rates

How can a loan charging 1% a month cost more than 12% a year?

Choose the right annual rate

A monthly rate describes what happens in one month. To compare it with a yearly rate, decide whether you are simply quoting twelve months of interest or actually compounding the money. The annual percentage rate (APR), in the lecture’s nominal-rate convention, annualizes using simple interest. The effective annual interest rate (EAR) annualizes using compound interest and measures the actual one-year growth. Use APR for a nominal quotation; use EAR when comparing compounded annual returns.

APR = m × i
EAR = (1 + i)m − 1 = (1 + r/m)m − 1

Here i is the decimal rate per compounding period, m is the number of periods per year (positive integer), and r is the nominal annual rate in decimal form, with i = r/m and i > −1. Assume the periodic rate is constant and interest is reinvested. APR and EAR are both annual rates, but not interchangeable. With positive rates and more than one compounding period, EAR exceeds APR.

Lecturer example: 1% monthly

  1. Given: 1% each month, one year, 12 monthly periods.
  2. Normalize: i = 0.01 per month; m = 12 periods/year.
  3. APR: 0.01 × 12 = 0.12 = 12.00%.
  4. EAR: (1 + 0.01)12 − 1 = 1.1268250301… − 1 = 0.1268250301… ≈ 12.68%.
  5. Check: $100 becomes $112.682503… (display $112.68); nominal simple interest alone would give $112.00. The difference in rates is about 0.68 percentage points.

Notation check: The slide first writes EAR = (1 + .01)12 − 1 = r, using r as the effective rate, but its general formula uses r as nominal annual APR. In the general formula substitute .12, not .12682503. Both printed answers (12.68% and 12.00%) are correct.

Discount when compounding is continuous

If interest compounds continuously, use the exponential rather than a finite number of compounding periods. The lecturer gives the value today of $1 in year t. In this convention r means a continuously compounded rate per year, and t is elapsed years. The exponential denominator represents cumulative growth. The teaching bridge below reverses the same growth factor for any cash flow.

PV of $1 = $1 / ert
Teaching bridge: PV = Cte−rt; FV = PV × ert; annual effective rate = er − 1.

ILLUSTRATIVE: $1 received in two years

  3. Substitute: PV = $1/e0.12 × 2.
  4. Compute: e0.24 ≈ 1.271249; PV ≈ $0.7866 (about $0.79).
  5. Check: positive r and future receipt give PV below $1.

Answer and why

Part II · Investment decisions

Use discounting to judge a project, compare its return with its hurdle rate and value several cash flows.

Calculate NPV and decide whether to invest

A building costs $700,000 today and sells for $800,000 next year. Is the $100,000 apparent profit enough to justify buying it?

Compare everything at the same date

You could put the purchase money into another equally risky investment instead. The opportunity cost of capital is the expected return available from that alternative. It is the benchmark return this project must compensate you for giving up. Use it to discount the project's future proceeds before comparing them with the cost today.

Net present value (NPV) measures the extra value a project creates today, after covering the investment and the required return. For the shareholder, it is the net contribution to wealth. Use NPV when the question asks whether an investment creates value in money terms, rather than only asking for the present value of its proceeds.

PV = C1 / (1 + r)
NPV = PV − required investment = C0 + C1 / (1 + r)

C0 is the signed cash flow today (negative for a purchase), C1 is the cash flow at the end of year one, and r is the decimal annual discount rate. All monetary terms are in the same currency, here USD. This is the one-period case with no other cash flows and r > −1. For several payments, use multiperiod DCF.

Lecturer example: the four-step office-building decision

  1. Forecast cash flows (step 1): purchase cost $700,000 at t = 0; C0 = −$700,000. Forecast sale C1 = +$800,000 at t = 1.
  2. Choose the opportunity cost (step 2): equally risky market investments offer 7% annually. Normalize r = 7/100 = 0.07/year; one annual period.
  3. Discount the proceeds (step 3): PV = $800,000 / (1 + 0.07) = $800,000 / 1.07 = $747663.551402… ≈ $747,663.55. The lecturer rounds to $747,664.
  4. Subtract the investment and decide (step 4): NPV = −$700,000 + $800,000/1.07 = $47,663.55 (slide: $47,664). Recomputed using unrounded PV.
  5. Interpret and check: PV exceeds the cost; NPV is positive, so accept. The extra wealth today is $47,663.55, not the undiscounted $100,000 profit. Multiplying the unrounded PV by 1.07 recovers $800,000.

Apply the NPV rule at the required return

Accept an independent investment if NPV is positive; reject if it is negative. Zero means the project exactly meets the required return and creates no extra wealth under the forecast. A larger sale price alone is not enough; the dated cash flows and the benchmark matter.

Lecturer follow-up: required return rises to 10%

  1. Given: same C0 = −$700,000, C1 = $800,000 in one year; normalize 10% to 0.10/year (three percentage points above 7%).
  2. PV = $800,000 / 1.10 = $727272.727273… ≈ $727,272.73.
  3. NPV = −$700,000 + $800,000/1.10 = $27,272.73 (slide: $27,273).
  4. Still accept: NPV remains positive but is smaller than at 7%. Higher required return reduces the value of the same sale; see rate sensitivity.

Quick check: The PV of expected proceeds exceeds the purchase cost. What decision follows for an independent project?

Answer and why

Accept: NPV is positive. A negative cash flow at purchase is normal; the discounted benefits must exceed that cost. Nominal profit ignores timing and opportunity cost.

See how risk and the cost of capital change NPV

How can the same $800,000 future sale be worth less today without its forecast price changing?

Choose a return that reflects risk and time

Investors compare alternative investments. If a future payoff is uncertain, they generally demand a higher return for bearing more priced risk. The required return used as the discount rate is also called the hurdle rate. The lecture identifies two reasons for discounting: a dollar today is worth more than a dollar tomorrow (time), and an otherwise comparable safe dollar is worth more than a risky dollar (risk).

The benchmark comes from capital-market returns. For an absolutely safe cash flow, use the return on a matching safe security, such as a US government bond in the lecturer's example; for uncertain expected cash flows, use the expected return on securities with equivalent risk. Match currency and timing. Do not pick a rate merely because it makes a project look attractive.

Calculate sensitivity using the same project

When a question changes the cost of capital but leaves expected cash flows unchanged, recalculate the present value at each rate. Do not reduce the sale proceeds themselves or confuse a change in the hurdle rate with the project's actual return.

PV = C1 / (1 + r)
NPV = C0 + C1 / (1 + r)
General: NPV = C0 + C1/(1+r) + C2/(1+r)2 + C3/(1+r)3 + … = C0 + PV

As before, C0 is the signed initial cash flow, Ct is the expected cash flow (USD) at the end of year t, and r is the matching annual decimal discount rate, r > −1 (constant across the cash flows in this formula). For the building, only one future payment is forecast.

Lecturer example: change only the cost of capital

  1. Given: C0 = −$700,000 now; C1 = $800,000 in one year; baseline 7%, alternatives 12% and 5%.
  2. Normalize: rates = .07, .12, .05 per year; one annual period. The alternative rates change by +5 and −2 percentage points versus baseline.
  3. At 7.000000000000001%: PV = $800,000/(1 + 0.07) = $747,663.55 (rounded); NPV = $800,000/1.07 − $700,000 = $47,663.55.
  4. At 12%: PV = $800,000/(1 + 0.12) = $714,285.71 (rounded); NPV = $800,000/1.12 − $700,000 = $14,285.71.
  5. At 5%: PV = $800,000/(1 + 0.05) = $761,904.76 (rounded); NPV = $800,000/1.05 − $700,000 = $61,904.76.
  6. Interpret/check: NPV decreases as the rate rises, because the future positive cash flow is divided by a larger factor while the initial outlay is unchanged. All three cases still have positive NPV.

Fact check: The slide prints $714,286 − $700,000 = $14,286 at 12%, $747,664 − $700,000 = $47,664 at 7% (both valid nearest-dollar displays), and $761,000 − $700,000 = $61,904 at 5%. The last line contains a PV typo and a rounding discrepancy: actual PV is $761,904.76 and NPV is $61,904.76, or $61,905 rounded to dollars.

Positive NPV means an independent project is worth more than it costs and increases shareholder wealth under the assumptions. A higher discount rate makes that test harder for this project's positive future payoff. See multiperiod NPV for several dated payments.

Quick check: An expected positive future payoff and initial cost stay the same, but comparable investment risk raises the hurdle rate. What happens to this project's NPV?

Answer and why

Falls: a higher rate reduces the present value of the fixed positive future payoff. The initial cost remains unchanged.

Compare the project return with its hurdle rate

The building earns 14.3% in one year. Would you still buy it if your alternative earns 12%?

Measure the return the project itself offers

NPV tells you how many dollars of extra wealth an investment creates. The rate-of-return rule asks a different question: does the investment earn a greater percentage return than its opportunity cost of capital? For one initial outlay and one positive payoff a year later, the return is simply profit divided by the original investment. It is not the market's required return: that is the benchmark you compare it against.

Use this ratio for a single one-year investment with no intermediate cash flows. Do not divide by the final sale price, or use the single-year ratio for several differently timed payments.

Return = profit / investment = (C1 − I) / I

I = −C0 is the positive initial investment in USD (I > 0), C1 is the positive payoff after one year in USD. The ratio is dimensionless; multiply by 100 for the annual percentage.

Lecturer example: the building at a 12% benchmark

  1. Given: I = $700,000 at t = 0; C1 = $800,000 at t = 1; opportunity cost of capital = 12% per year.
  2. Normalize: cash flows already in USD; one annual period; required return r = .12.
  3. Substitute: return = ($800,000 − $700,000) / $700,000.
  4. Calculate: profit = $100,000; $100,000/$700,000 = 1/7 = 0.1428571429…
  5. Result: 14.285714…% ≈ 14.3% per year (slide: .143 or 14.3%).
  6. Interpret/check: 14.2857…% > 12%; accept. This is about 2.29 percentage points above the hurdle rate. Multiplying $700,000 by (1 + 1/7) recovers $800,000.

Connect the rate of return to NPV

The lecture describes two generally interchangeable rules: accept a project with a positive NPV, or accept a project that offers a rate of return above its opportunity cost of capital. The internal rate of return (IRR) is the discount rate at which NPV equals zero. In this one-year example, the return calculated above is exactly that rate.

Teaching bridge: 0 = −I + C1/(1 + IRR)
I(1 + IRR) = C1 ⇒ IRR = C1/I − 1

For this usual one-outlay, one-positive-payoff case, NPV falls as the hurdle rate rises. At the IRR, the project breaks even in present-value terms. Below it, NPV is positive and both rules accept; above it, both reject.

Qualification: The lecturer's equivalence works for independent conventional projects (initial outlay followed by nonnegative cash flows). Unconventional sign changes can produce multiple or no usable IRRs, and mutually exclusive projects can be ranked differently by IRR and NPV; use NPV to compare value created at the appropriate discount rate.

Quick check: For this conventional building project, if the hurdle rate exactly equals IRR, what is NPV?

Answer and why

Zero: IRR is the rate that makes the PV of the payoff equal the initial investment. Nominal profit ignores discounting; negative NPV occurs here only above the IRR.

Discount several cash flows with DCF

What if you rent out the building for two years instead of selling it after one?

Bring every payment back to the same date

A project can pay you more than once, so you cannot simply discount all its receipts as though they arrived together. Discounted cash flow (DCF) values a stream of dated cash flows by converting each payment to its present value and adding the results. Payments farther into the future get more periods of discounting at a positive rate.

Choose this general method when amounts or payment dates vary. Use the single-payment formula only for one future payment; combine payments occurring on the same date before discounting them.

PV0 = C1/(1+r) + C2/(1+r)2 + … + CT/(1+r)T = Σt=1T Ct/(1+r)t
NPV0 = C0 + Σt=1T Ct/(1+r)t

Ct is the signed cash flow in USD at the end of year t; T is the last payment year; r is the constant annual discount rate in decimal form (r > −1). Σ means add each term across the stated years. C0 happens today and is already in present-value terms. Assume a rate appropriate to the timing and risk of the forecast cash flows.

Lecturer example: rent the building, then sell it

  1. Given: initial cost $700,000; rent $30,000 at the end of each of the first two years; sale $840,000 at the end of year 2; required annual return 12%.
  2. Normalize and date: r = .12; C0 = −$700,000; C1 = $30,000; C2 = $30,000 + $840,000 = $870,000.
  3. Substitute: NPV = −$700,000 + $30,000/1.12 + $870,000/1.122.
  4. Calculate: 1.122 = 1.2544; PV1 = $30,000/1.12 = $26785.71428571…; PV2 = $870,000/1.2544 = $693558.67346939… .
  5. Result: PV of receipts = $720,344.39; NPV (using unrounded terms) = $20,344.39 ≈ $20,344.
  6. Interpret/check: NPV is positive, so the project creates value at 12%. Each discounted positive receipt is smaller than its future amount; multiplying each unrounded PV by its own discount denominator recovers the original receipt.

Rounding note: The slide displays $26,786 and $693,559 for the two PVs, then $20,344 for NPV. Adding those rounded whole-dollar PVs gives $20,345 after subtracting the initial cost. The slide's final $20,344 is correct from unrounded inputs; retain full precision until the end.

Choose shortcuts only when the payments fit

Bond and stock markets trade and value claims on safe and risky future cash flows. DCF estimates what an asset is worth relative to comparable investment opportunities. The general method works for dated cash flows under appropriate discounting, but equal recurring amounts allow shortcuts: a finite stream is an annuity and a stream continuing forever is a perpetuity. Growing streams also have formulas. See the next topics and ensure the rate matches the interest-payment frequency.

Quick check: Rent and sale proceeds both arrive at the end of year 2. How do you value them today?

Answer and why

Add them and discount the combined cash flow for two years. The date, not the type of income, determines the exponent.

Part III · Level cash flows

Use financial shortcuts for regular payments, loans and accumulated savings.

Value ordinary, due and deferred perpetuities

What lump sum would buy an income of $1 billion every year forever?

Find the value of a never-ending level payment

A perpetuity is a stream of equal payments that theoretically continues forever; an ordinary perpetuity begins one period from today. Imagine investing a lump sum and withdrawing only the interest each year. You can keep receiving that interest without reducing the principal. Choose this shortcut when payments are constant and never end, rather than an annuity with a fixed end date.

r = C / PV
PV0 = C1 / r

C is the level cash flow per year (USD/year), C1 is the first payment at year 1, PV0 is today's value in USD, and r is the constant annual decimal discount rate. Assume r > 0, equal payments each year, and no growth. Rearranging r × PV = C gives PV = C/r. The positive rate makes the infinite discounted sum finite.

Lecturer example: the Melinda and Bill Gates hypothetical

  1. Given: $1 billion every year forever; first payment in year 1; annual discount rate 10%.
  2. Normalize: C1 = $1bn/year; r = 0.10/year; annual payments.
  3. Substitute: PV0 = $1bn / 0.10.
  4. Compute: 1 / 0.10 = 10; $1bn × 10 = $10bn, matching slide 21.
  5. Interpret/check: $10bn × 0.10 = $1bn/year. At the assumed return the principal can fund the annual payment indefinitely.

Move the first payment to the correct date

A perpetuity due begins today instead of next year. Add the immediate payment, since it needs no discounting. A deferred perpetuity starts later: first value it exactly one period before its first payment, then discount that value back to today. If an ordinary perpetuity is delayed by d years, the first payment is at t = d + 1, not t = d.

Due: PV0 = C0 + C/r = (C/r)(1+r), when C0 = C.
Deferred: PV0 = (C/r)/(1+r)d, for first payment at t = d + 1 (d ≥ 0 whole years).

Lecturer follow-up: postpone payments three years

  1. Given: same $1bn/year forever, r = .10; delay d = 3 years relative to the ordinary stream. First payment year 4.
  2. Substitute: PV3 = $1bn/.10 = $10bn; PV0 = $10bn/(1.10)3.
  3. Compute: 1.103 = 1.331; reciprocal ≈ 0.75131480; PV = $7.51314801bn.
  4. Result: $7.51bn (slide: $7.51bn). Relative change = (7.513148… − 10)/10 × 100 = −24.87% (slide: −25%).
  5. Check: $7.513148…bn × 1.331 = $10bn at t = 3; waiting lowers today's value.

Timing clarification: The slide says the investment does not start making money for three years. Its printed discount factor implies year 4 is the first payment. If the first receipt were actually at year 3, discount the year-2 perpetuity value twice instead: $8.2645bn.

Answer and why

Value a level annuity today

Is a $365 million lottery jackpot actually worth $365 million today?

Turn a never-ending stream into a finite one

An annuity pays a fixed amount each year for a specified number of years. Use its present-value shortcut for equal year-end payments with a known final date. Unlike a perpetuity, payments stop; unlike general DCF, every payment has the same size. An ordinary annuity starts one period from today.

The lecturer derives the formula by subtracting two perpetuities. Start with the value of a stream that pays forever. Remove another stream that starts just after the final desired payment. Everything after year t cancels, leaving the first t payments.

PV0 = C[1/r − 1/(r(1+r)t)] = (C/r)[1 − 1/(1+r)t]
AF(r,t) = [1 − (1+r)−t]/r

The annuity factor AF is the total present value of one dollar paid each period for t periods. C is the payment amount in USD each year, r is the constant annual decimal discount rate, and t is the number of annual year-end payments. The derivation uses r > 0; the finite-sum formula also works for −1 < r < 0, and at r = 0 its limit is t, so PV = Ct. Match payment periods to the rate.

Lecturer example: Tiburon Autos

  1. Given: C = $5,000 each year-end for five years; annual rate 7%; find the car's cost in today's dollars.
  2. Normalize: r = .07/year; t = 5 annual payments, first at year 1.
  3. Substitute: PV = $5,000 × (1/.07 − 1/[.07 × 1.075]) = ($5,000/.07)[1 − 1/1.075].
  4. Compute: 1.075 = 1.4025517307…; AF = (1 − 0.712986179…)/.07 ≈ 4.100197436.
  5. Result: $5,000 × unrounded AF = $20,500.99 ≈ $20,501 (slide).
  6. Interpret/check: today's cost is less than the five undiscounted payments totaling $25,000. The separate annual PVs sum to the same amount.

Apply the same shortcut to a large prize

The lottery advertises the sum of future installments, not their value today. A winner cannot spend the entire advertised amount immediately. At a positive discount rate, receiving the same money across thirty years is worth less than receiving it today. The calculation values that specific installment stream, not a separately quoted cash-option payout.

Lecturer example: $365 million jackpot

  1. Given: 30 annual year-end installments of $12.167m, advertised $365m; r = 6%.
  2. Normalize: C = $12.167m per year; r = .06/year; t = 30.
  3. Substitute: PV = 12.167 × [1/.06 − 1/(.06 × 1.0630)] million USD.
  4. Compute: 1.0630 ≈ 5.74349117; AF = [1 − 0.174110131…]/.06 ≈ 13.764831151.
  5. Result: 12.167 × unrounded AF ≈ $167.476701m = $167,476,700.62 (about $167.5m, matching slide).
  6. Check: direct discounting of the 30 installments gives the same value, substantially below their nominal sum.

Rounding note: 30 × $12.167m = $365.01m because the stated installment is rounded; the slide's $167,500,000 is the rounded PV, not the exact cent result.

Quick check: Five equal year-end payments and then nothing. Which method directly values them today?

Answer and why

Ordinary annuity PV: the payments are equal, finite, and begin next year.

Remember

  • Finite equal year-end payments: ordinary annuity PV.
  • AF = [1 − (1+r)−t]/r.
  • Subtract two perpetuities to remove the infinite tail.
  • Watch out: nominal total is not present value; check the first payment date.

Calculate loan payments and amortization

Why does a fixed loan payment repay more of the debt each year, even though you never increase the payment?

Find the fixed annual installment

A bank lends you a lump sum today in exchange for a series of equal repayments. The present value of those installments must equal the loan principal. Use the ordinary annuity formula from the preceding topic, but solve for the payment instead of PV. Dividing the principal by the number of years alone would ignore interest.

PV = A × AF(r,t), where AF(r,t) = [1 − 1/(1+r)t]/r
A = PV / AF(r,t)

PV is the loan amount in USD at time zero; A is the fixed payment in USD each year; r is the annual decimal interest rate; t is the number of annual year-end payments. Divide both sides of the first equation by AF to isolate A. Assume a fixed rate, fully repaid principal, no fees or additional borrowing, r > 0; for r = 0 use A = PV/t.

Lecturer example: a house mortgage

  1. Given: borrow $250,000 from a local savings bank; 12% annual interest; thirty equal annual installments.
  2. Normalize: PV = $250,000; r = .12/year; t = 30, end-of-year payments.
  3. Substitute: AF = 1/.12 − 1/[.12(1+.12)30].
  4. Calculate: 1.1230 ≈ 29.95992212; AF = (1 − 0.033377924…)/.12 ≈ 8.055183968.
  5. Result: A = $250,000 / unrounded AF = $31,035.91 per year.
  6. Interpret/check: discounted value of all thirty payments = $250,000. The first year's interest alone is $30,000, so the payment must exceed that to reduce the balance.

Fact check: The lecturer prints AF = 8.055 and $250,000/8.055 = $31,037. Using that rounded factor gives $31,036.62, which rounds to $31,037. The full-precision factor instead gives $31,035.91. Use full precision until the final display.

Split every payment into interest and principal

An amortizing loan pays down the outstanding principal over time. The interest component depends on the opening balance. What remains of the fixed payment reduces principal. As the balance shrinks, the interest portion falls and the principal portion grows; the final balance reaches zero.

Interest in year k = Bk−1 × r
Principal repaid = A − interest
Bk = Bk−1 − principal repaid

Bk−1 is the opening balance and Bk the closing balance for year k, both USD; the opening balance for the first year equals the amount borrowed. The three relationships are the arithmetic behind the slide's table.

Lecturer example: $1,000 electric scooter

  1. Given: PV = $1,000; four-year loan; annual interest 10%; equal annual year-end payments.
  2. Normalize: r = .10/year, t = 4; B0 = $1,000.
  3. Substitute: AF = [1 − 1/1.104]/.10 = [1 − 1/1.4641]/.10 ≈ 3.169865446.
  4. Payment: A = $1,000 / unrounded AF = $315.47080371… ≈ $315.47 per year (slide).
  5. Year 1: interest = $1,000 × .10 = $100.00; principal repaid = $315.4708037… − $100 = $215.47; ending balance = $1,000 − unrounded principal repaid = $784.53.
  6. Repeat: year 2 interest = $784.529196… × .10 = $78.45; principal = A − interest = $237.02; closing balance = $547.51. Apply the same steps in years 3 and 4 as shown below.
  7. Check: total principal repaid = $1,000.00; closing balance = $0.00. Total interest = 4 × unrounded A − $1,000 = $261.88.

Quick check: During the fixed-payment scooter loan, what happens to the interest portion each year as the balance declines?

Answer and why

Interest falls. The same total payment therefore repays more principal.

Related: Annuity factor and PV.

Remember

  • Loan payment = principal divided by the annuity factor.
  • Interest = opening balance × period interest rate.
  • Principal repaid = payment − interest; this reduces the balance.
  • Watch out: the full payment is not the principal repayment, and early rounding can distort the result.
