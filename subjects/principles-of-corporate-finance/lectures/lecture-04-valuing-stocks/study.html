Read an equity index quote and its history

The S&P 500 fell 0.40% in a day but gained 13.81% in a year. Contradiction?

Start with the index overview

Distinguish primary and secondary stock markets

You buy a company's share. Did your money go to the company or to another shareholder?

Follow the share, not the word “secondary”

Common stock represents an ownership share in a publicly held company. On the primary market, a corporation sells new shares to investors, including through an IPO (initial public offering). The company receives the proceeds of those new shares before costs. On the secondary market, investors buy and sell previously issued shares; the selling shareholder receives the money instead. This distinction explains why share trading can occur without the company raising any fresh capital.

Quick check: Who receives the purchase money when investors buy newly issued company shares?

Answer and why (also works without JavaScript)

The company issuing the new shares. Primary-market issuance creates and sells new shares; before fees, the proceeds flow to the issuer.

Don't confuse an offering with a market

Slide 13 calls a later issue of new shares a “secondary offering” but places that transaction in the primary market. Here, “secondary offering” describes a later new-share sale; “secondary market” describes trading shares that already exist. Slide 14 then shows the Magnificent Seven: Meta, NVIDIA, Microsoft, Amazon, Apple, Tesla and Alphabet. Their logos illustrate prominent shares, not how a particular purchase was executed or whether any company received new funding.

Quick check: What does the Magnificent Seven logo slide establish?

Answer and why (also works without JavaScript)

It identifies seven equity-company names, without specifying transaction types or returns. It names Meta, NVIDIA, Microsoft, Amazon, Apple, Tesla and Alphabet. It does not classify a specific trade.

Worked example: one company, two different transactions

ILLUSTRATIVE numbers to make the cash direction visible. No numerical share-sale example appears on slide 13.

  1. A company issues 200 new shares at €12 each. This is a primary-market transaction.
  2. Investors pay 200 × €12 = €2,400 to the company, gross of any issuance costs.
  3. Later, an investor sells one of those existing shares to another investor for €15. This is a secondary-market transaction.
  4. The first investor receives the €15. The company receives €0 directly from that resale, although its traded share price may change.

Your turn: spot the market

Quick check: ILLUSTRATIVE: Two years after its IPO, a firm creates 200 additional shares and sells them to new investors. Which market is this?

Answer and why (also works without JavaScript)

Primary market, because these 200 shares are newly issued. Correct. New share creation and sale is primary-market activity, even when a later share issue is called a secondary offering.

Remember

  • Primary: new shares sold by a company; proceeds go to the issuer.
  • Secondary: already-issued shares sold by an investor; proceeds go to the seller.
  • A later new-share offering can still be primary-market activity.
  • Watch out: “secondary offering” and “secondary market” are not interchangeable in this lecture.

Would you pay $174.98 for a share earning $3.10 a year? First, decode the quote.

Read the price before the ratios

Slide 15 shows a historical Yahoo Finance snapshot for NVIDIA (NVDA) on 22 August 2025, 11:22 CET, not today's market price. Trading volume is 135,452,544 shares, versus a three-month average of 179,996,637. These measures describe different periods, not fair value.

Quick check: NVIDIA's P/E is 56.45. What does that figure compare?

Answer and worked reasoning (visible without JavaScript)

Share price compared with trailing EPS. Divide $174.98 by $3.10 per share: 56.445… ≈ 56.45. It is a unitless multiple, not 56.45%.

Translate P/E, yield and beta without mixing units

EPS means earnings per share. TTM means the trailing 12 months, not the calendar year. P/E is price divided by EPS, showing how many dollars of price correspond to one dollar of past annualized earnings. Dividend yield relates a quoted per-share dividend to the share price. Beta measures sensitivity to broad-market movements; the slide labels 2.14 “Volatility”, but beta is not the stock's total volatility. None of these numbers alone proves the stock is cheap.

Worked example: verify the lecturer's P/E

  1. Read the share price = $174.98 and EPS (TTM) = $3.10/share.
  2. Use P/E = price per share ÷ earnings per share.
  3. Calculate $174.98 ÷ $3.10 = 56.445… ≈ 56.45, exactly matching the displayed ratio after rounding.
  4. Interpret 56.45 times as a valuation multiple, not a 56.45% return or yield.

Check the inset's financial-statement units

The red-bordered earnings table is labelled “All numbers in thousands”. Its TTM column and the 1/31/2025 annual column cover different periods. For TTM, gross profit is 148,515,000 minus 44,395,000 = 104,120,000 (in the table's thousands units). Operating profit is 104,120,000 minus 17,938,000 = 86,182,000. These numbers belong to company financial statements, not the $174.98 share quote.

Compare ETFs with buying individual shares

Want exposure to dozens of businesses without placing dozens of stock orders?

One trade can hold a whole portfolio

Buying an individual common share gives exposure to one company. An exchange-traded fund (ETF) instead holds a portfolio of securities and has its own shares that investors can buy or sell in a single exchange trade. You buy the fund share, not a separate share of every company in the basket. The lesson highlights passive investing: following an index rather than selecting securities one by one. It names BlackRock, Vanguard and State Street as ETF providers.

Quick check: What do you actually buy when placing one trade in an ETF that tracks a stock index?

Answer and why (available without JavaScript)

A share of the ETF. The ETF holds a portfolio of securities; your fund share gives exposure to that portfolio in one trade. It does not mean individually purchasing all constituent shares.

SPDRs and closed-end funds are not synonyms

The slide calls SPDRs (Standard & Poor's Depository Receipts, “spiders”) ETFs that track S&P indexes. It also mentions closed-end mutual funds, another way to invest in a portfolio of securities, describing them as predominantly actively managed. That contrast is about typical strategy, not an absolute rule: the slide emphasizes passive ETFs but does not establish that every ETF must be passive. A fund's name alone does not tell you exactly which securities it holds.

Worked example: choose the instrument

  2. Buying a single individual stock would give exposure only to that company's business.
  3. Look for a fund designed to track the chosen index. Slide 16 names SPDRs as examples of S&P-tracking ETFs.
  4. One ETF trade can give access to the fund's basket; check the chosen fund's actual index rather than assuming every SPDR tracks the same one.

Distinguish accounting book value from market value

A business has €40m of accounting equity. Would buyers necessarily pay €40m for it?

Book value comes from the accounting balance sheet

Book value is the firm's net worth recorded in its financial statements: assets minus liabilities, using accounting amounts. The lecturer notes that these amounts depend on reporting standards, including IFRS and US GAAP. For a firm, the shorthand is book equity = recorded assets − recorded liabilities. This gives an accounting measure, not a quotation from buyers. Slide 17 also links book and liquidation value; that particular link needs a correction below.

Quick check: The balance sheet shows €40m in book equity. What does that prove about the price investors would pay?

Answer and explanation (available without JavaScript)

Accounting net worth does not establish market price. Market valuations may differ, and selling assets in liquidation would not necessarily realize their recorded book amounts.

Market-value-based accounting asks a different question

A market-value-based balance sheet, as the lecturer defines it, assigns market values to assets and liabilities. Its net value can differ from book equity because the measurements differ. Nor should you automatically treat the resulting net asset figure as the price of all shares traded on a stock exchange. Keep separate the recorded asset values, an estimate of their market values, and actual sale proceeds if assets are liquidated.

Don't confuse net worth with other stock indicators

A dividend is a periodic cash distribution from the company to shareholders. The P/E ratio is share price divided by earnings per share (EPS); the price and EPS must refer to the same share basis. Both are listed alongside book and market value on slide 17. Dividend payments and P/E tell you different things from the net-assets calculation. See the NVIDIA quote lesson for a numerical P/E example.

Worked example: two values from the same company

ILLUSTRATIVE figures, because slide 17 contains no numerical balance sheet.

  1. Accounting assets = €100m; accounting liabilities = €60m.
  2. Book equity = €100m − €60m = €40m.
  3. Estimated market values of assets = €130m; liabilities = €70m.
  4. Market-value-based net assets = €130m − €70m = €60m.
  5. Difference = €60m − €40m = €20m. This does not by itself establish the firm's traded share-market value or liquidation proceeds.

Enter a number in €m (for example, 85).

Hint and full solution (available without JavaScript)

Market-valued net assets = market-valued assets − market-valued liabilities = €180m − €95m = €85m. The result is a market-value-basis balance-sheet figure, not automatically a share-market quotation.

Fact check, slide 17: The lecturer prints “Book Value (-> Liquidation Value)”. That is not a general equality. Book equity uses accounting carrying amounts; actual liquidation proceeds depend on realizable sale prices and settled liabilities. Preserve the source label, but do not assume the amounts match.

Remember

  • Book equity = recorded assets − recorded liabilities. Accounting standards affect the recorded amounts.
  • Market-value-based net assets use market valuations, not just book figures.
  • Dividends are shareholder cash payments; P/E = share price ÷ EPS.
  • Watch out: book value is not guaranteed liquidation value or the quoted market value of shares.

Choose between comparable and intrinsic valuation

Is a company worth what its peers trade for, or what its future cash flows are worth?

Route 1: price it using comparables

Slide 18 first names multiples (comparables) valuation, also labelled pricing. The idea is to use how markets price similar businesses as a reference. For example, you might compare P/E ratios: a share's market price relative to its earnings per share, introduced on slide 17. This route begins with other traded companies, not your own long-term cash-flow forecast. The next comparables lesson develops this route.

Route 2: value the cash flows themselves

Intrinsic valuation asks what expected future payments are worth today. Slide 18 names exactly two subroutes: 2.a Dividend Discount Method, using future distributions to shareholders, and 2.b Enterprise Discounted Cash Flow, using cash flows of the business. Both rely on discounting future amounts back to present value. The slide introduces the methods, but gives no calculation, forecast, rate or numerical result yet. Their detailed treatment appears in the dividend valuation and enterprise DCF lessons.

Quick check: Which of the following is the lecturer's intrinsic valuation route?

Answer and reasoning (available without JavaScript)

Discounting future dividends or enterprise cash flows. Those are the two methods listed under intrinsic valuation. Market multiples belong to the other route.

  2. You forecast a share's future dividends. Choose route 2.a: Dividend Discount Method.
  3. You forecast the cash flows available to the whole business. Choose route 2.b: Enterprise Discounted Cash Flow.
  4. Check the conclusion: each route starts with different evidence. You cannot compute a defensible numerical answer from the slide's labels alone.

Compare a share with its valuation peers

Apache trades at 7.9 times earnings, while its peers average 20.8. Is that automatically a bargain?

First find a sensible peer group

Slides 19–20 call this comparables (trading-multiples) valuation: compare a target business with companies in a similar industry. P/E is share price divided by earnings per share (EPS). P/B is share price divided by book value per share. Both are ratios, not dollars. A peer group's average provides a pricing reference, not a guaranteed intrinsic value. Compare one target with its own peers

Calculate shareholder return from dividends and capital gains

If you pay $100, receive $5, and can sell a year later for $110, how much did you earn?

Your return comes from two places

A shareholder may earn a dividend (cash paid by the company) and a capital gain (a rise in the share's selling price). The slide calls their combined percentage the expected return, total shareholder return, or market capitalization rate. If the expected resale price falls instead, that part is a capital loss. Add the two per-share cash amounts and compare them with the initial purchase price.

r = (Div₁ + P₁ − P₀) / P₀

P₀ = share price now; P₁ = expected share price in one year; Div₁ = expected dividend paid within that year; r = expected one-year return as a decimal. Multiply by 100 to express it as a percentage.

Quick check: If the expected resale price falls but the dividend stays unchanged, what happens to total expected return?

Answer and reasoning (available without JavaScript)

Expected return falls. In (Div₁ + P₁ − P₀)/P₀, the dividend and initial price are fixed, while lowering P₁ reduces the expected capital gain.

The price today reflects future dividends

Slide 21 introduces the Dividend Discount Method: the equity approach values a share by discounting all expected dividends back to today. For a future dividend Divₜ at year t, divide by (1+r)ᵗ. Here r is investors' required expected return, their opportunity cost or cost of equity. Dividends farther away count less in today's value when the discount rate is positive. The infinite sum is the principle; later topics show how to calculate with it.

P₀ = ∑t=1∞ Divₜ / (1+r)ᵗ

Worked lecturer example: Fledgling Electronics

  1. Buy today at P₀ = $100 per share.
  2. Expect a one-year resale price P₁ = $110 and an end-year dividend Div₁ = $5.
  3. Dividend return = $5 / $100 = 5%. Capital-gain return = ($110 − $100) / $100 = 10%.
  4. Total expected return: r = (5 + 110 − 100)/100 = 15/100 = 0.15 = 15%.
  5. Interpretation: the forecast is 15% over one year, assuming both payments occur as expected.

Enter a percentage such as 5, not the fraction 0.05.

Hint and complete worked solution (works without JavaScript)

Dividend return = $12/$120 = 10%. Capital gain (loss) = ($114−$120)/$120 = −5%. Total expected return = 10% − 5% = 5%. Equivalently, (12+114−120)/120×100 = 5%.

Fact check, slide 21: The slide says “future (Free) Cash Flows (to the Firm)” and also “firm value” but displays an equity dividend-per-share discount formula. That formula estimates value per share; enterprise free cash flow uses a different cash-flow definition and discount rate. The original language is preserved in the source walkthrough below.

Remember

  • Expected return = dividend yield + capital-gain yield over the same period.
  • Fledgling's source figures give 5% + 10% = 15%.
  • Dividend discounting values a share from its future expected dividends and cost of equity.
  • Watch out: do not add dollars to percentages, forget the initial-price denominator, or mix equity dividends with enterprise FCF.
