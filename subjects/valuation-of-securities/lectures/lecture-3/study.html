I. Price a bond

Start with the promised cash flows, then discount them with the right rate and payment frequency.

Identify bond cash flows and the investor’s claim

Would you rather lend money to a government or buy a piece of it? A bond is the lending deal.

What are you actually buying?

A bond is a debt security: the issuer borrows money from investors and promises payments on specified dates. The bondholder is a creditor, not an owner of the issuer. A government can issue bonds to raise funds, just as a company can borrow to finance its activities.

A bond and a loan both create an obligation to repay borrowed money, generally with interest. A conventional bank loan is usually negotiated with a lender, whereas a bond is typically issued as a standardized security that can be traded between investors. Selling a bond normally changes who receives its future payments, not the issuer’s promised payment schedule. This is the teaching explanation of the breakout questions in slide 3, not a recorded lecturer answer.

Quick check: What does purchasing an ordinary bond make you?

Answer and why

A creditor. The issuer owes the promised debt payments; a bond does not normally confer ownership.

Follow the cash flows

For the fixed-payment, non-defaulting bond used in this lecture, a coupon payment is the periodic interest paid to the investor; the face value (principal) is the amount promised back at maturity, the final repayment date. The coupon rate determines interest relative to face value, not relative to the market purchase price.

Price annual coupons and principal

The French bond promises €125 in total. Why is its value today only €112.11?

Discount every promised payment

Present value (PV) is what a future payment is worth today at the required return. A bond’s value is the sum of those discounted payments. If the question supplies a discount rate and future cash flows, solve for price; if it supplies price and asks for the rate, use the same equation in reverse (next topic).

PV = C1/(1+r) + C2/(1+r)2 + … + (FV+CN)/(1+r)N

PV = Σt=1N [Ct/(1+r)t] + FV/(1+r)N

Ct = coupon in currency at period t; FV = face value in currency; r = required yield per period (decimal); N = number of periods; t = payment period. For fixed annual coupons, C = coupon rate × FV. Assume payments at period ends, a common discount rate r > −1 and repayment as promised.

Worked example A · French government bond
  1. GIVEN: valuation at the beginning of 2024–2029 five-year interval as in the slide’s Excel dates (1 Jan 2024 to 1 Jan 2029); payment years 2025–2029, FV = €100, annual coupon €5, annual required rate 2.4%.
  2. Normalize: r = 0.024, N = 5, C = €5. Final payment €5 + €100 = €105.
  3. Substitute: 5/1.024 + 5/1.0242 + 5/1.0243 + 5/1.0244 + 105/1.0245.
  4. Discounted components (€): 4.882813 + 4.768372 + 4.656613 + 4.547474 + 93.258734.
  5. Sum before rounding: €112.11400453 ≈ €112.11, matching the lecture.
  6. Sanity check: above €100 face value because the 5% coupon rate exceeds 2.4%; below €125 total promised receipts because the discount rate is positive.

Quick check: Which amount belongs in the numerator for year five?

Answer and why

€105. The last coupon and principal arrive together; €100 omits the coupon and €125 counts earlier coupons twice.

Use the shortcut when coupons are equal

An annuity is a series of equal payments at regular intervals. For a fixed coupon, price the annuity and principal separately; when coupons vary, use the individual cash-flow method above.

PV(bond) = PV(coupon annuity) + PV(final principal)

PV = C[1/r − 1/(r(1+r)N)] + FV/(1+r)N

C is the constant coupon; the bracket is the N-period annuity factor. Use r ≠ 0; teaching bridge: at r = 0 the value is simply N × C + FV.

  1. Coupon PV = 5[1/0.024 − 1/(0.024 × 1.0245)] = €23.29616256.
  2. Principal PV = 100/1.0245 = €88.81784197.
  3. Total = €112.11400453 ≈ €112.11. Slide 5 displays €23.30 + €88.82; these individually rounded components add to €112.12, so only round the total at the end.

Excel (lecture): =PRICE(DATE(2024,1,1),DATE(2029,1,1),0.05,0.024,100,1)

Settlement, maturity, annual coupon rate, annual yield, redemption per 100 face and annual payment frequency. PRICE quotes per €100 face; here that equals the bond value in euros.

Quick check: A bond has different coupon amounts each year. Which method fits?

Answer and why

Use the general sum. The annuity shortcut assumes a constant coupon.

Find the yield to maturity from a price

You pay €112.11 for a bond with a €5 annual coupon. Are you actually earning 5%?

Reverse the bond pricing formula

Yield to maturity (YTM) is the single discount rate that makes the present value of all promised future payments equal to the current bond price. The lecture’s French bond pays regular interest but repays only €100 at maturity, less than the purchase price. Yield blends coupon income with that eventual loss relative to the amount paid.

Recognize the problem by what is missing: a given price and known future payments mean solve for yield; a given yield means solve for price (annual pricing). Do not simply divide the coupon by face value. That tells you the coupon rate, not the return on the purchase.

112.11 = 5/(1+y) + 5/(1+y)2 + 5/(1+y)3 + 5/(1+y)4 + 105/(1+y)5

y is the annual YTM in decimal form, with annual end-of-period cash flows and y > −1. All cash flows are in euros. Assume coupons and principal are paid as promised. The same y discounts every payment.

Worked example · solve rather than price
  1. GIVEN: price €112.11, face value €100, coupon €5 annually, N = 5; final receipt €105. Unknown: y.
  2. Normalize the time periods to years; use the displayed equation, with a trial decimal rate in every denominator.
  3. Teaching bridge: try 2% (y=0.02): the five discounted payments sum to €114.140379, which is above the price. Try 3% (0.03): the sum is €109.159414, below the price. The yield is between these rates.
  4. Try 2.4%: 5/1.024 + 5/1.0242 + 5/1.0243 + 5/1.0244 + 105/1.0245 = €112.114005, which rounds to €112.11. Solving exactly against the rounded price gives y ≈ 0.024007996.
  5. Answer: YTM ≈ 2.40% per year. The tiny difference from 2.4% reflects rounding of the quoted price. Verify by substituting the rate back into the price equation.
  6. Sanity check: 2.40% is below the 5% coupon rate, consistent with paying more than face value and receiving €100 principal at maturity.

For these positive promised cash flows, the implied price falls continuously as the rate rises, so searching between trial rates works. A spreadsheet or financial calculator can do the search rather than testing dozens of rates by hand. YTM is a promised cash-flow return measure, not a guarantee of the return you actually achieve.

Quick check: The trial rate produces a price above the given market price. What should you do next?

Answer and why

Increase the trial yield. Higher discount denominators lower the present value of the same promised payments.

Apply annual bond pricing to IBM and Japan

Why would anyone pay $1,161.84 for a bond that repays just $1,000?

Case B: IBM's corporate bond

Separate the rate printed on the bond from the return investors require. The coupon rate determines the promised payment; the required yield determines how much that payment is worth today. The IBM case uses the Wall Street Journal AAA yield of 7.5% as the required return for an AAA-rated bond. These are historical case inputs, not current market quotes.

Worked example B · IBM
  1. GIVEN: FV = $1,000; annual coupon rate 11.5%, paid every September 30 for five years; additional $1,000 principal at maturity; annual required YTM 7.5%.
  2. Normalize: C = 0.115 × 1,000 = $115/year; r = 0.075; N = 5. Assume five full annual periods and promised payments occur.
  3. Use the cash-flow PV formula: 115/1.075 + 115/1.0752 + 115/1.0753 + 115/1.0754 + 1115/1.0755.
  4. Discounted amounts ($): 106.976744 + 99.513250 + 92.570465 + 86.112061 + 776.662875 = 1161.835396.
  5. Result: $1,161.84, matching slide 11. Sanity check: 11.5% > 7.5%, so the value is above face value, but below $1,575 undiscounted receipts.

Price bonds with semiannual payments

Three years does not always mean three cash flows. What happens when the bond pays you twice a year?

Match the payment and discount periods

Semiannual means once every six months. A bond paying twice per year divides its annual coupon into two equal instalments. Count half-year periods rather than calendar years and use the required return for each half-year. The annual bond pricing formula itself does not change; only the units of its inputs change.

C = FV × annual coupon rate / 2; N = 2 × years

PV = Σk=1N C/(1+r)k + FV/(1+r)N

C is the half-year coupon in USD, FV is principal in USD, k counts half-years, N is the total number of six-month periods, and r is the required return per half-year in decimal form (r > −1). If r is already semiannual, do not divide it again. For a nominal annual yield quoted with semiannual compounding, divide that quote by two; an effective annual yield instead requires r = √(1+annual effective yield) − 1 (teaching bridge).

Worked example D · US Treasury note
  1. GIVEN: February 2022 purchase, three years, $1,000 face value, 11.25% annual coupon, semiannual payments, required semiannual return 0.085%. Assume six full periods and payment as promised.
  2. Normalize: C = $1,000 × 0.1125/2 = $56.25; N = 3 × 2 = 6; r = 0.085/100 = 0.00085. Final payment = $1,056.25.
  3. Substitute: PV = 56.25/1.00085 + 56.25/1.000852 + 56.25/1.000853 + 56.25/1.000854 + 56.25/1.000855 + 1056.25/1.000856.
  4. Discounted amounts ($): 56.202228 + 56.154497 + 56.106806 + 56.059156 + 56.011546 + 1050.879115 = 1331.413347 (unrounded sum).
  5. Result: $1,331.41. Check: positive discounting puts it below $1,337.50 in total undiscounted cash flows; the half-year coupon rate 5.625% is higher than 0.085%, so it is above $1,000 face value.

Reprice when the required return increases

The Treasury still pays the same coupon and principal. Investors now demand a higher return, so they pay less for that fixed schedule. Change only the discount rate in the valuation formula, not the coupon.

Common questions

Read price–yield curves and identify premium or discount

Two bonds pay the same coupon rate today. Why does a change in interest rates hurt one investor much more than the other?

Read the axes before interpreting the curve

Slide 16 shows a Treasury yield curve, which compares yields at different maturities at a particular observation time. Its horizontal labels are 1M, 3M, 6M, 2Y, 5Y, 10Y and 30Y; the vertical axis shows yield in percent, with labelled ticks from 4.0% to 6.0% in half-percentage-point increments. The historical latest, week-ago and month-ago lines generally slope upwards. Calculate the weighted average receipt time

Duration (Macaulay duration) is the average time at which promised cash flows arrive, weighted by their present-value shares. Unlike maturity, it accounts for intermediate coupons. Use this formula when asked for weighted timing, not when asked directly for a percentage price change. Think of each payment as a weight on a timeline: a large payment far in the future pulls the average to the right.

D = Σt=1T [t × PV(Ct)] / PV = 1×PV(C1)/PV + 2×PV(C2)/PV + … + T×PV(CT)/PV

t = receipt time in years; T = final year; Ct = total payment ($), including redemption at T; PV(Ct) = Ct/(1+y)t; PV = sum of discounted payments ($); y = annual yield as decimal. The weights are fractions of price and must sum to 1. Assume positive promised annual payments and y > −1. D is measured in years.

Worked example · 9% bond at 4% yield
  1. GIVEN: $90 annually for seven years, $1,000 redemption, final payment $1,090; y = 0.04.

  5. Sum using full precision: D = 5.688417 years ≈ 5.69 years. Check: weights sum to 1 and D lies between the first and last receipt dates, 1 and 7 years.

Estimate price changes using modified duration

Rates rise by half a percentage point. Can we estimate the loss without discounting all seven payments again?

Turn timing into price sensitivity

Recall Macaulay and modified duration. The lecturer calls the latter “volatility.” It estimates the local relative price response to a small yield change. When asked for a quick approximate price change, use modified duration; when asked for the exact new price, discount the cash flows at the new yield. They are not interchangeable.

Dmod = D/(1+y); ΔP/P ≈ −Dmod × Δy; ΔP ≈ −P × Dmod × Δy.

D is duration in years; y is annual yield in decimal form (y > −1); P is the original price in dollars; Δy is the signed rate change in decimals. Dmod measures sensitivity to the annual rate. Multiply the relative change by 100 for percent, or by P for dollars. Assume fixed promised annual cash flows and a small yield movement; the minus sign captures the inverse relationship noted in slide 19.

Worked example · 9% seven-year bond, slide 23

  4. Yield falls to 3.5%: Δy = −0.005. Estimated change = +2.7348%; price ≈ $1335.66.

Read the term structure and distinguish interest rates

Use spot rates to price bonds and infer YTM

If a one-year payment and a two-year payment face different interest rates, why would you discount both at the same rate?

Spot rates first, yield after price

Each promised payment can be valued using today's rate for its own maturity. The law of one price says identical claims must have the same price in a well-functioning market. A coupon bond is a package of dated payments: if you can buy those same claims separately, their values should add up to the same price. Assume comparable risk and identical payment timing. Prices adjust to the term structure, and then YTM is inferred from price, not imposed as the same rate on every maturity.

PV1=1/(1+r1); PV2=1/(1+r2)2; P=Σt=1T Ct/(1+rt)t

rt is the annual effective spot rate for t years (decimal, > −1); Ct is the full promised payment in currency units, including principal at maturity; P is today's price. The discount factor dt = 1/(1+rt)t multiplies its corresponding payment. YTM y instead solves P=Σt=1TCt/(1+y)t using one common rate. Use spot discounting if the maturity-specific rates are supplied; solve YTM only after price is known.

Explain forward rates and the yield curve theories

Why accept 5% for a year when you could lock in 7% for two years?

Three reasons investors still buy short-term bonds

The expectations hypothesis says the term structure reflects expected future short-term rates. If investors expect those rates to rise, a short bond today can be attractive because they expect to reinvest at a higher rate later. Under unbiased expectations, with no additional risk premium, rolling over short bonds and holding a long bond offer the same expected return over the same horizon (slide 35).

Risk aversion complicates this: long bonds have greater exposure to changing rates. If uncertainty is mainly about future real rates, investors prefer maturities matching their investment horizons; this can twist the curve either way. The review slide calls an associated idea liquidity-preference theory: investors may require a liquidity premium for holding longer securities instead of more liquid short-term ones (brief teaching bridge for slide 51 terminology, not a claim that the curve must rise). Under the inflation-premium theory, uncertainty about future inflation makes investors demand additional compensation for long-term bonds.

Quick check: Can the shape of the curve be explained by risk preferences alone?

Answer and why

The lecture explicitly allows horizon matching to twist the term structure either way; inflation risk can also influence long yields.

Calculate the implied return during year two

When given one-year and two-year spot rates and asked for the break-even second-year rate, compare wealth over the same two-year horizon. Do not subtract 5% from 7%: that compares rates over different maturities. Divide the two-year growth factor by the first-year growth factor to isolate year two.

(1+r2)2=(1+r1)(1+f1,2); f1,2=(1+r2)2/(1+r1)−1

r1, r2 are effective annual spot rates, f1,2 is the implied annual return from end of year 1 to end of year 2; all in decimals and greater than −1. Assume comparable safe dated claims, annual compounding and no transaction costs. Expectations theory additionally interprets this implied rate as the expected future one-year rate, not a guaranteed realized rollover return.

Worked example · comparing investment strategies
  1. GIVEN: initial $100, r1=5%=0.05, r2=7%=0.07, one- and two-year horizons.
  2. Strategy 1: $100 × 1.05 = $105 at year 1.
  3. Strategy 2: $100 × 1.072 = $100 × 1.1449 = $114.49 at year 2.
  4. To break even, $105 × (1+f1,2) = $114.49. Divide by $105 and subtract 1: f1,2 = 1.072/1.05−1 = 0.090380952 = 9.0381%. Slide rounds to 9%.
  5. Check: reinvest $105 at the exact implied rate for one year, recovering $114.49. The required return for year two is higher than the two-year annual rate, as expected for an upward curve.

The lecturer asks whether the extra 9% return makes investing for two years worthwhile. That depends on the investor's expectation for the second-year rate, the need for funds after year one and the risk accepted. Under the unbiased expectations model the break-even rate is the expected future rate; with premiums they need not coincide.

Quick check: If you expect the one-year rate next year to exceed the implied 9.0381%, which strategy offers higher expected terminal wealth, ignoring risk?

Answer and why

The $105 first-year proceeds would grow beyond $114.49 at a second-year rate above break-even.

Hint and worked answer

Two-year wealth = $100 × 1.06² = $112.36; first-year wealth = $105. Divide: 112.36/105−1 = 0.070095238 ≈ 7.01%. Reverse check: 105 × (1+f) = 112.36.

Square the two-year growth factor, divide by the one-year growth factor, subtract one and convert to percent.

Remember

  • Implied forward rates isolate the return over a future interval.
  • Expectations, maturity preferences and inflation risk can explain curve shapes.
  • Watch out: a forward rate is not necessarily the future realized spot rate; compare investments over equal horizons.

IV. Separate money returns from purchasing power

Read inflation history and convert between real and nominal amounts before interpreting interest rates.

Recognize inflation and deflation in historical data

If prices fall instead of rising, what happens to the purchasing power of the money your bond pays?

Read positive and negative inflation

Inflation is an increase in the general price level over time; deflation is a decrease. Positive inflation means the same fixed cash payment buys less of a typical basket; deflation means it buys more, other things equal. A drop in a positive inflation rate is not the same as deflation: prices can still be increasing, just more slowly.

Slide 36 shows annual US inflation from the early twentieth century. The series has especially high positive bars near the end of the 1910s (around 20%) and negative bars in the early 1920s and early 1930s (around −10%). The lecture highlights the severe deflation in the Great Depression. Later there are further positive spikes. The zero line separates rising from falling prices.

Worked teaching bridge · ILLUSTRATIVE basket priced at $100
  1. GIVEN: initial general-price basket cost $100; compare one-year inflation of +3% (the approximate US long-run headline in slide 37) with −10% (an approximate historical trough shown in slide 36). These are illustrative applications, not actual basket observations.
  2. Convert rates to decimals: π=0.03 or −0.10. New price = initial price × (1+π), assuming the stated inflation rate applies to the basket over one year.
  3. At +3%: $100 × 1.03 = $103. At −10%: $100 × 0.90 = $90.
  4. Check the signs: a positive inflation rate raises the basket cost; a negative rate lowers it. A fixed $100 payment buys less than the basket in the first scenario, more in the second.

Quick check: Inflation falls from +8% to +3%. Does that necessarily mean the price level has fallen during the year at 3%?

Answer and why

Inflation is still positive. The rate of increase is lower, not negative.

Compare inflation across countries

Slide 37 emphasizes that long-run inflation differs significantly around the world. Its headline gives approximately 3% average US inflation for 1900–2020, while the chart is labelled 1900–2011. The bars suggest Switzerland near 2.4%, the US near 3.1% and Italy near 10.8%. These figures are historical visual estimates, not current inflation figures or a precise data series. The German bar excludes 1922/23; take that exclusion seriously when comparing the averages.

For a bond investor, cash payments stated in money do not automatically tell how much can be bought with them. Over long horizons, even moderate inflation matters because price increases accumulate. To compare purchasing power consistently, use the real versus nominal conversion in the next topic.

Quick check: Can you assume the chart proves Germany's average includes the extraordinary inflation of 1922/23?

Answer and why

Germany is labelled “ex 1922/23.” Comparisons must preserve the stated exclusions and historical periods.

Convert nominal cash flows and rates into real terms

Your bond pays 5%, but prices rise 3%. Did your purchasing power really grow by 5%?

Convert money into purchasing power

A nominal cash flow is measured in the money units of its payment date; a real cash flow measures purchasing power in base-date money. Inflation increases the price of goods, so divide a future money amount by the accumulated price increase to express it in today's purchasing power. This is not discounting for the time value of money: it only removes inflation.

CFtreal = CFtnominal/(1+π)t

CF = cash payment (currency units), t = elapsed years, π = constant annual inflation as a decimal, π > −1. Use this for purchasing power at the base date; for different annual inflation rates use the product of their inflation factors instead.

Worked example · ILLUSTRATIVE (slide formula, no original numerical example)
  1. GIVEN: €1,000 nominal receipt in three years, annual inflation 3%.
  2. Normalize: t=3, π=0.03. Price-level growth = 1.033 = 1.092727.
  3. Real cash flow = 1000/1.092727 = 915.141659 ≈ €915.14 in today's purchasing power.
  4. Check: positive inflation makes real value lower than €1,000; multiplying the real amount by the price-level factor recovers €1,000.

Quick check: With negative inflation (deflation), does a positive fixed future cash flow have greater or smaller base-date purchasing power than its nominal amount?

Answer and why

The compounded price-level factor is less than one when inflation is negative but greater than −100%; division increases the amount.

Convert quoted returns into real returns

A nominal interest rate measures growth in money; a real interest rate measures growth in purchasing power. Compare the money growth factor against price-level growth over the same period. Use the exact transition formula for precise calculations; subtraction is only a rule of thumb for small rates.

1+rreal = (1+rnominal)/(1+π); rreal = (1+rnominal)/(1+π) − 1

Rule of thumb: rreal ≈ rnominal − π

All rates are annual effective decimals for the same year, π > −1, rnominal > −1. Use expected inflation for an expected real return calculation (a forecast), actual inflation to measure purchasing-power growth already realized.

Worked example · ILLUSTRATIVE one-year savings
  1. GIVEN: €100, annual nominal rate 5%, inflation 3%; rnominal=0.05, π=0.03.
  2. Money after one year = €100×1.05 = €105. Deflate to base-year money: 105/1.03 = €101.941748.
  3. Growth relative to €100: (105/1.03)/100−1 = 1.05/1.03−1 = 0.019417476.
  4. Exact annual real return = 1.9417% ≈ 1.94%. Subtraction gives 5%−3%=2% (approximate).
  5. Check: nominal money grows 5%, but purchasing power grows less because the price level rises. If inflation exceeds the nominal return, real return turns negative.

Quick check: At 5% nominal return and 8% inflation, what happens to purchasing power?

Answer and why

Exact real rate = 1.05/1.08−1 = -2.7778%. Positive money growth does not guarantee positive purchasing-power growth.

Interpret the Fisher effect

Slide 39 calls this the “Fischer effect”: expected inflation changes nominal interest rates but does not change the required real rate, holding other determinants fixed. Investors require nominal compensation for the anticipated loss of purchasing power. This statement concerns the required rate in the model, not a guarantee of every observed market rate.

Fact check · slide 39: “same proportional change” is an approximation, not an exact equality of rate changes. Rearranging the exact formula gives Δrnominal=(1+rreal)Δπ at fixed real rate; for small real rates this is approximately one-for-one in percentage points. For example, if expected inflation rises from 3% to 4% with our real rate 1.9417% unchanged, the new nominal rate is (1+0.019417476)×1.04−1 ≈ 6.0194%, an increase of 1.0194 percentage points.

Quick check: In the Fisher-effect model, if expected inflation increases with the required real rate fixed, which rate adjusts upward?

Answer and why

The nominal rate compensates for expected inflation. The model holds the required real rate fixed.

Hint and worked answer

Divide the money growth factor by the inflation growth factor, subtract one and express it in percent.

Remember

  • Real cash flows measure purchasing power; compound the inflation factor for multi-year receipts.
  • Exact real rate = (1+nominal rate)/(1+inflation)−1.
  • Watch out: nominal minus inflation is only an approximation; distinguish expected from realized inflation.

Explain real rates through saving and investment

Why could borrowing rates fall sharply while the inflation-adjusted cost of borrowing changes far less?

What the UK bond chart tells us

The lecture's historical UK comparison shows that ten-year real interest rates were much more stable than ten-year nominal interest rates. Both are measured in percent. Nominal borrowing costs include compensation for inflation; real borrowing costs measure purchasing power. Recall the Fisher relationship: a difference between nominal and real rates can reflect inflation compensation, rather than a change in the underlying real cost of capital.

Quick check: Which series fluctuates more in the UK chart?

Answer and why

The blue nominal series varies more over the period than the lighter real series. This is a historical observation, not a universal law.

Find the real cost of capital in Fisher's classical model

In the classical theory attributed to Irving Fisher, the actual money borrowing rate is nominal; the theoretical inflation-adjusted borrowing rate is determined by the supply and demand for capital. Savers supply capital according to their willingness to save. A higher real reward encourages saving in this model. Investors demand capital for investment opportunities; as borrowing gets more expensive, fewer projects are worthwhile. The crossing determines the equilibrium real interest rate, where the amount supplied equals the amount demanded.

Worked teaching bridge · ILLUSTRATIVE decision

Suppose firms discover more profitable investment opportunities. Holding savers' supply schedule fixed, capital demand shifts right: at the old rate firms want more funding than savers offer. The real rate rises until saving and investment demand match again. This differs from a rise in expected inflation alone, which in the Fisher-effect model raises the nominal rate while the required real rate stays fixed.

Quick check: If more investment opportunities increase capital demand while supply is unchanged, what happens to equilibrium real r in the diagram?

Answer and why

The downward demand curve shifts right, crossing the upward supply curve at a higher real rate.

Your turn · ILLUSTRATIVE: Households become more willing to save at every real interest rate, but investment opportunities remain unchanged. Predict the direction of the equilibrium real interest rate and explain why.

Hint and worked answer

Identify the curve that moves, not a movement along it. Saving supply shifts right. At the old rate more capital is supplied than demanded; the rate falls to the new crossing.

Remember

  • The historical UK chart shows more stable real than nominal rates.
  • Equilibrium real r is determined by capital supply and demand in the classical model.
  • Watch out: a shift in investment opportunities is not the same as a change in expected inflation.

Compare Treasury bill returns with inflation

A government bill earns 12% in a year when prices surge 25%. Are you actually richer?

Read nominal bill returns against inflation

A Treasury bill is a short-term government debt instrument. The three historical charts compare its nominal return (orange) with inflation (blue) over 1953–2011. Focus on which line is higher at the same date: when the bill return exceeds inflation, purchasing power grows; when inflation exceeds it, purchasing power falls. The lines sometimes move together but are not identical.

In the UK, inflation raced ahead of bill returns in the mid-1970s. In the US, both rose around the late 1970s and early 1980s, with the bill return peaking later. Germany's inflation peak is lower on these charts, and its bill returns sometimes substantially exceed inflation. Compare observations within the same year and check each chart's scale before comparing countries.

Quick check: Which visual condition indicates a negative real bill return?

Answer and why

Inflation exceeds the nominal return, so the amount of goods the money can buy falls.

Convert the chart's gap into a real return

Use the exact Fisher conversion for purchasing-power returns, not the nominal rate alone. The vertical difference in the graph gives the subtraction approximation in percentage points. At high inflation, that approximation can differ substantially from the exact answer. Treasury bills can therefore have positive nominal returns while investors lose purchasing power.

V. Recognize credit and sovereign risk

Distinguish promised returns from payments that an issuer can actually deliver.

Interpret ratings, default risk and corporate bond yields

A corporate bond promises a 25.70% yield. Is that an incredible return or a warning sign?

Read the credit rating before the yield

A credit rating is an assessment of an issuer's or obligation's creditworthiness, including the likelihood of default (failure to meet promised obligations). Companies seek ratings to communicate credit quality and help investors judge financing risk. Strong ratings indicate lower assessed risk; weak ratings indicate higher risk, not certain default. The lecturer names Moody's, Standard & Poor's (S&P) and Fitch. Investors demand an interest-rate premium for bearing default risk, because promised coupon and principal payments are the best-case cash-payment scenario.

Investment grade is the higher-rated group through Baa/BBB; junk bonds are speculative-grade bonds below that boundary. The lecture uses broad categories without rating modifiers.

Quick check: In the lecture's scale, which S&P rating is the lowest broad investment-grade category?

Answer and why

BBB corresponds to Baa, the last investment-grade row. BB/Ba is speculative grade.

Compare quoted prices and promised returns

As rating quality decreases in slide 48, the quoted YTM increases. All sample bonds mature in 2017, but they have different coupons and prices. Promised yield is the YTM calculated on promised payments; realized return depends on actual receipts, reinvestment and sale or redemption proceeds. A low purchase price may imply a high promised yield because repayment is uncertain. Do not read the YTM as an assured return or the risk premium as a directly measured default probability.

Worked teaching bridge · compare the AAA and CCC quotes
  1. GIVEN: Johnson & Johnson AAA, coupon 5.15%, price 122.88% of face, YTM 1.27%; Caesars Entertainment CCC, coupon 5.75%, price 41.95% of face, YTM 25.70%. Both list 2017 maturity. For price conversion only, assume ILLUSTRATIVE $1,000 face value.
  2. Normalize price percentages: 122.88/100=1.2288 and 41.95/100=0.4195. Multiply by face to get $1228.80 and $419.50, respectively.
  3. Compare annual quoted yields by subtraction: 25.70% − 1.27% = 24.43 percentage points (2,443 basis points). This is a difference between the two quoted YTMs, not a relative percent increase or a pure estimate of default risk.
  4. Sanity check: the better-rated bond trades above par and the lower-rated bond far below par. Despite similar coupons, the lower price of the CCC bond is consistent with its high promised yield. To calculate an actual return, actual cash receipts and holding period would be necessary.

Quick check: Does the CCC bond guarantee a 25.70% annual return?

Answer and why

Default or a different realized cash-flow schedule can cause actual returns to differ from the promised YTM.

  • Ratings indicate assessed credit risk, not guaranteed outcomes.
  • Baa/BBB is the lowest broad investment-grade category in the lecture.
  • Watch out: promised YTM can be much higher than realized return; a rating does not determine the realized return.

Compare sovereign debt in domestic and foreign currencies

If a government can create its own currency, why can it still have a debt crisis?

Government debt is not automatically safe

Sovereign debt is debt issued by a national government. The lecture describes it as generally less risky than corporate debt, but emphasizes that sovereign defaults can have catastrophic consequences. In a crisis the government's ability to raise taxes or obtain funding may be insufficient to service debt. Greater perceived default risk generally lowers bond prices and raises their promised YTM, other things equal, as with corporate bonds.

Inflation is a different risk: a government can repay all promised money yet reduce the real value of its debt through inflationary policies. The bondholder receives the nominal amount but loses purchasing power. Recall the real cash-flow formula.

Worked teaching bridge · ILLUSTRATIVE

A government promises a fixed cash payment next year. It repays exactly as agreed, but the general price level rises. The bondholder has not experienced a missed payment; the same nominal cash now buys less. In contrast, failure to deliver the promised cash constitutes default.

Quick check: A sovereign repays its promised nominal amount, but inflation reduces purchasing power. What has happened?

Answer and why

Inflation can erode real value even when nominal contractual payments are made in full.

Identify which currency the government owes

Own-currency debt is generally less risky than foreign-currency debt for a sovereign with monetary control. As the lecture puts it, governments can print money to repay bonds. They cannot independently create another country's currency, so foreign-currency obligations require foreign exchange obtained through earnings, reserves, borrowing or other available funding. A crisis can make that funding difficult.

Fact check · slides 49–50: the statement that default occurs when a foreign government borrows dollars is too categorical: borrowing in foreign currency creates exposure, not inevitable default. Own-currency issuance also does not eliminate default risk; monetary financing can face institutional limits and cause inflation.

The Eurozone is the important exception to the simple own-currency argument. Member governments cannot print euros independently to repay domestic debts. The European Central Bank (ECB) controls euro-area monetary policy and the lecture describes it as controlling the money supply. Debt in domestic legal tender therefore need not imply national monetary autonomy.

Quick check: Can a Eurozone member government independently create euros to repay its bonds?

Answer and why

Monetary authority is shared at euro-area level; member governments do not individually control issuance.

Your turn · ILLUSTRATIVE: A government owes US dollars but collects taxes mainly in its own currency. Its domestic currency depreciates against the dollar. What happens to the domestic-currency cost of servicing the dollar debt, assuming the dollar payment is fixed?

Hint and worked answer

Think about how many units of domestic currency are needed to buy one dollar. Depreciation means more units are needed, so the domestic-currency burden rises. This can increase default risk, but does not mean default must occur.

Remember

  • Sovereign debt can default, and the consequences can be severe.
  • Currency denomination and monetary control affect repayment risk.
  • Watch out: nominal repayment does not ensure real purchasing power, and Eurozone members cannot independently create euros.
