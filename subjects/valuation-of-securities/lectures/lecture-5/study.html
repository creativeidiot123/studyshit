Part 1 · Why NPV comes first

Start with cash-flow timing, the discount rate and the value created by a project.

Apply the NPV rule

If you spend $1 million on a project, how much must it be worth to make shareholders richer?

Put cash flows on the same date

A project may cost money today but produce cash over several years. Adding those amounts directly ignores when the money arrives. Net present value (NPV) is the value today of all the project's cash flows, including its initial investment. Discounting converts future money into today's money.

Choose NPV when the question asks how much value an investment creates in money terms, given its cash flows and a required return. Unlike the payback criterion, it evaluates value rather than just how soon the investment is recovered.

NPV = C0 + C1/(1+r) + C2/(1+r)2 + C3/(1+r)3 + … [L003]

Ct is the signed cash flow in dollars at period t; C0 is today's flow (usually negative). r is the discount rate per period, as a decimal. With a constant annual rate, assume year-end future cash flows and r > −1. Dividing by (1+r)t reverses t periods of compounding.

Invest only when the project adds value

The opportunity cost of capital is the return shareholders could earn on an alternative investment of comparable risk. The financial manager can either fund project X or return cash through dividends or share buybacks, allowing shareholders to invest in financial assets themselves. A project should beat that alternative. Accept if NPV > 0, reject if NPV < 0; zero means financial indifference under the model. [L004] [L005]

Worked example: project X (source L004)

  1. Given: $1m cash, $9m other assets at t=0; project future cash flows worth PV million dollars today.
  2. Normalize: all amounts are already market values today in $ millions; no further discounting is needed for the balance-sheet comparison.
  3. Reject: 1 + 9 = $10m.
  4. Accept: 0 + 9 + PV = (9 + PV) million dollars.
  5. Subtract: (9 + PV) − 10 = PV − 1 million dollars = NPV.
  6. Break-even: PV − 1 = 0 ⇒ PV = $1m. Above this threshold the firm gains value; below it the firm loses value.

Teaching bridge (ILLUSTRATIVE): compute the PV rather than being given it

  1. Given: C0 = −$1m, C1 = +$1.21m at year-end, r = 10% per year.
  2. Convert r = 10/100 = 0.10; t = 1.
  3. NPV = −1 + 1.21/(1+0.10), in $m.
  4. PV = 1.21/1.10 = $1.10m; NPV = −1 + 1.10 = +$0.10m ($100,000).
  5. Check: the one-year payoff exceeds the break-even payoff 1 × 1.10 = $1.10m, so NPV must be positive. Accept X.

Quick check: If X has a present value of $0.9m and costs $1m, what should the manager do?

Answer

Reject: NPV = −$0.1m.

Your turn (ILLUSTRATIVE): Keep X's $1m cost and $1.21m year-end payoff, but raise the annual discount rate to 25%. Is value still created?

Hint and worked answer

Discount only the year-end receipt and subtract today's outlay. NPV = −1 + 1.21/1.25 = −1 + 0.968 = −0.03200 million dollars. Reject.

Lecture roadmap: the remaining topics compare NPV with book rate of return, payback and IRR, then handle limited capital and the profitability index. [L002]

Remember

  • NPV measures how many dollars of value a project adds today.
  • The discount rate reflects the foregone alternative return.
  • Accept positive NPV; reject negative NPV.
  • Watch out: do not discount C0 or confuse gross PV with net value.

Benchmark investment criteria against NPV

If two investment methods disagree, which one should decide where the money goes?

Prerequisite: Apply the NPV rule.

Popularity is not proof of value

Slide 6 shows NPV and IRR as the two most widely applied investment criteria. Payback is also common, while the accounting measure and profitability index appear less often. The slide does not identify the survey date or sample, so treat these percentages as the lecture's evidence, not as a current universal statistic. [L006]

Read the source chart

  1. Given: IRR 76%, NPV 75%.
  2. Both figures use the same percentage scale; subtract directly: 76 − 75 = 1 percentage point.
  3. IRR is slightly more common in the chart. It is not 1% more profitable; the chart measures usage, not profitability.

Three tests for every investment criterion

Time value of money means a dollar today is worth more than a dollar later under a positive discount rate. A good decision method must respect the timing of receipts and payments. Second, NPV depends on forecast cash flows and the opportunity cost of capital, not accounting income or an arbitrary recovery deadline. Those are also the two inputs to the equation in the first topic.

Third, value additivity means values expressed in today's dollars can be added. Use this rule when asked for the combined value of projects with additive cash flows, valued on the same date and with appropriate discount rates, rather than averaging their return percentages. [L007]

NPV(A + B) = NPV(A) + NPV(B)

ILLUSTRATIVE teaching bridge: combine projects

  1. Given: A has NPV +$120,000, B has NPV −$40,000. Independent projects; both values at t=0 in dollars.
  2. Normalize: no further discounting or currency conversion needed.
  3. Substitute: NPV(A + B) = 120,000 + (−40,000) = +$80,000.
  4. Check: adding a negative-value project lowers A's value by $40,000. If B is optional, A alone is better.

Quick check: A criterion ignores the timing of cash flows. Which NPV principle does it violate?

Answer

Time value of money: future and immediate cash cannot simply receive equal weights.

Your turn (ILLUSTRATIVE): Suppose B instead creates $30,000 of value and A is unchanged. What is their combined NPV?

Hint and worked answer

Both amounts are already present values. $120,000 + $30,000 = $150,000. Both projects create value.

Remember

  • NPV respects cash-flow timing.
  • Forecast cash flows and opportunity cost drive NPV.
  • Combine present values measured on the same date.
  • Watch out: method popularity does not establish correctness.

Part 2 · Alternative investment criteria

Compare accounting and cash-recovery measures before calculating the return implied by discounted cash flows.

Calculate and critique the book rate of return

Can an investment look more profitable just because the accountant changes how it is depreciated?

Prerequisite: Benchmark investment criteria against NPV.

Measure accounting profitability

The book rate of return (accounting rate of return) divides average accounting income by the average book value of the investment over its life. It measures profit relative to the recorded assets, rather than the economic value created by the cash flows. Use it when the problem gives book income and book assets or asks for an accounting return. Do not substitute cash receipts for income, as you would use cash flows in calculating NPV.

Book rate of return = book income / book assets = average annual book income / average book value [L008]

The numerator is annual accounting profit in currency per year; the denominator is the average recorded asset value in the same currency. The denominator must be nonzero. Multiply the ratio by 100 to express the annual accounting rate in percent. The slides do not prescribe a detailed averaging or depreciation convention; use the one specified in the question.

ILLUSTRATIVE: two-year machine investment

  1. Given: cost $100,000 at t=0, two-year life, zero residual value, straight-line depreciation, operating cash inflows $60,000 at each year-end, no other costs or taxes.
  2. Normalize: all values in $; annual depreciation = (100,000 − 0)/2 = $50,000 per year.
  3. Book income per year = 60,000 − 50,000 = $10,000; average annual income = (10,000 + 10,000)/2 = $10,000.
  4. Average book value using each year's opening and closing values = [(100,000 + 50,000)/2 + (50,000 + 0)/2]/2 = (75,000 + 25,000)/2 = $50,000.
  5. Substitute: book rate of return = 10,000/50,000 = 0.20 = 20% per year.
  6. Check: annual book income equals one fifth of average book assets. This is not a 20% discounted cash-flow return.

Why this measure can mislead

As slide 30 emphasizes, the company chooses which expenditures to capitalize as assets and how fast to depreciate them. The resulting income and book value depend on those choices and tax/accounting figures. They are not market values or cash flows. The measure is nondynamic: averaging gives no special weight to profit earned earlier. It ignores the time value of money, so a higher book return need not mean higher NPV. For shareholder value decisions, return to the NPV rule. [L030]

Quick check: Depreciation policy changes reported profit but leaves actual project cash flows and the discount rate unchanged. Which measure can change?

Answer

Book rate of return can change; NPV stays unchanged under the stated assumptions.

Your turn (ILLUSTRATIVE): The same machine instead produces $65,000 cash per year. The book-value schedule and all other assumptions stay unchanged.

Hint and worked answer

Annual income = 65,000 − 50,000 = $15,000. Average book value stays $50,000. 15,000/50,000 × 100 = 30%. The accounting return rises by 10 percentage points.

Remember

  • Divide average book income by average book value.
  • Use the question's accounting assumptions and averaging convention.
  • Book returns ignore time value and are not market-value returns.
  • Watch out: cash receipts are not accounting income.

Compare payback, discounted payback and NPV

Would you reject a project that takes three years to recover its cost but makes much more money?

Prerequisites: NPV rule; NPV principles.

How quickly does the investment recover its cost?

The payback period is the number of years before cumulative cash flow recovers the initial outlay. Use ordinary payback when the question asks when undiscounted cash receipts cover the investment. Start with the negative initial cash flow, add each year's receipts, and find the first nonnegative cumulative total. The lecture uses whole years and year-end receipt timing, not fractional-year interpolation.

Payback = first year n with C0 + C1 + … + Cn ≥ 0.

Ct is signed cash flow in dollars at year t. For the conventional projects here, one initial outlay is followed by nonnegative cash flows. The payback rule accepts only projects recovering the investment by a chosen deadline. The choice of deadline is arbitrary. [L009]

  1. Given: three $2,000 investments, receipts at years 1–3 as shown, cutoff ≤ 2 years, annual cost of capital 10% = 0.10.
  2. A cumulative cash: −2,000 → −1,500 → −1,000 → +4,000. First recovery: year 3.

Discount the receipts before counting recovery

Discounted payback asks how long it takes to recover the initial investment in present-value terms. If the problem gives a cost of capital and asks for discounted recovery, first discount each future receipt. NPV instead sums every discounted cash flow through the end of the project, even after recovery.

Discounted CFt = Ct/(1+r)t; discounted payback = first n with Σt=0n Ct/(1+r)t ≥ 0.

r is the constant annual discount rate as a decimal (r > −1); t is the number of years. If the running balance never reaches zero, there is no discounted payback within the project life. For conventional flows, this is the first year with nonnegative cumulative NPV.

Worked example: discount the same three projects (L012)

  1. Keep r = 0.10. Denominators for years 1, 2 and 3 are 1.10, 1.21 and 1.331; t=0 cash is unchanged.
  2. A: −2,000 + 500/1.10 + 500/1.21 + 5,000/1.331 = −2,000 + 454.545… + 413.223… + 3,756.574… = $2,624.34. Cumulative PV at year 2 = −$1,132.23; at year 3 = +$2,624.34. Recovery: year 3.
  3. B: −2,000 + 500/1.10 + 1,800/1.21 = −2,000 + 454.545… + 1,487.603… = −$57.85. Year 1 = −$1,545.45, year 2 and 3 = −$57.85. Never recovers.
  4. C: −2,000 + 1,800/1.10 + 500/1.21 = −2,000 + 1,636.364… + 413.223… = $49.59. Year 1 = −$363.64, year 2 and 3 = +$49.59. Recovery: year 2.
  5. Check: at a positive rate, discounted receipts never exceed nominal receipts, so discounted recovery cannot happen earlier than ordinary recovery in these projects. The rounded NPVs agree with the lecture.

Why the payback cutoff fails

Ordinary payback ignores all cash flows after the cutoff and treats earlier receipts equally without discounting. B and C have identical ordinary payback, but C receives more money sooner, so it has higher NPV. Discounted payback fixes the timing problem, yet a two-year cutoff still rejects A, the strongest value creator. For independent projects with unconstrained funds, NPV accepts A and C and rejects B; if only one can be chosen, A has the largest NPV. [L030]

Quick check: Which project is wrongly accepted under ordinary payback ≤ 2 years if the objective is value creation?

Answer

B, because its NPV is negative.

Your turn (ILLUSTRATIVE variant): Keep project B's original cash flows but lower the annual cost of capital to 5%. Calculate its NPV.

Hint and worked answer

NPV = −2,000 + 500/1.05 + 1,800/1.05² = −2,000 + 476.1905 + 1632.6531 = $108.84. Positive NPV; discounted recovery now occurs in year 2.

Related summary: slide 30 (L030).

Remember

  • Ordinary payback accumulates nominal cash flows.
  • Discounted payback accumulates present values.
  • NPV measures full project value, not recovery speed.
  • Watch out: an arbitrary cutoff can destroy shareholder value.

Calculate IRR and use its decision rule

What is the highest required return a project can face before it stops creating value?

Prerequisite: Apply the NPV rule.

Find a project's break-even rate

The internal rate of return (IRR) is the discount rate that makes a project's NPV exactly zero. Instead of asking how many dollars a project creates at a given cost of capital, IRR asks which rate would make the project break even in present-value terms. Use the single-payoff shortcut only when one receipt follows the initial investment by one period. For several receipts, solve the full discounted cash-flow equation.

Lecturer derivation: single payoff (L013)

  1. Rate of return = payoff / investment − 1.
  2. Set NPV = C0 + C1/(1+discount rate) = 0.
  3. Move C0 to the other side: C1/(1+discount rate) = −C0.
  4. Multiply by the denominator and divide by −C0: 1+discount rate = C1/(−C0).
  5. Therefore discount rate = C1/(−C0) − 1. This is the same one-period return as step 1.

0 = C0 + C1/(1+IRR) + C2/(1+IRR)2 + C3/(1+IRR)3 + … + CT/(1+IRR)T [L014]

Each Ct is a signed cash flow in dollars at time t, T is the final period and IRR is a decimal rate per period greater than −1. For this lecture example, annual cash flows arrive at year-end. Solving for IRR is similar to solving a bond's yield to maturity: find the unknown rate, not a new cash flow.

Calculate the IRR of Tool A

Source example (L015), with algebra as a teaching bridge

  1. Given: C0 = −$4,000, C1 = $2,000 and C2 = $4,000. Periods are years; find the annual rate.
  2. Substitute the source values: 0 = −4,000 + 2,000/(1+IRR)1 + 4,000/(1+IRR)2.
  3. Let x = 1+IRR (x > 0). Multiply through by x² to remove fractions: 0 = −4,000x² + 2,000x + 4,000.
  4. Divide by −4,000: x² − 0.5x − 1 = 0. Solve: x = (0.5 ± √(0.25+4))/2. The negative root is outside the domain.
  5. x = 1.280776406…; IRR = x − 1 = 0.280776406… = 28.08% annually (rounded to two decimals, matching the slide).
  6. Check: using the unrounded rate, 2,000/x ≈ $1561.55 and 4,000/x² ≈ $2438.45. Together they equal $4,000, giving NPV ≈ $0.
  7. Excel: put −4000, 2000, 4000 in A1:C1 in time order and enter =IRR(A1:C1).

Use the IRR rule, but know its boundary

For a conventional investment (an initial outlay followed by positive receipts), NPV falls as the discount rate rises. Accept when the opportunity cost of capital r is below IRR; reject when it is above. Equality means NPV = 0. This discounted-cash-flow criterion agrees with the NPV rule for this independent project; the next four topics show where IRR can mislead. [L016] [L017] [L030]

Quick check: Tool A's required return is 20% annually. What is the decision?

Answer

Accept. NPV = −4,000 + 2,000/1.20 + 4,000/1.20² = $444.44.

Your turn (ILLUSTRATIVE variant): Tool A now pays only $3,000 in year 2. All other cash flows are unchanged. Find its IRR.

Hint and worked answer

0 = −4,000+2,000/x+3,000/x². Multiply by x² and divide by −4,000: x²−0.5x−0.75=0. Positive root x=(0.5+√3.25)/2=1.15138782. IRR=(x−1)×100=15.14%. The smaller receipt reduces IRR.

Related summary: slide 30 (L030).

Remember

  • IRR is the discount rate at which NPV = 0.
  • One-period returns have a shortcut; multiple flows need the full equation.
  • Accept conventional independent investments when IRR exceeds r.
  • Watch out: IRR is a rate; NPV is money. They answer different questions.

Part 3 · Four traps in the IRR rule

Test the IRR decision against cash-flow signs, project scale, timing and term-specific capital costs.

Distinguish lending from borrowing

A 50% return sounds fantastic. Would you still want it if you were the one paying it?

Prerequisite: Calculate IRR and use its decision rule.

Check the direction of the cash flows first

Before comparing IRR with the opportunity cost of capital, identify who gives money to whom. In lending, you pay money now and receive repayments later; a higher return is desirable. In borrowing, you receive money now and repay it later; a lower financing rate is desirable. The same IRR can therefore represent either an attractive return or an expensive loan.

For a two-cash-flow stream with opposite signs, use the one-period zero-NPV equation from the previous topic. It works for both lending and borrowing, whereas the ordinary investment decision rule assumes an initial outflow.

NPV = C0 + C1/(1+r);   IRR = C1/(−C0) − 1.

C0 and C1 are signed dollar amounts today and one year later. r and IRR are annual decimal rates greater than −1, with C0 nonzero. The sign of the initial cash flow determines which direction the profile moves.

Worked source example (L018)

  1. Given: A pays $1,000 at t=0 and receives $1,500 at t=1. B receives $1,000 at t=0 and pays $1,500 at t=1. Annual opportunity cost = 10% = 0.10.
  2. IRR for A = 1,500/1,000 − 1 = 0.50 = 50%.
  3. IRR for B = (−1,500)/(−1,000) − 1 = 0.50 = 50%.
  4. Discount the future receipt/payment at 1+0.10 = 1.10: 1,500/1.10 = $1,363.636…
  5. NPV(A) = −1,000 + 1,363.636… = +$363.64 (slide: +364).
  6. NPV(B) = 1,000 − 1,363.636… = −$363.64 (slide: −364).
  7. Check: at 50%, 1,500/1.50 = $1,000. Both NPVs are zero; at any given rate the opposite streams must have equal and opposite NPVs.

Reverse the IRR decision for borrowing

A is worth accepting at 10%, because lending earns more than the alternative. B should be rejected because borrowing at 50% costs more than the 10% benchmark. Increasing r reduces the present value of A's future receipt, but it also reduces the burden of B's future repayment. Consequently A's NPV falls and B's rises. For these conventional one-period streams the lending rule is IRR > r, while the borrowing rule is IRR < r. When the two rates match, NPV is zero. [L018] [L030]

Answer

B is borrowing at a rate above the benchmark; its NPV is negative.

Your turn (ILLUSTRATIVE variant): Keep B's cash flows, but raise the comparable annual borrowing benchmark to 60%. What is B's NPV?

Hint and worked answer

NPV = 1,000 − 1,500/1.60 = 1,000 − 937.50 = +$62.50. Borrowing at 50% is now cheaper than the alternative at 60%.

Related summary: slide 30 (L030).

Remember

  • Check whether the initial flow is a payment or receipt.
  • Lending favors a higher IRR; borrowing favors a lower IRR.
  • NPV gives the same value-based decision rule in either case.
  • Watch out: do not blindly accept every stream with IRR above r.

Detect multiple or missing IRRs

What if one project has two different returns, and another has no return rate at all?

Prerequisites: Calculate IRR; lending versus borrowing.

Multiple zero crossings

Nonconventional cash flows can switch between payments and receipts more than once. A delayed large payment becomes less burdensome in present-value terms as the discount rate increases, so NPV need not fall continuously. When cash flows change sign repeatedly, find the NPV at the actual cost of capital rather than blindly comparing one IRR with that rate.

NPV(r) = −30 + Σt=19 10/(1+r)t − 65/(1+r)10 = 0 at an IRR

Amounts here are in billions of Australian dollars, t is the year, and r is the annual decimal discount rate (r > −1). The ten-year cash-flow equation, not the one-period shortcut, determines the roots.

Worked source example (L019–L020)

  1. Given: C₀ = −30; C₁ through C₉ = +10 each; C₁₀ = −65 (A$bn). Annual end-of-year flows.
  2. At 0%: −30 + 9 × 10 − 65 = −5 A$bn.
  3. At 10% (r = 0.10): PV of nine receipts = 10 × [1 − (1.10)−9]/0.10 = 57.5902; PV of terminal payment = −65/1.1010 = -25.0603.
  4. Sum: NPV(10%) = 2.5299 A$bn. At 35%, NPV = -6.5797 A$bn. There is a zero crossing on either side of 10%.
  5. Numerically solve NPV(r)=0 by narrowing the sign-changing brackets [0%,10%] and [10%,35%]. Roots: 3.503024% and 19.542150%; slide displays 3.50% and 19.54%.
  6. Substitution at full precision gives NPV ≈ 0 for each root. At 10%, lying between them, NPV is positive; outside those roots the example rejects.

Computed reconstruction of slide 20: NPV rises, peaks near 10%, then falls. Both intercepts are IRRs.

Fact check: Slide 20 says multiple sign changes produce multiple IRRs. More precisely, they can produce multiple IRRs, one repeated root, or no IRR; slide 31 and the next example recognize this qualification. Do not count sign changes as a guaranteed number of distinct roots.

A project with no IRR can still create value

An IRR might not exist at all when the NPV profile never reaches zero. NPV remains meaningful and supplies a decision at the relevant discount rate.

Worked source example: project C (L021)

  1. Given: C₀ = +$1,000, C₁ = −$3,000, C₂ = +$2,500; r = 10% = 0.10 per year.
  2. NPV = 1,000 − 3,000/1.10 + 2,500/1.10².
  3. Compute: 1,000 − 2727.2727 + 2066.1157 = +$338.84 (source: +339).
  4. To test for IRR, let x = 1+r > 0. Multiply the zero-NPV equation by x²: 1,000x² − 3,000x + 2,500 = 0.
  5. Divide by 500: 2x² − 6x + 5 = 0. Its discriminant is (−6)² − 4×2×5 = −4. A negative discriminant means no real roots, hence no IRR.
  6. Check: positive NPV at 10% is consistent with no zero crossing. Accept based on NPV.

Quick check: With a required return of 10%, should the A$-billion project be accepted?

Answer

Accept: NPV at 10% = 2.5299 A$bn.

Your turn: Project C now has a 20% cost of capital. Calculate its NPV, even though no IRR exists.

Hint and worked answer

1,000 − 3,000/1.20 + 2,500/1.20² = 1,000 − 2,500 + 1736.1111 = $236.11. The project still creates value.

Related summary: slide 31 (L031).

Remember

  • Multiple sign changes warn that the IRR rule can fail.
  • Check all relevant zero crossings or use NPV directly.
  • A project without an IRR can have positive NPV.
  • Watch out: do not automatically reject when one IRR is below r.
