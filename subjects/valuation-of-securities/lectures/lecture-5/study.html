Part 1 · Why NPV comes first

Start with cash-flow timing, the discount rate and the value created by a project.

Apply the NPV rule

If you spend $1 million on a project, how much must it be worth to make shareholders richer?

Put cash flows on the same date

A project may cost money today but produce cash over several years. Adding those amounts directly ignores when the money arrives. Net present value (NPV) is the value today of all the project's cash flows, including its initial investment. Discounting converts future money into today's money.

Choose NPV when the question asks how much value an investment creates in money terms, given its cash flows and a required return. Unlike the payback criterion, it evaluates value rather than just how soon the investment is recovered.

NPV = C0 + C1/(1+r) + C2/(1+r)2 + C3/(1+r)3 + … [L003]

Ct is the signed cash flow in dollars at period t; C0 is today's flow (usually negative). r is the discount rate per period, as a decimal. With a constant annual rate, assume year-end future cash flows and r > −1. Dividing by (1+r)t reverses t periods of compounding.

Invest only when the project adds value

The opportunity cost of capital is the return shareholders could earn on an alternative investment of comparable risk. The financial manager can either fund project X or return cash through dividends or share buybacks, allowing shareholders to invest in financial assets themselves. A project should beat that alternative. Accept if NPV > 0, reject if NPV < 0; zero means financial indifference under the model. [L004] [L005]

PV denotes the present value of project X's future cash flows. The project replaces cash, not the other assets.

Worked example: project X (source L004)

  1. Given: $1m cash, $9m other assets at t=0; project future cash flows worth PV million dollars today.
  2. Normalize: all amounts are already market values today in $ millions; no further discounting is needed for the balance-sheet comparison.
  3. Reject: 1 + 9 = $10m.
  4. Accept: 0 + 9 + PV = (9 + PV) million dollars.
  5. Subtract: (9 + PV) − 10 = PV − 1 million dollars = NPV.
  6. Break-even: PV − 1 = 0 ⇒ PV = $1m. Above this threshold the firm gains value; below it the firm loses value.

Teaching bridge (ILLUSTRATIVE): compute the PV rather than being given it

  1. Given: C0 = −$1m, C1 = +$1.21m at year-end, r = 10% per year.
  2. Convert r = 10/100 = 0.10; t = 1.
  3. NPV = −1 + 1.21/(1+0.10), in $m.
  4. PV = 1.21/1.10 = $1.10m; NPV = −1 + 1.10 = +$0.10m ($100,000).
  5. Check: the one-year payoff exceeds the break-even payoff 1 × 1.10 = $1.10m, so NPV must be positive. Accept X.

Quick check: If X has a present value of $0.9m and costs $1m, what should the manager do?

Answer

Reject: NPV = −$0.1m.

Your turn (ILLUSTRATIVE): Keep X's $1m cost and $1.21m year-end payoff, but raise the annual discount rate to 25%. Is value still created?

Hint and worked answer

Discount only the year-end receipt and subtract today's outlay. NPV = −1 + 1.21/1.25 = −1 + 0.968 = −0.03200 million dollars. Reject.

Lecture roadmap: the remaining topics compare NPV with book rate of return, payback and IRR, then handle limited capital and the profitability index. [L002]

Remember

  • NPV measures how many dollars of value a project adds today.
  • The discount rate reflects the foregone alternative return.
  • Accept positive NPV; reject negative NPV.
  • Watch out: do not discount C0 or confuse gross PV with net value.
