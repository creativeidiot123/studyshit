1. What the bond lecture covers

This is Lecture 3, Valuation of Bonds, in Prof. Dr. Thorsten Feix’s Valuing Securities and Companies course. The stated theme is applying the net present value (NPV) idea to debt securities.

The lecturer’s explicit objectives are to calculate bond values, connect prices to interest rates and yields, explain duration and volatility, understand the term structure and its competing explanations, distinguish real from nominal rates using Fisher’s relation, and evaluate default risk and ratings.

Opening breakout (slide 3)
  1. What is a bond?
  2. In what ways is it similar to or different from a loan?
  3. What payments does the investor receive after buying a bond?

These are questions posed by the slides. No spoken student answers or lecturer discussion was supplied.

Visual reference: slide 1 shows Wall Street signage; slide 2 pairs objectives with a footprint image; slide 3 uses financial trading imagery beside the three breakout questions. These images are contextual, not numerical evidence.

2. French government bond: cash flows and present value

In the French government bond case, an investor receives recurring coupon interest and then recovers principal at maturity together with the last coupon. The diagram explicitly separates the coupon stream from the principal repayment.

French government bond cash flows as shown on slide 4
Payment year20252026202720282029
Investor receives€5€5€5€5€105

Here the face value is €100, the annual coupon is €5 and the maturity is five coupon periods. At the slide’s discount rate of 2.4%, the present-value expression is:

PV = 5/1.024 + 5/(1.024)² + 5/(1.024)³ + 5/(1.024)⁴ + 105/(1.024)⁵ ≈ €112.11.

Slide 5 rewrites the same calculation as two components: the present value of the coupon annuity (€23.30) plus the discounted €100 principal (€88.82). That is the central structural idea for ordinary fixed-coupon bonds.

PV = Coupon × [1/r − 1/(r(1+r)N)] + FV/(1+r)N
Original spreadsheet expression (slide 5): =PRICE(DATE(2024,1,1),DATE(2029,1,1),0.05,0.024,100,1). It is recorded here as source syntax, not verified as a fully specified current Excel workflow.

3. Yield to maturity: solving in reverse

Once a bond’s market price is known, slide 6 proposes reversing the present-value calculation to solve for yield to maturity (YTM), denoted y. Instead of inserting a required rate to find price, insert the observed price and find the single yield that makes the discounted payments add up.

€112.11 = 5/(1+y) + 5/(1+y)² + 5/(1+y)³ + 5/(1+y)⁴ + 105/(1+y)⁵

For this example, the underlying discount rate is about 2.4%. The source describes YTM as combining coupon receipts with the bond’s declining value over its remaining life, phrasing particular to this premium-price French example. Slide 6 highlights the separate discounted components using a series of arrows.

4. General bond valuation formulas

The following three slides state the general formula in expanded, summation and annuity-shortcut forms. They are different layouts of the same present-value logic.

PV = C₁/(1+r) + C₂/(1+r)² + … + (FV+CN)/(1+r)N
PV = Σt=1…N Ct/(1+r)t + FV/(1+r)N

Source notation: Ct is the coupon interest payment (given as coupon rate × face value), FV is face value, r is yield to maturity, and N is maturity. The summation spans coupon periods t=1 through N.

For level coupon payments, slide 9 gives the annuity shortcut:

PV(bond) = Coupon × [1/r − 1/(r(1+r)N)] + FV/(1+r)N

Visual reference: slides 7–9 place these formulas in large highlighted equation panels against a light-bulb motif. The panels do not introduce further empirical data.

5. Practice cases: IBM and Japanese government bond

Case B: IBM corporate bond

The exercise gives $1,000 face value, an 11.5% annual coupon ($115), annual September 30 payments over five years, and $1,000 principal repayment at maturity. It states an AAA rating and a 7.5% reference AAA YTM; those are historical classroom assumptions, not verified current issuer facts.

PV = 115/1.075 + 115/(1.075)² + 115/(1.075)³ + 115/(1.075)⁴ + 1,115/(1.075)⁵ = $1,161.84

Case C: Japan bond

Slide 12 asks about a ¥200 bond with an 8% annual coupon and 4.5% YTM, bought in July 2023 and maturing in 2028. Slide 13 then changes the dates to July 2025 and 2029, yet still shows five coupon periods, each ¥16, ending in ¥216.

Displayed setup: 16/1.045 + 16/(1.045)² + 16/(1.045)³ + 16/(1.045)⁴ + 216/(1.045)⁵
Source discrepancy, preserved for audit: slide 13 prints ¥243.57 and also contains “Yen231”. Evaluating the displayed five-period cash flows gives approximately ¥230.73, not ¥243.57. The 2025–2029 dates also do not naturally describe five full annual intervals. These contradictions are flagged; no lecture statement has been silently replaced.

Visual reference: IBM’s logo graphic is used on slide 10; a Japanese lantern illustration appears on slide 12. Neither supplies additional inputs to the calculations.

6. Semiannual payments: the U.S. government note

The U.S. note example is a three-year security with $1,000 face value and an 11.25% annual coupon paid semiannually. That makes six scheduled half-year coupon payments of $56.25 (= $1,000 × 11.25% / 2). The slide writes the demanded semiannual return as .085%.

The expression on slide 15 divides the first five payments by powers of 1.00085 and the final ($1,000 principal plus $56.25 coupon) payment by the sixth power:

PV = Σt=1…5 56.25/(1.00085)t + 1,056.25/(1.00085)⁶
Source discrepancy, preserved for audit: the slide prints both $1,331.40 and $1334. Direct calculation at 0.085% per half-year produces approximately $1,331.41. The wording, period rate, displayed formula and conflicting results are all retained.

The slide 14 headline says “US government securities as a USD 22 tr. market”; treat this strictly as a historical lecture assertion, not a contemporary market size estimate.

Visual reference: Federal Reserve / U.S. government imagery fills the background of slides 14–15; slide 15 presents the six discounted cash flows in a white formula panel.
