Topic 01 · slides 1–7

Explain how central-bank rates affect the economy

A central bank moves one interest rate. Why might a company postpone opening a factory?

1. Follow the money from policy rates to businesses

Central banks set the direction of monetary policy, using tools such as key interest rates to influence borrowing and spending. Commercial-bank interest rates generally follow their central bank's rates. Monetary-policy transmission is that chain of effects on the real economy, meaning production, investment and economic activity. Higher loan rates can reduce demand for credit, discourage investment and slow growth; lower rates can work in the opposite direction. The link is influential, not automatic.

ILLUSTRATIVE · Follow a credit decision
  1. 1. A central bank raises its policy interest rate.
  2. 2. A commercial bank increases the loan rate it offers a small manufacturer.
  3. 3. The manufacturer postpones borrowing to expand its production line.
  4. 4. Investment is lower than it otherwise might have been, weakening economic growth.

Without JavaScript, all steps stay visible.

Quick check: If a central bank cuts its policy rate, which result is most consistent with the lecture's transmission mechanism?

Answer without JavaScript

Commercial-bank loan rates tend to fall. This can encourage credit and investment, but there is no automatic guarantee.

2. Recognize three central-bank policy approaches

Not every central bank is trying to optimize the same thing. The lecture contrasts a multiple-objective approach (several economic aims), price stability or inflation targeting (steering inflation toward a target) and exchange-rate targeting (keeping a fixed exchange rate stable). The Fed is the example for multiple objectives; the ECB for inflation targeting. For a fixed-rate country, maintaining the exchange rate shapes its policy choices.

ILLUSTRATIVE · Identify the approach
  1. 1. A central bank explicitly pursues both low unemployment and low inflation: classify it as multiple objectives.
  2. 2. Another sets policy to reach a particular inflation target: classify it as inflation targeting.
  3. 3. A third focuses on holding its currency at a fixed exchange rate: classify it as exchange-rate targeting.
Quick check: Which approach explicitly prioritizes keeping a currency's exchange rate constant?

Answer without JavaScript

Exchange-rate targeting, the approach described for fixed-exchange-rate countries.

3. Know what the lecture will build toward

This lecture belongs to the macroeconomics strand of Financial Economics, alongside the programme's microeconomics and policies strands. The opening agenda asks how monetary policy differs under floating versus fixed exchange rates in the short and medium/long term, and compares floating, intermediate and fixed regimes; it poses questions rather than supplying the answers. The six sections progress from the ECB and traditional instruments to financial-crisis measures, the European sovereign-debt/euro crisis, quantitative easing (QE), Covid-era policy and the response to rising inflation since 2022.

Quick check: Which is explicitly listed as a learning goal in the introductory slides?

Answer without JavaScript

Understand quantitative easing (QE). The slides also ask for traditional monetary-policy tools and policy changes across crises and inflation.

Remember
  • Central-bank rates influence commercial-bank rates, credit, investment and growth.
  • The link is monetary-policy transmission; it changes incentives, not individual loan approvals.
  • The Fed, ECB and fixed-rate countries illustrate different policy-goal approaches.
  • Watch out: a fixed-exchange-rate objective is not the same as an inflation target.

Topic 02 · slides 8–10

Identify the ECB’s objectives and responsibilities

The euro-area economy slows down. May the ECB abandon price stability to stimulate growth?

1. Put the ECB’s primary objective first

The European Central Bank (ECB) conducts monetary policy for the euro area. Under the treaty provision quoted on slide 9 (Article 282(2) TEU), the European System of Central Banks (ESCB) has one primary objective: maintain price stability, meaning broadly stable prices over time. It must support the EU’s general economic policies only without prejudice to that priority. This hierarchy matters whenever economic goals pull in different directions.

Slide 9 describes the ECB’s inflation aim using the words “below, but close to, 2%”. Keep that exact lecture-era wording for source fidelity; the slide does not establish that it is the target currently in force.

ILLUSTRATIVE · Interpret the slide’s 2% wording
  1. 1. Suppose the inflation rate is 1.8% per year.
  2. 2. Compare it with 2.0%: 2.0% − 1.8% = 0.2 percentage points below 2%.
  3. 3. That describes a distance from the slide’s stated benchmark, not a mechanical rule for the ECB’s next decision.
Quick check: Which goal has priority under the ECB/ESCB mandate on slide 9?

Answer without JavaScript

Maintaining price stability. Other EU economic policies receive support only without prejudice to that objective.

2. Contrast the ECB’s mandate with the Fed’s

Slide 9 contrasts the ECB with the US Federal Reserve (Fed). The US statute cited there (U.S. Code §225a) directs the Board of Governors and the Federal Open Market Committee to keep long-run monetary and credit growth consistent with the economy’s production potential, promoting maximum employment, stable prices and moderate long-term interest rates. Compared with the ECB’s explicitly ranked mandate, this is the lecture’s example of several goals.

ILLUSTRATIVE · Classify a policy statement
  1. 1. A policy statement says price stability is the overriding statutory objective.
  2. 2. Classify it with the ECB’s stated hierarchy, not the Fed’s multiple-objective framing.
  3. 3. If a statement explicitly combines employment, stable prices and moderate long-term rates, identify the Fed quotation instead.
Quick check: Which objective appears explicitly in the lecture’s Fed statute quotation?

Answer without JavaScript

Maximum employment. Stable prices and moderate long-term interest rates are also named.

3. Identify the ECB’s actual jobs

A mandate describes why an institution acts; its functions describe what it does. Slide 10 goes beyond setting interest rates. The ECB defines and implements euro-area monetary policy, conducts foreign-exchange operations, holds and manages foreign reserves, and promotes smooth payment systems. It also has bank-regulation and supervisory responsibilities, and tasks relating to banknotes, statistics, macroprudential policy, financial stability, and international and European cooperation.

ILLUSTRATIVE · Match a task to the ECB
  1. 1. A question mentions oversight of banks: connect it to banking supervision and financial stability.
  2. 2. A question mentions cross-border currency transactions: connect it to foreign-exchange operations.
  3. 3. A question asks what keeps transfers functioning: connect it to the smooth operation of payment systems.
Quick check: Which task is part of the ECB’s responsibilities?

Answer without JavaScript

Promoting smooth operation of payment systems, one of the functions listed on slide 10.

Remember
  • Price stability is the ECB/ESCB’s primary treaty objective.
  • Other EU economic policies receive conditional support; slide 9 uses the historical “below, but close to, 2%” description.
  • Fed goals in the lecture also include maximum employment and moderate long-term interest rates.
  • Watch out: the ECB’s responsibilities extend well beyond interest-rate decisions, including reserves, payments and bank oversight.

Topic 03 · slides 11–16

Distinguish the Eurosystem and the ECB’s decision-making bodies

Who decides euro-area monetary policy, and who makes it happen?

1. Separate the Eurosystem from the ESCB

Think of two overlapping institutional names, not two competing central banks. The Eurosystem consists of the European Central Bank (ECB) and the national central banks (NCBs) of euro-area countries. The larger European System of Central Banks (ESCB) includes those institutions plus the NCBs of EU countries outside the monetary union. The slide 11 diagram also shows the General Council in the broader institutional structure. Membership of the ESCB does not mean a country uses the euro.

ILLUSTRATIVE · Sort an institution
  1. 1. Take the central bank of a euro-area member: it belongs to the Eurosystem.
  2. 2. The Eurosystem sits inside the ESCB, so that central bank also belongs to the ESCB.
  3. 3. Take an EU central bank from outside the euro area: it belongs to the ESCB, but not the Eurosystem.
Quick check: Which group includes EU central banks from outside the euro area?

Answer without JavaScript

ESCB. The Eurosystem is the narrower euro-area group.

2. Identify the decision-making body

The Governing Council is the ECB’s main monetary-policy decision maker. Slide 14 lists the six Executive Board members and governors of NCBs from the 21 euro-area member states. It meets twice a month to assess economic and monetary developments, takes monetary-policy decisions every six weeks, and publishes regular accounts of decision meetings before the next meeting. The group photograph on slide 13 shows the council, not an individual voting roster.

Worked example · Count members using the slide’s numbers
  1. 1. Executive Board: 6 members.
  2. 2. Euro-area NCB governors: 21 members.
  3. 3. Total: 6 + 21 = 27 Governing Council members. This is not a count of votes; voting rotation is explained in the next topic.
Quick check: Using slide 14, how many people make up the Governing Council?

Answer without JavaScript

27 members = 6 Executive Board members + 21 euro-area NCB governors. Do not confuse this with voting rights.

3. Explain what the Executive Board does

The Executive Board has six members: the ECB president, vice-president and four others. Slide 12 names Christine Lagarde as president; slide 15 displays the Board’s group photograph. The Board prepares Governing Council meetings, implements the policy decisions, gives instructions to euro-area NCBs and manages the ECB’s day-to-day business. Remember the division: the Council decides, the Board prepares and executes.

ILLUSTRATIVE · Follow a policy decision
  1. 1. The Executive Board prepares material for a Governing Council meeting.
  2. 2. The Governing Council takes the monetary-policy decision.
  3. 3. The Executive Board implements it, instructs euro-area NCBs as needed and runs daily ECB operations.
Quick check: Which body implements Governing Council decisions?

Answer without JavaScript

The Executive Board implements policy, instructs euro-area NCBs and manages day-to-day ECB activities.

Remember
  • Eurosystem = ECB + euro-area NCBs; ESCB also includes non-euro EU NCBs.
  • Governing Council decides monetary policy; Executive Board prepares and implements it.
  • Slide 14: 6 + 21 = 27 members; decisions every six weeks.
  • Watch out: 27 council members does not mean 27 votes; see voting rotation.

Topic 04 · slides 17–18

Work out who votes in ECB monetary-policy decisions

The Governing Council has 27 members in the lecture, so why are there only 21 voting rights?

1. Separate council membership from voting rights

Before 2015, the lecture says every ECB Governing Council member had one vote. Since 2015, rotating voting rights mean that some national central-bank governors temporarily lack a vote even while serving on the Council. The six Executive Board members always have a vote. The previous governance topic counts 27 Council members from slide 14; slides 17–18 instead count 21 voting rights. Never use these two numbers interchangeably.

Worked example · Count the ECB votes (slides 17–18)
  1. 1. Permanent Executive Board voting rights: 6.
  2. 2. Rotating rights for the five highest-ranked countries: 4.
  3. 3. Rotating rights for the remaining countries: 11.
  4. 4. Total: 6 + 4 + 11 = 21 voting rights, although 6 + 21 = 27 people are on the Council in slide 14.
Quick check: How many ECB Governing Council voting rights are shown on slide 18?

Answer without JavaScript

21 voting rights, not 27 members. The 15 rotating NCB votes plus six permanent Board votes make 21.

2. Explain how the monthly ECB rotation works

Slide 17 groups euro-area countries by the size of their economies and financial sectors. Ranks 1–5, listed as Germany, France, Italy, Spain and the Netherlands, share four votes with monthly rotation. Ranks 6–21, the other 16 countries, share eleven votes, also rotating monthly. One vote belongs to each available voting right, not to every attending governor. The six Executive Board votes do not rotate.

Worked example · Who lacks a vote this month?
  1. 1. Among the top five governors: 5 − 4 = 1 does not vote.
  2. 2. Among the other sixteen governors: 16 − 11 = 5 do not vote.
  3. 3. Altogether, 1 + 5 = 6 governors lack a vote; this also checks as 27 − 21 = 6.
Fact check · Conflicting slide counts. Slide 18’s graphic says “Further 14 members share 11 votes”, while slide 17 says ranks 6–21 include 16 countries. The latter is arithmetically consistent with the slide’s 21-country count: 21 − 5 = 16. Both source statements are retained in L017 and L018.
Quick check: In the five-country ECB group, how many governors lack a vote during one monthly rotation?

Answer without JavaScript

One: five governors share four voting rights, so 5 − 4 = 1.

Slide 18 also sketches the US Federal Reserve’s Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC). Its diagram allocates seven voting rights to the Fed’s Board of Governors and five to regional Federal Reserve Banks: twelve in total. The graphic separates New York from groups of regional Reserve Banks whose votes rotate. The ECB diagram, by comparison, gives six Board votes and fifteen rotating national-central-bank votes.

Worked example · Check the two committee totals
  1. 1. ECB: 6 Executive Board + 15 NCB votes = 21.
  2. 2. Fed FOMC: 7 Board of Governors + 5 Reserve Bank votes = 12.
  3. 3. Difference in committee voting rights: 21 − 12 = 9. This is not a measure of policy influence.
Quick check: How many FOMC votes are shown on the Fed side of slide 18?

Answer without JavaScript

Twelve: 7 Board of Governors votes + 5 Reserve Bank votes.

Topic 05 · slides 19–20

Explain how ECB independence is protected

A government wants cheaper loans. Can it order the ECB to cut rates?

1. Protect ECB decisions and decision-makers

Institutional independence means the ECB takes no instructions from EU institutions, member-state governments or other bodies. Personal independence protects its Executive Board members: their term is eight years, non-renewable, and dismissal is possible only for incapacity or serious misconduct. Disagreeing with a rate decision is not one of those dismissal grounds. These protections let the ECB make unpopular decisions without following government orders.

ILLUSTRATIVE · Political pressure
  1. A finance minister orders an ECB rate cut.
  2. Identify the blocked action: institutional independence means the ECB cannot take this instruction.
  3. A Board member could not be dismissed merely for disagreeing with the minister; the slide's only stated dismissal grounds are incapacity and serious misconduct.
Quick check: What term does slide 19 give an ECB Executive Board member?

Answer without JavaScript

Eight years, without renewal. Dismissal is limited to incapacity or serious misconduct.

2. Choose tools independently, but respect the lending ban

Functional or operational independence means the ECB can choose its monetary-policy instruments and decide how and when to use them. Yet slide 20 expressly prohibits ECB loans to EU institutions and national public-sector entities. Instrument freedom is therefore not unlimited. The distinction is important: selecting a permitted policy tool is different from directly lending to a government body, which the slide says is forbidden.

ILLUSTRATIVE · Tool choice versus financing
  1. The ECB decides when to use a permitted monetary-policy instrument: operational independence applies.
  2. A national public-sector entity requests an ECB loan: the slide's lending prohibition applies.
  3. Conclusion: independence in choosing instruments does not override the prohibition.
Quick check: Can the ECB directly lend to a national public-sector entity because it is independent?

Answer without JavaScript

No. The ECB can choose permitted instruments but cannot lend to those public-sector bodies.

3. Separate financial resources from legal rights

Financial independence gives the ECB its own budget; its capital is subscribed and paid by euro-area national central banks. Legal independence gives the ECB its own legal personality, including the ability to apply to the European Court of Justice. The first concerns the resources available for running the institution; the second concerns acting in its own name under the law. Neither is a substitute for the other.

ILLUSTRATIVE · Match the safeguard
  1. The question asks who supplies the ECB's subscribed and paid capital: euro-area national central banks, under financial independence.
  2. The question asks whether the ECB can apply to the European Court of Justice: yes, under legal independence.
  3. Remember: owning a budget is financial; acting as a legal entity is legal.
Quick check: Which safeguard allows the ECB to apply to the European Court of Justice?

Answer without JavaScript

Legal independence, due to its own legal personality and ability to apply to the Court.

Remember
  • Institutional independence blocks outside instructions; personal independence protects Board members.
  • Executive Board appointments last eight years without renewal; dismissal is tightly restricted.
  • The ECB controls permitted policy tools, its own budget and capital, and its legal standing.
  • Watch out: instrument autonomy does not allow loans to EU bodies or national public-sector entities.
Lecture details (2 slides)
  • Slide 19: Institutional ECB independence: no instructions from EU institutions, member-state governments or other bodies. Personal independence: ECB Executive Board members have eight-year non-renewable terms and dismissal only for incapacity or serious misconduct.
  • Slide 20: Functional/operational independence: instruments and authority to choose how/when to use them; ECB prohibited from loans to EU bodies or national public-sector entities. Financial independence: own budget, capital subscribed and paid by euro-area NCBs. Legal independence: own legal personality, can apply to European Court of Justice.

Topic 06 · slides 21–23

Explain how banks obtain ECB refinancing

A commercial bank needs euros for its payments. How can it borrow directly from the ECB?

1. Exchange eligible collateral for central-bank liquidity

In slide 21, the ECB supplies liquidity, meaning funds a bank can use for payments, while the commercial bank provides eligible collateral, assets accepted as security for the borrowing. The bank also pays interest. The collateral must satisfy the ECB's minimum requirements. This is refinancing: the bank receives funds without the ECB simply handing it free money. The diagram below shows what moves between the two institutions.

ILLUSTRATIVE · Follow a refinancing operation
  1. A bank needs additional liquidity and holds assets that meet the ECB's collateral requirements.
  2. The bank provides qualifying collateral and receives euros from the ECB.
  3. The bank owes interest for the borrowed funds. The arrangement is borrowing, not a gift.
Quick check: What does the commercial bank provide to obtain ECB refinancing?

Answer without JavaScript

Eligible collateral meeting minimum requirements, plus interest on the borrowing.

2. Match the ECB's instruments to their time horizons

The ECB uses open-market operations to manage liquidity, steer interest rates and signal its monetary-policy stance. Slide 22 highlights main refinancing operations (MROs) among operations lasting one week or longer. By contrast, standing facilities operate overnight: the marginal lending facility supplies short-term funds, while the deposit facility absorbs funds banks place at the ECB. Those facilities help limit volatility in overnight rates. Their interest-rate corridor comes in the next topic.

ILLUSTRATIVE · Choose the relevant operation
  1. A bank needs funds only overnight: identify the marginal lending facility.
  2. A bank wants to place extra funds overnight at the ECB: identify the deposit facility.
  3. The ECB wants to steer system liquidity through regular one-week refinancing: identify an MRO.
Quick check: Which ECB instrument is a main open-market refinancing operation?

Answer without JavaScript

MRO. It is an open-market operation; the other two are overnight standing facilities.

3. Allocate funds in a historical MRO auction

Slide 23 describes pre-financial-crisis weekly MRO auctions. The ECB announced the total amount of one-week liquidity available and a minimum interest rate. Banks submitted bids specifying both an amount and a rate. The higher-rate bids received allocations first until the announced funds were exhausted. This historical tender design is not a description of all later ECB operations: the crisis changed the procedures.

ILLUSTRATIVE · Rank bids and allocate €80 million
  1. The ECB offers €80 million for one week at a 3.0% minimum bid rate. The eligible bids are A: €35m at 3.4%; B: €30m at 3.2%; C: €45m at 3.0%.
  2. Allocate Bank A €35m first. Remaining supply: €80m − €35m = €45m.
  3. Allocate Bank B €30m next. Remaining supply: €45m − €30m = €15m.
  4. Allocate Bank C the last €15m. Check: €35m + €30m + €15m = €80m; C receives €30m less than it requested.
Quick check: In that illustrative €80m auction, how much is left for Bank C?

Answer without JavaScript

€15 million: €80m − €35m − €30m = €15m. It is an illustrative bid-allocation example, not a historical ECB tender result.

Remember
  • ECB refinancing exchanges euros for a bank's interest payments and eligible collateral.
  • Open-market operations steer liquidity and policy rates; standing facilities work overnight.
  • Pre-crisis MROs used weekly auctions for one-week liquidity, allocating higher-rate bids first.
  • Watch out: the auction procedure described is historical; later crisis measures changed ECB operations.
Lecture details (3 slides)
  • Slide 21: Bank ↔ ECB schematic: ECB supplies euros/liquidity to bank; bank supplies interest plus eligible collateral subject to minimum collateral requirements.
  • Slide 22: ECB monetary-policy operations: open-market operations with maturities of one week or more; overnight standing facilities (marginal lending, deposit facility). Open-market operations, especially main refinancing operations (MROs), steer interest rates, manage liquidity and signal stance; standing facilities supply/absorb overnight liquidity and curb short-rate volatility.
  • Slide 23: Pre-crisis main refinancing operations (MROs): weekly auctions; ECB announced total one-week funds and minimum MRO interest rate; banks bid amounts and rates; highest interest-rate bids receive allotments first.
Topic 07 · slides 24–26

Use standing facilities to interpret overnight interest rates

Why would a bank borrow overnight from another bank instead of paying the ECB’s lending rate?

1. Understand the overnight interest-rate corridor

The marginal lending facility lets banks borrow from the ECB overnight at its lending rate. The deposit facility lets banks place surplus funds at the ECB overnight at its deposit rate. Slide 24 describes these rates as a corridor for EONIA, the interbank overnight interest rate named in the lecture. A bank generally prefers borrowing from peers below the ECB lending rate; another prefers lending to peers above the ECB deposit rate. The corridor limits short-rate volatility.

ILLUSTRATIVE · Both banks prefer the market
  1. The ECB lending rate is 2.90%, while its deposit rate is 2.50% (the labels on slide 25).
  2. Suppose an interbank overnight loan is offered at 2.70% per year. A borrowing bank pays 0.20 percentage points less than the 2.90% ECB alternative.
  3. The lending bank receives 0.20 percentage points more than the 2.50% deposit alternative. Both prefer the illustrative 2.70% deal, ignoring other constraints.
Quick check: Why might a bank lend funds overnight to another bank?

Answer without JavaScript

To earn a rate above the ECB deposit rate, the slide’s lower corridor boundary.

2. Read the three labelled ECB policy rates

Slide 25 plots the deposit rate (blue), main refinancing operation (MRO) rate (red), and overnight lending rate (green). The horizontal axis extends from January 1999 to January 2029, although the plotted rate series stop around late 2026 or early 2027; the annual-percentage axis spans roughly −1% to 6%. Its labelled rate snapshot is 2.50%, 2.65%, and 2.90%, respectively. The historical series falls toward or below zero after the financial crisis, then rises sharply around 2022–2023.

Worked example · Calculate the slide’s rate gaps
  1. Top of the corridor: 2.90%; bottom: 2.50%.
  2. Corridor width: 2.90 − 2.50 = 0.40 percentage points (40 basis points).
  3. Distance from deposit to MRO: 2.65 − 2.50 = 0.15 pp. Distance from MRO to lending: 2.90 − 2.65 = 0.25 pp.
  4. Check: 0.15 + 0.25 = 0.40 pp. Here MRO is not exactly at the corridor midpoint.
Slide-date caution: Slide 25 labels MRO at 2.65%, but slide 40 calls 2.15% “currently”. The as-of dates are not reconciled in the slides, so neither number should be treated as a live quotation.
Quick check: How wide is the corridor using the labelled slide 25 rates?

Answer without JavaScript

2.90% − 2.50% = 0.40 percentage points = 40 basis points.

Topic 08 · slide 27

Describe how the ECB responded to the financial crisis

Banks stop lending to each other. What can the ECB change besides its interest rate?

1. Ease both borrowing costs and collateral rules

Slide 27 describes looser monetary policy during crisis periods. Lower ECB interest rates make refinancing cheaper. Looser minimum collateral requirements allow a wider range of assets to qualify as security for a bank's borrowing. These solve different problems: expensive funds versus inadequate eligible security. A bank still needs to meet the remaining collateral and funding conditions.

ILLUSTRATIVE · Spot the obstacle
  1. A bank has sufficient eligible collateral but finds central-bank funds expensive: an interest-rate cut addresses that cost.
  2. Another bank lacks assets acceptable under the old rules: looser collateral requirements may help.
  3. Neither measure means free funding or an end to eligibility checks.
Quick check: Which measure directly addresses a shortage of eligible collateral?

Answer without JavaScript

Looser minimum collateral requirements, subject to the revised eligibility rules.

2. Replace scarce auction allotments with longer, predictable funding

Before the crisis, the MRO auctions described by the lecture allocated a limited announced total. Fixed-rate full allotment instead supplies the full amount of each qualifying bank's request at the announced rate, subject to collateral and other conditions. Longer-term refinancing operations (LTROs) and targeted longer-term refinancing operations (TLTROs) extend the funding horizon beyond the familiar one-week operation. Full allotment changes the quantity constraint; longer maturity changes the borrowing period.

ILLUSTRATIVE · Calculate full allotment
  1. At a fixed announced rate, eligible Bank A requests €40 million and eligible Bank B requests €60 million.
  2. Assuming both satisfy all requirements, A receives €40 million and B receives €60 million.
  3. Total qualifying funds allotted: €40m + €60m = €100m. These are invented example amounts, not actual ECB tenders.
Quick check: Which rule fills eligible requests without a preset aggregate allotment?

Answer without JavaScript

Fixed-rate full allotment. Eligible amounts are provided at the set interest rate.

3. Recognize QE and the 2012 commitment

Slide 27 also includes quantitative easing (QE), expanded in the next topic, alongside the refinancing changes. Separately, Mario Draghi's 2012 statement pledged to do “whatever it takes” to preserve the euro, within the ECB's mandate. Those words signalled commitment rather than defining a loan facility. The slide groups tools used across crisis periods but does not establish that every one began in 2012 or at the same time.

ILLUSTRATIVE · Match the response
  1. Need funding for a longer period? Identify LTROs or TLTROs.
  2. Need asset-purchase stimulus rather than refinancing? Identify QE.
  3. Need to identify a statement signalling euro preservation? Recall Draghi's 2012 assurance, not a separate maturity or interest rate.
Quick check: What did Draghi's 2012 statement primarily communicate?

Answer without JavaScript

A commitment to preserving the euro within the ECB's mandate. The full wording remains in the slide source notes.

Remember
  • Rate cuts and broader collateral address borrowing costs and acceptable security.
  • Fixed-rate full allotment fills qualifying requests; LTROs/TLTROs extend maturity.

Part 3 · Quantitative easing and balance sheets

Follow an asset purchase from its purpose and programme to the charts, effects and criticisms.

Topic 09 · slides 28–29

Explain QE and identify what the ECB’s APP buys

When interest rates reach zero, how can central banks still inject liquidity?

1. Buy assets when conventional rate cuts run out of room

Slide 28 treats an interest rate of 0% as a floor: another rate cut is assumed unavailable. The alternative is quantitative easing (QE), in which a central bank buys assets such as government bonds, corporate bonds and asset-backed securities (ABS), securities supported by pools of underlying claims. Paying for those assets provides liquidity to markets. The lecture names the ECB and US Federal Reserve as central banks that used QE after the financial crisis.

ILLUSTRATIVE · Follow a €10m QE transaction
  1. A bank sells €10 million of eligible bonds to the ECB for €10 million.
  2. The bank’s bond holdings fall by €10m and its central-bank funds rise by €10m.
  3. The ECB now holds the bonds; the bank holds more liquidity. This asset swap does not automatically create €10m in new loans.
Fact check · Zero-rate assumption: Slide 28 presents 0% as the floor for interest-rate cuts, but the earlier slide 25 chart shows negative deposit rates. Treat the 0% argument as the lecturer’s simplified QE rationale, not a universal rule.
Quick check: How does slide 28 describe QE?

Answer without JavaScript

Central-bank purchases of securities such as government bonds, corporate bonds and ABS provide liquidity to markets.

2. Match the APP’s four programmes to their assets

The slide calls the ECB’s QE framework the Expanded Asset Purchase Programme (APP), labelled since January 2015 (01/2015). The ABSPP purchases asset-backed securities; CBPP3 covered bonds, meaning bonds secured by an asset pool; CSPP corporate-sector securities; and PSPP public-sector securities. These names identify asset types, not the volume of each programme. The January 2015 label is the lecture’s umbrella description, not an individual launch date for every component.

ILLUSTRATIVE · Classify three eligible assets
  1. An asset-backed security matches ABSPP; a covered bond matches CBPP3.
  2. A corporate-sector bond matches CSPP.
  3. A government or qualifying public-sector bond matches PSPP, subject to its restrictions.
Quick check: Which APP component covers corporate-sector bonds?

Answer without JavaScript

CSPP, the Corporate Sector Purchase Program.

3. Interpret the PSPP country-share rule and 33% limit

Slide 29 says PSPP purchases are allocated relative to each country’s shareholding in the ECB. It also states the ECB may own no more than 33% of a country’s debt. These are two different constraints: one concerns the country distribution of purchases; the other, the maximum holding. Retain the lecturer’s 33% wording, but do not treat it as a blanket current legal rule for every security and date without further verification.

ILLUSTRATIVE · Calculate the stated cap
  1. Assume a hypothetical debt base of €300 million.
  2. Under the simplified 33% statement, the maximum is 0.33 × €300m = €99m.
  3. Check: €300m − €99m = €201m, or 67% of the hypothetical total. Actual PSPP limits can require more detail.
Quick check: What is 33% of a hypothetical €300m debt total?

Answer without JavaScript

0.33 × €300m = €99m, using the slide’s simplified percentage.

Remember
  • QE buys assets to add liquidity when conventional rate cuts offer limited room.
  • ECB APP components: ABSPP, CBPP3, CSPP and PSPP, each named for its assets.
  • Slide 29 links PSPP country purchases to ECB shareholdings and states a 33% debt cap.
  • Watch out: zero as a hard rate floor and the broad 33% rule are slide simplifications, not universal current policy.

Topic 10 · slides 30–31

Distinguish APP purchase flows from asset holdings

If the ECB buys fewer bonds, does the mountain of bonds it already owns suddenly disappear?

1. Read monthly net purchases: the flow

Slide 30 measures monthly net APP purchases, meaning the change from purchases after relevant repayments or redemptions, in EUR billions per month. Its horizontal axis runs from 2015–2026, and its vertical scale spans roughly −€60bn to +€90bn. Positive bars were substantial in 2015–2018, resumed in 2020–2022, and turned mostly negative after 2023. Negative net purchases indicate a shrinking purchase position, not necessarily active bond selling.

ILLUSTRATIVE · Work out one month’s net flow
  1. Assume the ECB purchases €15bn of APP bonds while €25bn of existing holdings mature without replacement.
  2. Net change from those transactions: €15bn − €25bn = −€10bn for that month.
  3. A negative monthly flow means holdings can shrink, even though the ECB still purchases some bonds.
Quick check: A monthly APP net purchase of −€10bn implies what?

Answer without JavaScript

Net transactions reduce the portfolio by €10bn in this simplified example; they do not erase the existing stock.

2. Read outstanding assets: the stock

Slide 31 shows a much larger value: the APP portfolio stock, in EUR billions outstanding. Its scale runs from 0 to €3,600bn across 2015–2026. The portfolio rises strongly, levels near 2019, increases again, peaks around 2022–2023 and declines through 2026. It remains substantial during months with negative net purchases because earlier purchases have not all matured. Think bathtub level versus water added or drained this month.

ILLUSTRATIVE · Update the APP stock
  1. Suppose €3,400bn in APP securities are outstanding at the start of the month.
  2. Net asset transactions are −€10bn, as in the previous example.
  3. Ignoring valuation and other accounting changes, end-of-month holdings = €3,400bn − €10bn = €3,390bn, still far above zero.
Quick check: Can the ECB’s APP holdings remain large during negative net-purchase months?

Answer without JavaScript

Yes. Stock accumulates past transactions and can decline gradually while remaining large.

3. Track the four programmes without confusing their labels

Both slide charts stack four familiar APP programmes: PSPP (blue, dominant), CBPP3 (orange), CSPP (yellow) and ABSPP (green). Slide 30 also overlays a dotted average monthly APP target, not a fifth purchase programme. Slide 31 is titled “Cumulated Purchases under the APP,” but its bars shrink after 2023. A running total of all historical gross purchases could never fall, so the graph is better read as holdings or a net evolving stock.

ILLUSTRATIVE · Spot the misleading title
  1. A chart labelled “cumulated gross purchases” falls from €3,000bn to €2,900bn.
  2. Gross lifetime purchases cannot decrease: a new purchase adds to their cumulative total.
  3. A decreasing measure instead describes a net stock, portfolio holdings or a similar adjusted series. Check what the axis and source actually count.
Fact check · Slide 31’s title: Its “Cumulated Purchases” label conflicts with the post-2023 decline. Preserve the title, but interpret the plotted declining measure as an evolving holdings/net stock rather than an irreversible running sum of gross purchases.
Quick check: Why is “cumulated purchases” a tricky label for the slide 31 chart?

Answer without JavaScript

The series declines, whereas cumulative gross purchases can only stay constant or rise. Holdings, unlike cumulative purchases, can shrink.

Remember
  • Slide 30 measures monthly net flows, which can be positive or negative.
  • Slide 31 measures a large outstanding stock that rises then contracts after 2022–23.
  • PSPP dominates the blue portions; CBPP3, CSPP and ABSPP add smaller segments.
  • Watch out: a shrinking “cumulated purchases” series is not cumulative gross purchases.

Topic 11 · slides 32–34

Compare central-bank balance-sheet expansion and runoff

The ECB’s assets balloon during crises, then shrink. Does a smaller balance sheet mean all its earlier purchases vanished?

1. Read the ECB balance sheet in the right units

A central bank’s balance sheet records its assets and liabilities. Slide 32 charts ECB assets from 1999–2025, with the vertical axis in EUR millions up to about 9,000,000. Assets expanded around the financial crisis, during QE after 2014 and in 2020–2021. They peak around 2021 and contract later. The chart stacks coloured asset components, but its legend is not legible enough to assign reliable names to each colour.

ILLUSTRATIVE · Convert chart units without inventing a peak
  1. The slide’s vertical-axis upper marker is approximately 9,000,000 EUR millions.
  2. Convert to billions: 9,000,000 ÷ 1,000 = 9,000 EUR billions.
  3. Convert to trillions: 9,000 ÷ 1,000 = €9 trillion. This is the approximate axis ceiling, not a claim that assets reached €9 trillion.
Quick check: What does the roughly 9,000,000 top tick on slide 32 mean?

Answer without JavaScript

About €9 trillion as an axis ceiling: 9,000,000 million euros = €9 trillion.

2. Compare ECB and Fed expansion and runoff

Slide 33 instead tracks the US Federal Reserve (Fed), over 2008–2026, in USD millions. Its vertical axis reaches roughly 8–9 million USD millions; it rises after 2008, expands sharply again in 2020, peaks around 2022 and generally declines thereafter. Balance-sheet runoff means the stock of assets contracts, for example as securities mature without full replacement. ECB and Fed both expanded around crises and later reduced assets, but their dates and currencies differ.

ILLUSTRATIVE · Decode the Fed chart’s scale
  1. Suppose a Fed chart tick is at 8,000,000 USD millions.
  2. 8,000,000 ÷ 1,000 = 8,000 USD billions = $8 trillion.
  3. The slide’s approximate 8–9 million range describes the axis scale. Do not turn that range into an exact peak or compare it numerically with euros without conversion.
Quick check: Which comparison is sound without converting units or matching dates?

Answer without JavaScript

Both expanded during crises and later contracted. Their currency and time axes differ.

3. Use a common index for international comparisons

Slide 34 changes the measurement entirely. The Economist chart dated 27 April 2017 compares the Bank of England, Fed, ECB and Bank of Japan over roughly 2007–2017 using an index with January 2007 = 100. An index tracks proportional growth from a common starting value rather than total dollars, euros or yen. Some balance sheets reach several times their 2007 size, but that does not tell us which institution held the most assets in cash terms.

ILLUSTRATIVE · Read an indexed balance sheet
  1. Suppose a central bank's balance sheet had an index of 100 in January 2007.
  2. If the index later reaches 300, divide 300 by 100 = 3: its balance sheet is three times as large.
  3. Relative growth is (300 − 100) ÷ 100 × 100% = 200%. You cannot infer its absolute currency value from the index alone.
Quick check: In the illustrative index example, 300 means:

Answer without JavaScript

300 ÷ 100 = 3× the baseline. The increase is 200%, not 300%.

Topic 12 · slides 35–37

Evaluate what QE achieves and the risks it creates

Can central-bank bond purchases help firms borrow and simultaneously weaken investors’ caution?

1. Trace what the ECB wants QE to achieve

The lecture presents four related QE aims, not guaranteed results: stabilize inflation; improve companies’ financing conditions; increase bank credit to the real economy, supporting investment and growth; and reduce instability in risky assets, long-term bonds and sovereign-bond markets. When the ECB purchases securities, extra demand can support their prices and ease financing conditions. Better market funding may encourage banks and companies to spend and invest, but each link depends on how financial markets and borrowers respond.

ILLUSTRATIVE · Identify the intended effect
  1. The ECB buys bonds to support prices and calm a stressed sovereign-bond market.
  2. Lower perceived financing pressure may make it easier for banks and firms to obtain funds.
  3. If more credit reaches firms, investment and growth may strengthen, helping inflation stabilize. This is a potential chain, not a guaranteed outcome.
Quick check: Which outcome is explicitly an aim of QE in slide 35?

Answer without JavaScript

Improved financing and more credit, alongside investment, growth, inflation stabilization and market stability.

2. Recognize the downside risks

Slide 36 raises three criticisms. Large holdings of sovereign bonds could generate losses if a country defaults; those losses ultimately concern the ECB’s shareholders and euro-area members. Cheap financing can also encourage asset-price bubbles, when prices rise beyond sustainable values, increasing financial instability. Finally, QE can affect exchange rates. These are potential consequences, not measured effects or proof that QE always causes bubbles, defaults or depreciation.

ILLUSTRATIVE · Calculate a potential bond loss
  1. Suppose a sovereign bond held at €100 falls to €70 after a credit event.
  2. The hypothetical loss in value is €100 − €70 = €30, or 30% of €100.
  3. That demonstrates loss exposure, not an automatic €30 taxpayer bill; actual accounting and burden-sharing are more complicated.
Quick check: Which is one of the slide 36 QE criticisms?

Answer without JavaScript

Cheap financing may encourage asset bubbles and financial instability. Sovereign-default losses and exchange-rate effects are other concerns.

3. Explain moral hazard in government-bond markets

Moral hazard arises when protection from a loss encourages someone to take more risk. Slide 37 contrasts primary-market purchases (buying newly issued government debt directly from the state, prohibited for the ECB) with secondary-market purchases (buying an existing bond from an investor). The initial private buyer still bears sovereign risk. But if investors expect the ECB to buy later, they may underestimate risk when buying new bonds, weakening market discipline.

ILLUSTRATIVE · Follow the risk incentive
  1. A private investor buys a new government bond directly from the issuer and initially bears the default risk.
  2. The investor expects the ECB may later buy existing bonds in the secondary market.
  3. That expectation might make the initial purchase feel safer and weaken scrutiny of the issuer, even though a later ECB purchase is not guaranteed.
Fact check · Slide 37 wording: The slide’s sentence about “cannot buy ... only on secondary markets, not on primary” is internally contradictory. Its intended distinction is that direct ECB government-bond purchases in the primary market are prohibited, while secondary-market purchases can occur; the original wording remains recorded in L037.
Quick check: Which government-bond purchase does slide 37 identify as prohibited for the ECB?

Answer without JavaScript

Direct primary-market purchases from the issuing government. Secondary-market purchases and the moral-hazard concern are different issues.

Remember
  • QE aims to stabilize inflation and markets while improving credit, investment and growth.
  • Risks include sovereign-bond losses, asset bubbles and exchange-rate effects.
  • Anticipated secondary-market ECB buying can weaken investors’ assessment of sovereign risk.
  • Watch out: ECB government-debt purchases in the primary market are prohibited, unlike permitted secondary-market purchases.

  • Slide 36: Critiques: losses on large government-bond holdings in event of country default would ultimately concern ECB shareholders/euro-area members; cheap financing could encourage asset bubbles and instability; exchange-rate effects.
  • Slide 37: Moral hazard: ECB government-bond purchases on secondary market versus prohibited direct/primary-market financing. Initial private investor must take sovereign risk; anticipated ECB secondary buying may reduce perceived risk of buying new bonds and weaken market discipline.

Topic 13 · slides 38–40

Connect euro-area inflation to ECB policy since 2022

Inflation surged toward 10% in 2022. Why did the ECB raise rates and let its bond portfolio shrink?

1. Read the inflation surge and subsequent fall

Slide 38 tracks the euro area’s Harmonised Index of Consumer Prices (HICP) inflation rate: the year-on-year percentage change in consumer prices. From approximately January 2020–April 2025, inflation dips near or below zero around 2020, surges to approximately 10% in 2022, then falls toward about 2% in 2024–April 2025. Falling inflation means slower price growth, not necessarily falling prices. These are approximate chart readings, not exact monthly observations.

Approximate slide-reading example · Inflation versus prices
  1. Slide 38 shows inflation around 10% in 2022 and near 2% later.
  2. Approximate fall in the annual inflation rate: 10% − 2% = 8 percentage points.
  3. Prices can still rise at 2% per year. This is disinflation (inflation slows), not necessarily deflation (prices fall).
Quick check: If inflation falls from around 10% to around 2%, what follows?

Answer without JavaScript

Prices can keep rising at a slower rate. The approximate inflation rate fell by eight percentage points.

2. Decompose inflation into its contributors

Slide 39’s European Commission Spring 2025 chart covers January 2021–January 2025. Its vertical axis spans roughly −2 to 12 percentage points of annual price change or contribution. The thick black line is total HICP inflation. The stacked areas separately show energy, unprocessed food, non-energy industrial goods, processed food (including alcohol and tobacco), and services. Energy drove much of the 2022 spike; services still contributed positively later. A category’s contribution is not the whole inflation rate.

ILLUSTRATIVE · Add component contributions
  1. Assume energy contributes +4 pp; unprocessed food +1 pp; industrial goods +1 pp; processed food +2 pp; services +2 pp.
  2. Add the illustrative contributions: 4 + 1 + 1 + 2 + 2 = 10 percentage points of total annual inflation.
  3. This is an invented arithmetic example, not a digitization of slide 39. Negative contributions would subtract from the total.
Quick check: What does the thick black line on slide 39 represent?

Answer without JavaScript

Total HICP inflation. The coloured stacked categories show individual contributions, which can be positive or negative.

3. Connect the inflation episode to policy tightening

Slide 40 describes ECB tightening after 2022. The main refinancing operation (MRO) rate reached 4.50% in September 2023; the same slide later calls it “currently 2.15%” without an as-of date. The ECB stopped reinvesting APP redemptions from July 2023 and PEPP redemptions from December 2024, allowing holdings to run off. It offered no new TLTROs or PELTROs. Regular MROs retained fixed-rate full allotment with one-week maturity; ordinary LTROs had three-month maturity.

ILLUSTRATIVE · Interpret tighter rates and runoff
  1. An MRO rate increase makes refinancing more expensive at that time, other conditions equal.
  2. If €20bn of APP assets mature and their proceeds are not reinvested, the holdings stock falls by €20bn, ignoring other transactions and valuation changes.
  3. Regular one-week MRO access can still operate through fixed-rate full allotment while the ECB ends reinvestments and offers no new TLTROs or PELTROs.
Slide-date caution: Slide 40’s undated “currently 2.15%” MRO figure differs from slide 25’s 2.65% label. Retain both as slide-specific statements; neither is verified as the rate today.
Quick check: Which pair of reinvestment changes does slide 40 give?

Answer without JavaScript

APP from July 2023; PEPP from December 2024. The slide separately notes no new TLTROs or PELTROs.

Institutional ECB independence: no instructions from EU institutions, member-state governments or other bodies. Personal independence: ECB Executive Board members have eight-year non-renewable terms and dismissal only for incapacity or serious misconduct.

topic-t11
L020Slide 20independence & restrictions

Functional/operational independence: instruments and authority to choose how/when to use them; ECB prohibited from loans to EU bodies or national public-sector entities. Financial independence: own budget, capital subscribed and paid by euro-area NCBs. Legal independence: own legal personality, can apply to European Court of Justice.

topic-t11
L021Slide 21monetary operation diagram

Bank ↔ ECB schematic: ECB supplies euros/liquidity to bank; bank supplies interest plus eligible collateral subject to minimum collateral requirements.

topic-t04
L022Slide 22tools & maturities

ECB monetary-policy operations: open-market operations with maturities of one week or more; overnight standing facilities (marginal lending, deposit facility). Open-market operations, especially main refinancing operations (MROs), steer interest rates, manage liquidity and signal stance; standing facilities supply/absorb overnight liquidity and curb short-rate volatility.

topic-t04
L023Slide 23auction mechanism

Pre-crisis main refinancing operations (MROs): weekly auctions; ECB announced total one-week funds and minimum MRO interest rate; banks bid amounts and rates; highest interest-rate bids receive allotments first.

Source caution: Specific description is historical pre-financial-crisis practice.

topic-t04
L024Slide 24interest-rate corridor

Marginal lending facility: banks borrow overnight from ECB at overnight lending rate. Deposit facility: banks deposit overnight at overnight deposit rate. Slide describes these as a corridor limiting EONIA interbank overnight rate: borrow from peers below ECB lending rate; lend to peers above ECB deposit rate.

Source caution: Slide uses EONIA terminology historically; preserve, not silently modernize.

ECB key rates time-series chart, horizontal axis Jan 1999–Jan 2029 (plotted observations stop around late 2026/early 2027); vertical axis percentages approximately −1% to 6%; three series: overnight deposit (blue), MRO (red), overnight lending (green). Labels on slide: MRO 2.65%; overnight lending 2.90%; overnight deposit 2.50%. Rates fell near/below zero after crisis and rose sharply around 2022–23.

Source caution: Rates labeled on chart differ from slide 40's 'currently 2.15%' MRO; dates/as-of context is not reconciled.

Interest-rate evolution comparing central banks. Left graphic (The Economist 06.09.2014) shows Federal Reserve, Bank of England, ECB, Bank of Japan policy-rate paths roughly 2000–2014. Right graphic (Financial Times 2022) shows tightening paths roughly 2007–2022, including Bank of England, US Fed, ECB and Bank of Canada.

Source caution: The slide title mentions BoJ but the right-hand chart's legend includes Bank of Canada instead.

topic-t12
L027Slide 27crisis measures & quotation

During financial crisis: lower ECB rates; looser minimum collateral requirements; QE; open-market changes to fixed-rate full allotment and longer maturities (LTROs, TLTROs). Mario Draghi, 2012: 'Within our mandate, the ECB is ready to do whatever it takes to preserve the euro. And believe me, it will be enough'.

Source caution: The slide groups interventions across crisis periods; exact start dates not individually supplied.

topic-t05
L028Slide 28QE rationale & assets

When central-bank rate already at 0%, the slide argues policy cannot be further expanded by lower rates, so central banks buy government bonds, corporate bonds or asset-backed securities (ABS) to inject liquidity: quantitative easing (QE); examples ECB and FED after financial crisis.

Source caution: The zero lower-bound statement is simplified lecturer reasoning, not a timeless absolute.

topic-t06
L029Slide 29APP programs & limits

Expanded Asset Purchase Programme (APP) listed as ECB QE since 01/2015: ABSPP (asset-backed securities), CBPP3 (covered bonds), CSPP (corporate sector), PSPP (public sector). PSPP buys by country shareholding in ECB; slide states ECB may own at most 33% of a country's debt.

Source caution: The 'since 01/2015' and 33% rule are stated in broad terms; preserve pending context checks.

Positive net purchases were high 2015–18, resumed 2020–22; negative net purchases dominate after 2023 (runoff). Source ECB (2026).

topic-t13
L035Slide 35QE aims & transmission

ECB QE aims: stabilize inflation; improve company financing conditions; increase bank credit to real economy, stimulate investment and growth; reduce instability in risky, long-term and sovereign-bond markets.

Source caution: Aims, not guaranteed realized outcomes.

topic-t08
L036Slide 36QE critiques

Critiques: losses on large government-bond holdings in event of country default would ultimately concern ECB shareholders/euro-area members; cheap financing could encourage asset bubbles and instability; exchange-rate effects.

Source caution: Lecturer's risk framing, not quantified outcomes.

topic-t08
L037Slide 37moral hazard & market distinction

Moral hazard: ECB government-bond purchases on secondary market versus prohibited direct/primary-market financing. Initial private investor must take sovereign risk; anticipated ECB secondary buying may reduce perceived risk of buying new bonds and weaken market discipline.

Source caution: Source text literally says 'cannot buy ... only on secondary markets', conflicting with its subsequent explanation; probable wording error flagged, not silently corrected.

ECB tightening after 2022: MRO rose to 4.50% (09/2023); slide calls MRO 'currently 2.15%'; PEPP stops reinvesting redemptions since 12/2024; APP no reinvestment of redemptions from 07/2023; no new TLTROs or PELTROs; MROs retain fixed-rate full allotment; MRO maturity one week, LTRO maturity three months.
