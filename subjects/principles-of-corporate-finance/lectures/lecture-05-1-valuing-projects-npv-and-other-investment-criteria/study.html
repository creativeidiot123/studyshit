Establish NPV as the value benchmark

Translate dated cash flows into shareholder value, then relate investment decisions to the shareholder’s alternative.

Calculate NPV and judge whether a project creates value

A company has $1 million in cash. Should it spend that money on Project X?

Discount future cash, then subtract what you spend

Future money is not interchangeable with cash today. Net present value (NPV) tells you how much value a project adds today after allowing for its investment cost and the time value of money. The basic rule is simple: accept a project when NPV is positive; reject it when NPV is negative.

NPV = C0 + C1/(1 + r) + C2/(1 + r)2 + C3/(1 + r)3 + …

On slide 3, C0 is the net cash flow immediately (usually negative), Ct is the net cash flow at the end of period t, and r is the opportunity cost of capital per period (for example, 10% = 0.10 per year). All cash flows use the same currency. The equation shown assumes the same per-period rate for each future date, with r greater than −100%.

The denominator grows with time because a payment two years away must be discounted twice. Adding today's equivalents is meaningful; simply adding unadjusted future payments is not.

Quick check: Why is C2 divided by (1+r)2?

Answer and reason (works without JavaScript)

It arrives after two periods. Discounting twice gives C2/(1+r)2.

Watch what happens to the company’s market value

Slide 4 starts with $1 million cash and $9 million of other assets. Rejecting Project X leaves the firm worth $10 million. Accepting it uses up the cash but creates a new asset with present value (PV). Whether shareholders win depends on the value of that asset compared with the $1 million paid.

Worked calculation: Project X (lecture balance sheet + ILLUSTRATIVE payoff)

  1. GIVEN. Lecturer’s starting amounts: cash = $1.00 million, other assets = $9.00 million, initial project outlay = $1.00 million at t = 0. ILLUSTRATIVE only: the project pays C1 = +$1.43 million at the end of year 1 and the annual cost of capital is r = 10%.
  2. Match units and dates. Work throughout in $ millions. Set C0 = −1.00 at t = 0 and r = 10/100 = 0.10 per year. Assume that single year-1 payoff is the project’s only future cash flow.
  3. Discount the payoff. PV = 1.43/(1+0.10)1 = 1.43/1.10 = $1.30 million.
  4. Subtract the investment. NPV = −1.00 + 1.30 = +$0.30 million.
  5. Use the lecturer’s market-value table. Reject X: 1 + 9 + 0 = $10.00 million. Accept X: 0 + 9 + 1.30 = $10.30 million.
  6. Interpret and check. Shareholder value rises by 10.30 − 10.00 = $0.30 million. That agrees with NPV. Since project PV ($1.30m) exceeds its cost ($1.00m), accepting X makes sense under these assumptions.

Explain NPV's opportunity cost and core principles

If shareholders can earn 10% elsewhere, why let the manager keep their money?

Money inside the firm is not free

Slide 5 offers two routes. A financial manager can spend cash on Project X, or the company can pay dividends or buy back shares, allowing shareholders to invest themselves. The opportunity cost of capital is the return shareholders could instead earn on a comparable-risk financial asset. Investing company cash is justified only when the project creates value after accounting for that foregone alternative.

Quick check: What makes a shareholder's outside investment relevant to Project X?

Answer and explanation without JavaScript

The shareholder's comparable-risk outside return is the opportunity cost sacrificed by funding X.

The popular appraisal methods are not equally reliable

Slide 6 says NPV and internal rate of return (IRR) are the most widely applied decision yardsticks. Its percentages describe how often methods were applied in that comparison, not the returns earned by any investment. The slide gives no survey date or sample size, so do not interpret the chart as current statistics.

Quick check: Does the slide's IRR 76% mean a project earns 76%?

Answer and explanation without JavaScript

No. IRR 76% is a method-usage figure in the lecture's chart; NPV 75%, payback 57%, book return 20% and PI 12% are the others.

Three NPV principles, one value-additivity equation

First, a dollar today is worth more than a dollar tomorrow: this is the time value of money. Second, NPV depends on forecast cash flows and their opportunity cost of capital. Third, we discount all project cash flows into today's money before adding them. That third principle is value additivity:

NPV(A + B) = NPV(A) + NPV(B)

This works when the projects' cash flows are additive and each is discounted appropriately. If one project changes another's cash flows, that interaction must also be valued.

ILLUSTRATIVE worked calculation: test value additivity

  1. GIVEN: A invests $1.000m now and pays $1.320m after one year. B invests $0.500m now and pays $0.605m after one year. Projects do not interact, and both face the same comparable-risk annual opportunity cost of 10%.
  2. Dates, signs and units: C0,A = −1.000, C1,A = +1.320; C0,B = −0.500, C1,B = +0.605. All amounts in $ millions; r = 10% = 0.10 per year and both future payments are at t = 1.
  3. Project A: NPV(A) = −1.000 + 1.320/(1.10) = −1.000 + 1.200 = +$0.200m.
  4. Project B: NPV(B) = −0.500 + 0.605/(1.10) = −0.500 + 0.550 = +$0.050m.
  5. Add separately: NPV(A) + NPV(B) = 0.200 + 0.050 = +$0.250m.
  6. Calculate jointly as a check: C0,A+B = −1.500 and C1,A+B = 1.320 + 0.605 = 1.925. NPV(A+B) = −1.500 + 1.925/(1.10) = −1.500 + 1.750 = +$0.250m.
  7. Interpret: Both methods agree. The two projects together add $250,000 of today's value under the assumptions; a positive result also passes the sign sanity check.

Answer and explanation without JavaScript

  • Cash held by a firm has an opportunity cost: shareholders could invest it themselves.
  • NPV depends on forecast cash flows and the appropriate cost of capital.
  • NPV(A+B) = NPV(A) + NPV(B) when relevant cash flows are additive.
  • Watch out: slide 6 percentages are criterion-use figures, not investment returns.

Test accounting and payback shortcuts

Compare three simpler decision rules with NPV, using the lecturer’s project cash-flow tables.

Recognise the limits of accounting return

A project reports a 20% return on paper. Does that mean investors actually earn 20%?

Measure reported profit against reported assets

The Book Rate of Return, also called the Accounting Rate of Return, compares a project's average accounting income with the average value of its assets in the accounts over its life. It sounds like a percentage return on an investment, but it measures reported accounting performance rather than what shareholders could earn from investing their money elsewhere.

Book Rate of Return = average book incomeaverage book assets

Slide 8 prints the shorter fraction book income / book assets; its bullet clarifies that both quantities are averages over the project life. The numerator is an earnings amount per period; the denominator is the asset's accounting carrying amount. Use matching currency units, a nonzero average asset value and a consistent averaging convention. Dividing the two gives a rate: multiply by 100 to express it as a percentage.

Quick check: What goes in the denominator of this lecture's book-return formula?

Answer and reason without JavaScript

Average book assets over the project life. These are recorded accounting values, not market valuations.

Calculate it, then spot what it leaves out

Think of accounting earnings as the profit the financial statements report after their expense rules. The asset book value reflects recorded values and accounting treatment, including depreciation. As the slide warns, tax and accounting choices can affect both the reported income and the asset values. The fraction does not say when the project pays cash or how much risk shareholders bear.

ILLUSTRATIVE worked example: three-year project

  1. GIVEN. Over three years, accounting incomes are $12,000, $18,000 and $24,000. The corresponding annual book asset values are $120,000, $90,000 and $60,000. These figures are invented for practice; slide 8 gives no numerical example.
  2. Normalize. Use three annual observations for both averages. All figures are in dollars; the quotient will be a dimensionless annual accounting rate, because the numerator is average annual income and the denominator is average asset value.
  3. Average income. ($12,000 + $18,000 + $24,000) ÷ 3 = $54,000 ÷ 3 = $18,000 per year.
  4. Average assets. ($120,000 + $90,000 + $60,000) ÷ 3 = $270,000 ÷ 3 = $90,000.
  5. Substitute. Book Rate of Return = $18,000 ÷ $90,000 = 0.20 = 20% per year.
  6. Interpret and check. Annual accounting income is one-fifth of the average carrying value; 20% × $90,000 = $18,000. That checks the arithmetic, but says nothing on its own about the project's NPV or actual cash return.

Your turn: change the accounting asset value

ILLUSTRATIVE variation. Keep average reported annual income at $18,000, but suppose the average recorded asset value is $120,000 instead of $90,000 (holding income fixed to isolate the denominator). What book rate of return, in percent, results?

Hint and full worked solution

Hint: Higher book assets in the denominator lower the rate if reported income stays unchanged.

  1. Average book income = $18,000 per year; average book assets = $120,000.
  2. Divide with matching dollar units: $18,000 ÷ $120,000 = 0.15.
  3. Convert to percentage: 0.15 × 100 = 15%.
  4. Sanity check: 15% × $120,000 = $18,000; the rate fell by 5 percentage points from 20%. That does not, by itself, prove the project's cash value changed.

Quick check: Why can book return mislead a project decision?

Answer and reason without JavaScript

Book return depends on tax and accounting values rather than market values or the timing of actual cash flows; therefore it is not a substitute for an NPV calculation.

Remember
  • Book rate of return = average book income ÷ average book assets.
  • The same metric is called Accounting Rate of Return.
  • Accounting and tax treatments affect its input numbers.
  • Watch out: a 20% book return is not proof of a 20% cash return or positive NPV.

Compare payback with NPV

Would you reject a project worth +$2,624 just because its big payoff arrives in year 3?

Find when the initial cash is recovered

The payback period is the number of years until a project's cumulative cash flows recover its initial investment. The payback rule accepts projects whose payback is within a manager-chosen cutoff. On slide 9, that cutoff is deliberately left open with “? tbd!”; it does not come from a valuation formula.

Payback simply adds actual cash amounts in calendar order. It does not discount them. This creates two problems highlighted by the lecturer: it ignores all cash flows after the cutoff date, and gives equal weight to cash received at different dates before that cutoff.

Quick check: With a two-year cutoff, how does payback treat a large receipt in year 3?

Answer and reasoning without JavaScript

The later receipt does not change whether the project pays back within two years. The payback rule can therefore reject a valuable project with delayed benefits.

Three projects expose the problem

Slides 10–11 use the same initial cost, $2,000, for Projects A, B and C, with cash flows at the end of years 1–3. The lecturer sets the opportunity cost of capital (CoC) at 10% per year and a two-year payback cutoff. For the slide's whole-year results, payback is recorded at the first year-end when cumulative receipts cover the cost.

Lecturer’s worked example: payback against NPV

  1. GIVEN ($, dated cash flows). Each has C0 = −2,000 at t = 0. A: C1 = 500, C2 = 500, C3 = 5,000. B: 500, 1,800, 0. C: 1,800, 500, 0.
  2. Normalize. The discount rate is r = 10% = 0.10 per year. Cash flows are in dollars, received at year-end. For payback, do not discount; for NPV, calculate C0 + C1/1.10 + C2/1.10² + C3/1.10³.
  3. A payback. Receipts accumulated: after year 1, $500; after year 2, $1,000; after year 3, $6,000. First recovery is year 3, so the two-year rule rejects A.
  4. A NPV. −2,000 + 500/1.10 + 500/1.21 + 5,000/1.331 = −2,000 + 454.55 + 413.22 + 3,756.57 = +$2,624.34. NPV says accept.
  5. B payback. After year 1, $500; after year 2, $2,300. Payback = 2 years. B NPV = −2,000 + 500/1.10 + 1,800/1.21 = −2,000 + 454.55 + 1,487.60 = −$57.85. Payback says accept, but NPV says reject.
  6. C payback. After year 1, $1,800; after year 2, $2,300. Payback = 2 years. C NPV = −2,000 + 1,800/1.10 + 500/1.21 = −2,000 + 1,636.36 + 413.22 = +$49.59. Both rules accept.
  7. Interpret and check. The slide rounds NPVs to A +$2,624, B −$58, C +$50. A has the largest positive NPV, while B destroys value. A's large year-3 inflow drives its high NPV; B's late timing makes its discounted receipts insufficient to cover $2,000.

Hint: Payback ignores rates; NPV does not. Use 1.20 and 1.20² = 1.44 as discount factors.

  1. Rate = 20% = 0.20 per year.

Calculate discounted payback from a cash-flow table

Project B recovers $2,300 on paper. Why does discounted payback say it never pays back?

Discount each receipt before you count it

Ordinary payback adds future receipts as though a dollar received tomorrow were worth a dollar today. Discounted payback fixes that timing problem: first convert each future inflow into its value today, then find the first year in which the accumulated discounted inflows cover the original cash outlay. If the project never covers the cost, report no discounted payback, not zero years.

Discounted CF at year t = Ct / (1 + r)t

Here Ct is the cash flow received at the end of year t, while r is the annual opportunity cost of capital in decimal form. A time-zero cost, C0, is already measured today and is not discounted. The slide assumes annual year-end receipts and a constant 10% annual rate (r = 0.10). Keep the money units consistent, and compare accumulated present values against the $2,000 initial cost.

Quick check: At 10%, what happens to a $500 inflow received in year 2?

Answer and worked explanation without JavaScript

Divide $500 by (1.10)² = 1.21. The result is $413.22 of present value, or $413 rounded as on slide 12.

Calculate IRR, then recognise its traps

Learn what IRR measures, when its decision rule works, and why borrowing, signs, scale and discount-rate patterns matter.

Calculate IRR from project cash flows

Tool A costs $4,000 and pays back $6,000 in total. What rate makes its NPV exactly zero?

Find the break-even discount rate

When a question gives cash flows but asks for a rate, use internal rate of return (IRR): the discount rate making NPV = 0. Unlike NPV, which calculates dollar value at a given rate, IRR solves for the unknown rate.

For a single investment at t = 0 followed by just one payoff at t = 1, slide 13 writes the return as payoff/investment − 1, equivalent to the rate that solves NPV = 0:

0 = C0 + C1/(1 + IRR)　⇒　IRR = C1/(−C0) − 1

Here C0 is a negative initial cost and C1 a positive year-1 receipt, both in dollars. For later-year payoffs, use slide 14’s complete equation, similar to finding a bond’s yield to maturity:

0 = C0 + C1/(1 + IRR) + C2/(1 + IRR)2 + … + CT/(1 + IRR)T

The exponent t counts periods; assume year-end payments and one annual IRR greater than −100%. For one initial outflow followed by inflows, the zero-NPV crossing is unique.

Quick check: A project lists its dated cash flows but does not give a discount rate. What must you solve for to report IRR?

Answer and reasoning without JavaScript

IRR is the rate making the sum of discounted project cash flows exactly zero. It is not the total cash profit or a payback year.

Work through Tool A with the lecturer’s numbers

Tool A costs $4,000 today and has two dated receipts, so the one-payoff shortcut does not apply. Slide 15 reports IRR = 28.08% and Excel's =IRR(A1:C1) with chronological cash-flow cells.

Lecturer example: Tool A, every step visible

  1. GIVEN (cash flows, dollars): C0 = −$4,000 at t = 0; C1 = +$2,000 at t = 1 year; C2 = +$4,000 at t = 2 years. The unknown is the constant annual IRR.
  2. Normalize periods and units. Use years, not months; all dollar amounts have matching units. Write the rate as a decimal r first, then convert to a percentage at the end.
  3. Substitute: 0 = −4,000 + 2,000/(1 + r) + 4,000/(1 + r)2. The year-2 term has a square because it arrives one year later than the first receipt.
  4. Teaching bridge: isolate the rate. Let x = 1 + r, where x > 0. Multiply the entire equation by x2: 0 = −4,000x2 + 2,000x + 4,000. Divide by −2,000 to get 2x2 − x − 2 = 0.
  5. Solve for the admissible root. x = (1 + √17)/4 ≈ 1.280776406. Therefore r = x − 1 ≈ 0.280776406 = 28.0776406% ≈ 28.08% per year. The other algebraic root has x < 0 and falls outside r > −100%.
  6. Substitute back and check. NPV ≈ −4,000 + 2,000/1.280776406 + 4,000/(1.280776406)2 ≈ $0.00. That zero is the defining condition.
  7. Interpretation. Tool A's break-even annual discount rate is about 28.08%, not a $2,000 profit rate. The value-based accept/reject comparison with the required return comes in the next topic.

Your turn: increase the final payoff

ILLUSTRATIVE variation. Keep the $4,000 initial cost and $2,000 year-1 inflow, but raise the year-2 inflow from $4,000 to $5,000. What is the new annual IRR, to two decimal places?

Hint and full worked solution

Hint: Set NPV to zero, but replace the year-2 amount with $5,000. Keep the year-2 discount exponent at 2.

  1. 0 = −4,000 + 2,000/(1+r) + 5,000/(1+r)2; write x = 1 + r > 0.
  2. Multiply by x2: 0 = −4,000x2 + 2,000x + 5,000, so 4x2 − 2x − 5 = 0.
  3. The admissible root is x = (1 + √21)/4 ≈ 1.395643924, giving r ≈ 0.395643924 = 39.56% per year.
  4. Substituting back gives NPV ≈ $0.00. Increasing a later receipt raises the break-even rate from 28.08% to 39.56%.
Remember
  • IRR is the annual discount rate that makes the full dated-cash-flow NPV equal to zero.
  • A single-payoff project has IRR = C1/(−C0) − 1; with several periods, solve the full discounted equation.
  • Tool A's $4,000 cost and $2,000/$4,000 inflows give IRR ≈ 28.08%; Excel: =IRR(A1:C1).
  • Watch out: IRR is a rate, not NPV in dollars; do not forget the second-year exponent.

Apply the IRR acceptance rule on the NPV curve

Tool A earns an IRR of 28.08%. Should you accept it if investors demand 30%?

Find the rate where NPV changes sign

When a problem gives both a project's IRR and an opportunity cost of capital, compare these rates to decide whether to invest. When it instead supplies a required rate and asks how many dollars a project creates, calculate NPV. IRR is a percentage; NPV is money. Confusing them is a classic exam trap.

For the lecturer's conventional investment, an outflow now followed by positive cash receipts, a higher required return reduces the value of those later receipts. Thus the NPV curve slopes down. Where that curve crosses zero is the IRR. At that exact rate, the discounted cash received equals the amount originally invested.

r < IRR ⇒ NPV > 0 ⇒ accept

To the left of the crossing, the required return is below IRR and NPV is positive. To the right, r exceeds IRR and NPV is negative, so reject. At r = IRR, NPV = 0. This sign pattern applies to this conventional cash-flow stream; the lecturer's later pitfalls explain exceptions.

Distinguish lending from borrowing in the IRR rule

Both projects have a 50% IRR. Would you ever choose to pay 50% interest?

First, read the direction of the money

When an exam asks whether an IRR is attractive, check the time-zero cash flow first. Lending starts with cash leaving your pocket and a larger receipt later. Borrowing starts with cash arriving, followed by a repayment. The internal rate of return (IRR) still makes NPV equal zero, but the same rate can be a benefit to a lender and a cost to a borrower.

Use NPV to value these opposite cash flows at a given opportunity cost of capital. Both projects in slide 18 pay or receive dollars at time zero and one year later, so the equations are:

NPVA(r) = −1,000 + 1,500/(1+r)
NPVB(r) = +1,000 − 1,500/(1+r)

The rate r is annual, entered as a decimal and greater than −100%; C0 happens now and C1 one year later. Discount the year-1 amount once. As r rises, lender A's positive future receipt shrinks in present value, but borrower B's negative repayment becomes less costly. So A's NPV falls and B's NPV rises.

Recognise when one required return is not enough

Slide 25 calls this IRR Pitfall 4: More Than One Cost of Capital. When early and late cash flows need different required rates, use NPV with each date's correct discount factor, not a single-rate IRR comparison. The term structure describes those changing rates.

The lecturer displays the ordinary constant-rate formula. It works when the appropriate discount rate r is the same every year:

Tell soft from hard capital rationing

Two projects create value, but the company can afford only one. Who is stopping the other?

Recognise a funding limit, not a bad project

Capital rationing is a limit on the amount of funds available for investment. It appears when the company has more potential projects than the budget permits. A project can have positive net present value (NPV) and still go unfunded: a cash ceiling is different from a negative value calculation.

The exam clue is a fixed investment budget and several projects competing for it. First check whether the proposed investment set fits the available funds. Then compare the value created by feasible choices. Do not simply accept every positive-NPV proposal if their combined upfront costs exceed the stated limit. The next topic introduces the lecturer’s profitability-index method for limited resources.

Find out where the limit comes from

Slide 28 distinguishes two reasons. Soft rationing is a spending limit imposed internally by management, such as deciding not to approve more than a particular amount this year. Hard rationing happens when the firm cannot obtain the extra funding in capital markets. The distinction is who or what restricts financing, not whether the proposed projects are profitable.
