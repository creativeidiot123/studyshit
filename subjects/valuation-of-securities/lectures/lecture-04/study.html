Part 1: Read the equity market

Start with market information, the way stocks trade, and the financial inputs used to compare them.

Read market indices and sectors

The S&P 500 fell 25.61 points. Is that a crash, or barely a wobble?

Start wide, then zoom in

To assess corporate performance, the lecture first opens the Financial Times market dashboard. An equity index tracks a group of stocks, so it summarizes a market rather than the price of one company. Choose a region, then compare indices and sectors before opening an individual stock. The dashboard offers equities, currencies, commodities, bonds, funds and ETFs, plus watchlists.

Read the latest level, today's change, 52-week range and session separately. The five-day FTSE 100 line rises 1.63% even though its daily quote falls 0.14%; a different time window can tell a different story.

Translate points into a percentage

Use a percentage change when the question compares movements of indices with different starting levels. Use point changes only for movement in the same index units; neither is automatically an investor's total return.

Previous level = current level − signed point change.
Daily change (%) = (signed point change ÷ previous level) × 100.

Both levels and the signed change are in index points; the resulting percentage is dimensionless, for the same session relative to the previous close. Previous level must be positive.

Worked example · S&P 500 (L005–L006)
  1. GIVEN: last level 6,370.17 points; today's signed change −25.61 points, historical 22 Aug 2025 snapshot.
  2. Same index and daily period; do not use the one-year change or the 52-week extremes.
  3. Previous close = 6,370.17 − (−25.61) = 6,395.78 points.
  4. Daily change = (−25.61 ÷ 6,395.78) × 100 = -0.40042%.
  5. Rounded: −0.40%, matching the slide.
  6. Sanity check: 25.61 is small relative to roughly 6,400; negative direction and less than 1% magnitude make sense.

Distinguish stock issuance, trading and funds

When you buy a share, does the company actually receive your money?

Follow the money

Common stock is an ownership share in a publicly held corporation. The primary market sells newly issued shares to investors, either at an initial public offering (IPO), when the company first goes public, or at a subsequent issuance. The company receives the new-issue proceeds before issuance costs.

In the secondary market, investors trade previously issued shares. The seller receives the purchase price, not the company. That distinction matters when assessing whether a transaction finances the business or simply transfers ownership.

Worked scenario (ILLUSTRATIVE)
  1. A listed company issues additional newly created shares to finance expansion.
  2. Investors subscribe; after issuance costs, the business receives proceeds. This is primary-market activity.
  3. One investor later sells their shares to another. The existing investor receives the price, so that trade is secondary-market activity.

The slide calls new follow-on issues “secondary offerings.” That phrase can also describe sales of existing shares; classify by whether the shares are newly issued or already outstanding.

Quick check: You buy an existing share from another investor on an exchange. Who receives the purchase price?

Answer and why

The selling investor receives the price. No new share is being issued.

Where shares trade

The lecture distinguishes organized exchanges, such as NYSE (New York Stock Exchange), Nasdaq and LSE (London Stock Exchange), from over-the-counter (OTC) trading, which occurs outside a centralized exchange. Dow Jones is listed among the key terms; distinguish the Dow Jones stock index from a trading venue.

Buy a portfolio in one trade

An exchange-traded fund (ETF) lets investors buy or sell a portfolio in a single trade. The lecture highlights passive index tracking and providers BlackRock, Vanguard and State Street. SPDRs (Standard & Poor's Depository Receipts, “spiders”) track several S&P indices. Closed-end mutual funds also hold portfolios of securities; the slide emphasizes active management for these funds.

The passive-versus-active distinction describes the lecturer's typical cases, not a strict rule: ETFs can also be actively managed.

Your turn: You want exposure to a group of index constituents in one trade rather than buying each stock separately. Which lecture instrument fits?

Answer and why

An index ETF combines the securities in a fund portfolio.

Read a company stock quote

A Netflix share cost far more than a NVIDIA share in the lecture snapshot. Does that make Netflix the more valuable company?

Find the actual security

After comparing indices, use the FT Equities Screener to select a company. A ticker identifies the listed security; open its summary and chart, then the financial statements when you need operating information. The lecture highlights Netflix and the Magnificent Seven: Meta, NVIDIA, Microsoft, Amazon, Apple, Tesla and Alphabet.

Read the NVIDIA quote

Market capitalization measures the market value of all outstanding shares, whereas a quote is the price of one share. A higher stock price alone does not identify the most valuable company. The day range and 52-week range give historical extremes; volume counts traded shares and can be compared with average activity.

The lecture calls beta “volatility.” Beta measures sensitivity to benchmark-market returns rather than total stock volatility; a beta of 2.14 is not a volatility of 214%.

Turn price into a valuation ratio

Earnings per share (EPS) expresses earnings per share of stock. TTM means trailing twelve months. To compare the price with those earnings, use the price-to-earnings ratio (P/E), not the dividend yield. A positive EPS makes the ordinary positive P/E interpretation meaningful.

P/E = share price ÷ earnings per share. Market capitalization = share price × outstanding shares (not traded volume).

Worked example · NVIDIA (L015)
  1. GIVEN: price P = $174.98/share, annual trailing EPS = $3.10/share, snapshot 22 Aug 2025.
  2. Both dollar-per-share units cancel. Use the same quoted share basis, not market capitalization or trading volume.
  3. P/E = 174.98 ÷ 3.10 = 56.445161.
  4. Round to two decimals: 56.45×, as shown in the lecture. Investors pay about $56.45 per dollar of trailing annual earnings per share.
  5. Sanity check: 3.10 × 56 = 173.60, close to the quote. P/E is not a percentage.

Your turn: Using the quoted $0.04 annual dividend and $174.98 share price, find the dividend yield as a percentage, rounded to two decimals.

Hint and worked answer

Dividend yield = dividend/share ÷ price/share × 100 = 0.04 ÷ 174.98 × 100 = 0.022860%, or 0.02%.

Read company financials and distinguish book from market value

Netflix earned billions, but how much of its sales actually became operating profit, and is that what its shares are worth?

Read earnings before interpreting the price

The FT Financials tab separates the income statement from cash flow and the balance sheet. Revenue records sales; operating income is the profit remaining after operating expenses, before financing and tax effects. Net income is the bottom-line earnings measure, not necessarily cash received. In the overview, Netflix's annual revenue rose from $33.72bn to $39.00bn (15.65%), and net income rose from $5.41bn to $8.71bn (slide: 61.09%).

Use operating margin when asked what proportion of sales becomes operating profit. Use revenue growth when asked how sales changed across periods. These answer different questions.

Operating income = revenue − total operating expenses.
Operating margin (%) = (operating income ÷ revenue) × 100.
Revenue growth (%) = [(new revenue − prior revenue) ÷ prior revenue] × 100.

Worked calculation · Netflix FY2024 (L011; teaching bridge from the source figures)
  1. GIVEN: revenue 39,001, total operating expenses 28,583, prior-year revenue 33,723; all USD millions.
  2. Keep the same annual periods and currency scale. Positive revenue is the denominator for margin; positive prior-year revenue is the base for growth.
  3. Operating income = 39,001 − 28,583 = 10,418 (USD millions), matching the printed line.
  4. Operating margin = 10,418 ÷ 39,001 × 100 = 26.71214%, or 26.71%.
  5. Revenue increased by 39,001 − 33,723 = 5,278 million; growth = 5,278 ÷ 33,723 × 100 = 15.65104%, or 15.65%.
  6. Interpretation: about $26.71 of each $100 of FY2024 revenue became operating income. Both ratios have sensible magnitudes; the operating profit is smaller than revenue, and growth is positive.

Your turn: Compute FY2023 operating margin using 6,954 million operating income and 33,723 million revenue, rounded to two decimals.

Hint and worked answer

6,954 ÷ 33,723 × 100 = 20.62094%, or 20.62%.

Read the cash and return measures

The overview separates profitability, returns, cash movements and balance-sheet data. Cash generated by operations differs from cash used for investment or financing. A positive profit does not guarantee a positive change in cash.

The rounded net-income endpoints imply 61.00% growth versus the slide's 61.09%; unrounded figures may explain the difference.

Separate accounting value from market value

Book value is accounting net worth (assets less liabilities); its measurement depends on accounting standards such as IFRS and US GAAP (slide spells US-GAPP). A market-value balance sheet instead uses market values for assets and liabilities. A dividend is a periodic cash distribution to shareholders. For the price-to-earnings ratio, recall the share-price calculation.

The slide associates book value with liquidation value. They are not interchangeable: actual liquidation proceeds can differ from accounting carrying amounts.

Quick check: Is the book value of equity automatically equal to the value investors place on its shares?

Answer and why

No. Market prices can reflect expectations not captured by accounting net worth.

Compare companies using trading multiples

If Coca-Cola and PepsiCo sell similar products, should their shares have similar prices or similar valuation ratios?

Choose the valuation route

Comparables valuation (pricing using trading multiples) estimates a target's indicative value from similar listed companies. Intrinsic valuation instead estimates value from the target's expected future cash flows: either the dividend discount method or enterprise discounted cash flow. The lecture treats peer pricing as a starting point, not proof of intrinsic value.

Choose peers with similar businesses and risk; then compare ratios that use the same earnings or book-value basis. The share prices themselves can differ because companies have different numbers of shares.

Compare like with like

Recall P/E from the NVIDIA quote. The price-to-book ratio (P/B) compares share price with accounting equity per share. Use a peer P/E when valuing the target from earnings per share; use peer P/B when valuing from book equity per share. Positive denominators, consistent periods and comparable accounting bases are needed for the ordinary positive-multiple interpretation.

P/E = P ÷ EPS; P/B = P ÷ BPS.
Indicative P = peer P/E × target EPS, or peer P/B × target BPS.

P, EPS and BPS (book equity per share) are in dollars per share in the source. The ratios are unitless; multiplying a ratio by the corresponding per-share measure gives dollars per share.

Worked example · Coca-Cola versus PepsiCo (L019; teaching bridge using lecture data)
  1. GIVEN: Coca-Cola's share price P = $55, P/E = 24×; PepsiCo's P/E = 23.1×. Assume the earnings measures are comparable.
  2. Rearrange P/E = P ÷ EPS by multiplying both sides by EPS and dividing by P/E: EPS = P ÷ (P/E).
  3. Target EPS = 55 ÷ 24 = $2.291667/share (keep full precision).
  4. Indicative target share price = 23.1 × (55 ÷ 24) = $52.9375, rounded to $52.94/share.
  5. Interpretation: peer pricing gives a slightly lower indicative price than $55 because PepsiCo's P/E is slightly lower than 24×. It is not a guaranteed market price.

Your turn: Use Coca-Cola's $55 price and P/B 12.4×, and PepsiCo's P/B 12.8×, to estimate Coca-Cola's price using the book-value method. Round to cents.

Hint and worked answer

Target BPS = 55 ÷ 12.4 = $4.435484. Indicative P = 12.8 × (55 ÷ 12.4) = $56.774194, rounded to $56.77. A higher peer P/B gives a higher indicative price under this method.

Interpret the comparison

Bank of America's ratios are near its bank peers. Apache trades at a much lower earnings multiple than its oil-and-gas peers, while its book multiple is closer to theirs. Such gaps invite questions about expected earnings, growth, risk or accounting differences. A cheap-looking multiple alone does not establish undervaluation.

Quick check: You have a target's EPS and comparable companies' P/E ratios. What is the correct way to estimate its share price?

Answer and why

Peer P/E × target EPS, provided earnings bases and peer risk are comparable.

Part 2: Value a stock using dividends

Build the dividend discount method from the one-period investment to the long-horizon and constant-growth models.

Price a one-period stock investment

You pay $100 today and expect $115 next year. What return are you earning, and what is that future money actually worth now?

Calculate the return from owning a share

A shareholder can earn money through dividends and through selling the share for more than they paid. The expected return is the forecast percentage gain over the holding period, including both sources. Use the return equation when today's price is known and the unknown is the forecast return.

r = (Div1 + P1 − P0) / P0.

P0 is today's price, P1 the forecast sale price at the end of the period, and Div1 the expected end-period dividend (all USD/share). r is a decimal rate per holding period. P0 must be positive; forecasts are not guaranteed outcomes.

Worked example A · forecast return (L023)
  1. GIVEN: P0 = $100/share; P1 = $110/share; Div1 = $5/share paid in one year.
  2. All amounts are USD/share and refer to the same one-year holding period.
  3. Capital gain = 110 − 100 = $10/share; combined gain = 5 + 10 = $15/share.
  4. r = (5 + 110 − 100) / 100 = 15 / 100 = 0.15.
  5. Convert decimal to percent: 15% expected annual return. This is 5% dividend yield plus 10% capital gain relative to the initial price.
  6. Check: $15 is 15% of the $100 invested.

Reverse the equation to calculate today's value

Use the one-period valuation formula when the return required for the risk is given and today's fair price is unknown. Present value is what a future receipt is worth today.

rP0 = Div1 + P1 − P0
(1+r)P0 = Div1 + P1
P0 = (Div1 + P1) / (1+r).

The first two lines are an algebraic teaching bridge: collect the price terms on one side, then divide by the one-period discount factor (1+r), which must be positive.

Worked example B · reverse price (L025)
  1. GIVEN: expected dividend = $5, sale price = $110; r = 15% per year.
  2. Normalize r = 0.15; both receipts arrive at t = 1.
  3. P0 = (5 + 110) / (1 + 0.15) = 115 / 1.15.
  4. Fair price = $100.00/share, matching the lecture.
  5. Check: $100 × 1.15 = $115, exactly the forecast receipt.

Your turn (ILLUSTRATIVE): Same forecast $5 dividend and $110 sale price, but the annual required return rises to 20%. Find today's fair price to cents.

Hint and worked answer

P₀ = 115 / 1.20 = 95.833333, rounded to $95.83. A higher required return lowers the value of unchanged future receipts; the increase from 15% to 20% is 5 percentage points.

Why the discount rate matters

In the equity approach, r is also called the market capitalization rate, cost of equity capital (the return investors require for the risk), opportunity cost or discount rate. Under the lecture's equilibrium assumption, equivalent-risk securities offer the same expected return. If a share is underpriced relative to its forecast cash flows, buying pressure pushes up its price; overpricing gives the opposite incentive.

Looking beyond a one-year holding period, the lecture states the general dividend valuation principle:

P0 = Σt=1∞ [Divt / (1+r)t].

Divt is expected dividend per share in future period t; the sum must converge. This does not assume investors only care about dividends: the future resale price reflects subsequent dividends. The next finite-horizon derivation explains the link.

Slide 21 says “share value or firm value.” Discounting dividends per share at the cost of equity produces equity value per share, not total enterprise value.

Quick check: If forecast cash flows are unchanged but the required return rises, what happens to today's fair price?

Answer and why

Falls: a higher required return discounts unchanged positive future receipts more heavily.

Extend stock valuation to two or more periods

If you hold Fledgling for two years rather than one, should its fair price today change?

Replace the future price with its own cash flows

Recall the one-period pricing formula. A year-1 buyer would value the dividend and sale price expected at year 2, just as you value the year-1 receipts today. If a question specifies cash flows at several dates, use the multi-period model rather than discounting every receipt by one year.

P1 = (Div2 + P2) / (1+r).

Here Pt is the share price at date t (after that date's dividend), Divt the end-period dividend, both USD/share; r is the constant required return per year, in decimal form, with 1+r > 0.

Slide 27 derivation
  1. Start with P0 = (Div1 + P1) / (1+r).
  2. Replace P1 with its own valuation: P0 = [Div1 + (Div2 + P2)/(1+r)]/(1+r).
  3. Distribute the outer division across both terms:
    P0 = Div1/(1+r) + (Div2 + P2)/(1+r)2.

The second receipt is discounted twice because it arrives two years from today.

Calculate Fledgling's price in two ways

Worked example · slides 28 and 30
  1. GIVEN: Div₁=$5.00, Div₂=$5.50, P₂=$121, r=15% annually; dividends and sale occur at year-end.
  2. Normalize r=0.15, so 1+r=1.15 and (1+r)²=1.3225.
  3. Calculate next year's price: P₁=(5.50+121)/1.15=126.50/1.15=$110.00.
  4. One-year method: P₀=(5+110)/1.15=115/1.15=$100.00.
  5. Two-year method: P₀=5/1.15+(5.50+121)/1.3225=4.347826+95.652174=$100.00/share.
  6. Check: both methods give the same value, as in the lecture. $131.50 of undiscounted receipts over two years is worth less today at a positive discount rate.

Slide 28 labels “next year (t=2),” but its equation discounts year-2 receipts to P₁ at t=1. The source numbers and equation are retained.

Your turn (ILLUSTRATIVE): The forecast year-2 sale price drops to $110. Dividends and required return remain unchanged. Compute today's share price to cents.

Hint and worked answer

P₀=5/1.15+(5.50+110)/1.3225 = 4.347826+87.334594 = 91.682420, rounded to $91.68. The lower sale price reduces present value by 11/1.3225 dollars.

Generalize to any investment horizon

The horizon value (terminal or continuing value) PH is the forecast share price at the end of the H-period investment. Receive each dividend through H and sell at H. Combining the last dividend with the sale price is valid because they happen at the same time.

P0 = Div1/(1+r)1 + Div2/(1+r)2 + … + (DivH+PH)/(1+r)H.
P0 = Σt=1H [Divt/(1+r)t] + PH/(1+r)H.

H is the number of periods, and t indexes each payment date. The summation counts dividend H already; add only the terminal price separately. The next topic explores how the resale price's present value shrinks as the horizon extends.

Quick check: In the two-year model, should the year-1 share price $110 also be included as a receipt?

Answer and why

No. The year-1 price represents the future cash flows already included by the two-year model.

  • Substitute the future price to extend the holding horizon.

See why distant sale prices matter less

Would a share worth over $1.37 million in 100 years make you rich today? Not necessarily: that future selling price contributes just $1.17 to the example's present value.

Separate today's value into two pieces

The Fledgling example keeps the first annual dividend at $5, annual dividend growth at 10% compounded, and annual required return at 15%. The finite-horizon model divides today's stock value into dividends received while holding it and the discounted price when selling it. In this example the forecast share price grows alongside dividends at 10% per year, starting from $100.

DivH = 5(1.10)H−1 for H ≥ 1;
PH = 100(1.10)H;
PV(dividends) = Σt=1H [5(1.10)t−1/(1.15)t];
PV(price) = PH/(1.15)H.

These are example-specific forecasts, not general rules for every stock. H is the holding horizon in years; all cash amounts are USD/share. Compare compound growth and discounting over matched annual periods.

Work out a distant sale today

Worked example · H = 100 years (L031–L032)
  1. GIVEN: initial price $100/share, Div₁=$5/share, r=15%/year, g=10%/year, H=100 years, year-end payments.
  2. Normalize r=0.15 and g=0.10. Compounding factors: 1.15 and 1.10.
  3. Div₁₀₀=5(1.10)⁹⁹=$62,639.15; P₁₀₀=100(1.10)¹⁰⁰=$1,378,061.23.
  4. PV(price)=100(1.10/1.15)¹⁰⁰=$1.173504, or $1.17/share.
  5. PV(dividends)=5/1.15 + 5.50/1.15² + … + 5(1.10)⁹⁹/1.15¹⁰⁰. Sum the 100 discounted dividends: $98.826496, or $98.83/share.
  6. Total PV=$98.826496+$1.173504=$100.00/share.
  7. Sanity check: 1.10/1.15 ≈ 0.956522 < 1, so each extra year reduces the discounted resale portion even while its nominal dollar price grows.

Your turn: Change the holding horizon from 100 to 20 years. Calculate only today's value of the future selling price, rounded to cents.

Hint and worked answer

P₂₀=100(1.10)²⁰=$672.75. Discount without intermediate rounding: 100(1.10/1.15)²⁰=$41.105212, or $41.11. Dividends account for the remaining $58.89.

Let the horizon go to infinity

At longer horizons, the present value of the dividends increases while the present value of the sale price shrinks. In the example both pieces still add to $100. Because the discount factor grows faster than the forecast share price, its discounted terminal contribution tends to zero. The dividend discount model (DDM) then values the indefinite stream of expected dividends.

P0 = Σt=1∞ [Divt/(1+r)t].

Use this infinite form only when the series converges and the discounted resale value tends to zero, rather than automatically deleting the sale price at a finite horizon. In constant positive-growth cases this requires the required return to exceed growth. As on slide 21, discounting dividends per share at cost of equity yields equity value per share, not enterprise value.

Quick check: In Fledgling's fixed 10% growth and 15% discount scenario, what changes when H increases from 20 to 50 years?

Answer and why

Dividend PV rises from $58.89 to $89.17, while sale PV falls from $41.11 to $10.83. Total stays $100.

Use the constant-growth dividend formula

What is a stock worth if its dividends grow forever at a steady rate? And why can a tiny change in the growth forecast make such a big difference?

Turn an infinite stream into one fraction

In the previous topic, we discounted dividends one at a time. If the dividend grows by the same percentage every year indefinitely, the constant dividend growth model (Gordon growth model) compresses that infinite sum into one formula for a growing perpetuity. Use it when the question specifies a constant perpetual growth rate and a next-period dividend, not when dividends follow several different growth stages.

P0 = Div1 / (r − g).

P0 is today's share value, Div1 is the expected dividend next period (USD/share), r is the per-period required equity return and g is the constant per-period dividend growth rate, both decimals. Assuming a positive dividend, constant rates and matching end-period payments, require r > g and 1+g > 0 for the usual finite positive value. If the problem gives the dividend just paid, Div0, first calculate Div1 = Div0(1+g).

Worked example · Fledgling (teaching bridge using L031 and L034)
  1. GIVEN: Div₁=$5/share, r=15% annually, g=10% annually, constant forever.
  2. Normalize rates: r=0.15 and g=0.10; the next annual dividend is already given.
  3. Spread r−g=0.15−0.10=0.05 (5 percentage points).
  4. P₀=5/(0.15−0.10)=5/0.05=$100.00/share.
  5. Check: 100 × 0.05 = $5; the result matches the original Fledgling horizon valuation.

Reverse the formula to estimate the required return

Use the reverse equation when the quoted price is known and the question asks for the cost of equity. The forward dividend yield is the forecast next dividend divided by today's price; the growth component adds the expected capital appreciation under the constant-growth assumption.

P0(r−g)=Div1

Understand sensitivity to growth

Holding the next dividend and required return fixed, higher growth narrows the denominator and raises today's value. The effect is nonlinear: as g approaches r from below, the model becomes extremely sensitive. By contrast, if the observed price and dividend are fixed, increasing g by one percentage point increases the inferred return by one percentage point.

Your turn (ILLUSTRATIVE): Increase perpetual growth from 10% to 11%, retaining the $5 next dividend and 15% required return. Find today's price.

Hint and worked answer

P₀=5/(0.15−0.11)=5/0.04=$125.00. A one-percentage-point growth increase raises value by $25, or 25% relative to the original $100.

Quick check: If a forecast assumes dividends grow at 16% forever and the required return is 15%, can the standard constant-growth formula give a valid positive finite price?

Answer and why

No. Discounted dividends fail to converge under those perpetual-growth assumptions.

L061 · Slide 61 · Enterprise DCF formula

Lecture names WACC in objectives/key terms but does not give WACC formula. For operating FCF the consistent enterprise discount rate is WACC (clarification for later teaching).

L066 · Slide 66 · Summary: PV equilibrium

Common stock value equals indefinite stream of dividend cash payments discounted at expected return on other securities with equivalent risk: PV=Σ[t=1…∞] Divₜ/(1+r)ᵗ. Starts from short investment horizons with dividends and capital gains, not a premise that investors only want dividends. P₀=(DIV₁+P₁)/(1+r) is equilibrium condition; if price over/under fair value, selling/buying pushes price to adjust.
