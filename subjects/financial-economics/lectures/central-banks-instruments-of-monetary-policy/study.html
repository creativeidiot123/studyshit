Topic 01 · slides 1–7

Why central banks move the economy

The ECB can change financing conditions without deciding which individual firm gets a loan.

Central-bank interest rates influence commercial-bank rates, credit, investment and growth. The lecture contrasts multiple-objective, inflation-targeting and exchange-rate-targeting central banks. Its opening agenda also flags floating, intermediate and fixed exchange-rate systems as comparison questions.

Lecture details (7 slides)
  • Slide 2: Agenda locates the lecture within Financial Economics: Microeconomics, Macroeconomics (highlighted), and Policies.
  • Slide 3: Macroeconomics agenda asks how monetary policy works with floating versus fixed exchange-rate systems, in the short term and the medium/long term; compares implications of floating, intermediate and fixed regimes. These are agenda questions, not answered on this slide.
  • Slide 4: Six lecture sections: Introduction; European Central Bank; ECB and Monetary Policy; ECB during the Financial Crisis; ECB after the Sovereign Debt Crisis (QE); ECB since 2022 amid rising inflation.
  • Slide 5: Identify traditional monetary-policy instruments; explain changes during the financial crisis, European sovereign debt/euro crisis, Covid crisis, and recent inflation; understand quantitative easing (QE).
  • Slide 6: Central banks determine monetary policy and key interest rates; commercial-bank rates follow; rates affect bank credit, investment, economic growth (monetary-policy transmission). Examples: ECB, FED (USA), BoJ (Japan), BoE (UK).
  • Slide 7: Three monetary-policy approaches: multiple objectives (low inflation, economic stability, low unemployment, growth, exchange-rate stability, fiscal coordination; FED example); price stability/inflation targeting (ECB example); exchange-rate targeting under fixed rates.

Topic 02 · slides 8–16

What the ECB is and who runs it

Who actually makes the euro area’s interest-rate decisions?

The ECB pursues price stability first, with other EU economic objectives subordinate to it. The Eurosystem and ESCB are not identical. The Governing Council decides monetary policy; the Executive Board prepares decisions, implements them and handles daily operations.

Lecture details (9 slides)
  • Slide 9: ECB/ESCB primary objective is price stability; without prejudice, support general EU economic policies (slide attributes to Article 282(2) TEU). Slide says ECB targets inflation below but close to 2%. Comparison: Federal Reserve statutory goals include maximum employment, stable prices, moderate long-term interest rates (U.S. Code §225a).
  • Slide 10: ECB defines/implements Eurozone monetary policy; conducts foreign-exchange operations; holds/manages currency reserves; promotes smooth payment systems; regulates/supervises Eurozone banks; responsibilities include banknotes, statistics, macroprudential policy, financial stability, international and European cooperation.
  • Slide 11: Eurosystem = ECB plus national central banks (NCBs) of monetary-union members; ESCB also includes NCBs of non-euro EU members. Governors of euro-area NCBs and ECB Executive Board form Governing Council; national central-bank governors of non-euro states are shown in General Council.
  • Slide 14: Governing Council is main ECB decision-making body; comprises six ECB Executive Board members and governors of NCBs of 21 euro-area member states; meets twice monthly, assesses economic and monetary developments; monetary decisions every six weeks; regular accounts published before next meeting.
  • Slide 16: Executive Board = president, vice president and four other members; prepares Governing Council meetings; implements Governing Council policy and instructs euro-area NCBs; handles day-to-day ECB business.

Topic 03 · slides 17–20

How ECB votes and independence work

Why don’t all 27 officeholders cast a vote on every decision?

The lecture shows 21 Governing Council voting rights: six fixed Executive Board votes plus 15 rotating NCB governor votes. It compares the Fed’s 12 FOMC votes and names institutional, personal, operational, financial and legal safeguards for ECB independence.

Lecture details (4 slides)
  • Slide 17: Until 2015 one vote per Governing Council member; from 2015 rotating voting for NCB governors. Executive Board members always vote. Ranked countries 1–5 (Germany, France, Italy, Spain, Netherlands on slide) share four rotating monthly votes; ranks 6–21 (remaining 16) share 11; one vote per available voting right.
  • Slide 18: ECB Governing Council diagram: 15 rotating NCB votes + six permanent Executive Board votes = 21 voting rights. US FED Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) diagram: five regional Reserve Bank voting rights + seven Board of Governors votes = 12. ECB diagram says 'Further 14 members share 11 votes'.
  • Slide 19: Institutional ECB independence: no instructions from EU institutions, member-state governments or other bodies. Personal independence: ECB Executive Board members have eight-year non-renewable terms and dismissal only for incapacity or serious misconduct.
  • Slide 20: Functional/operational independence: instruments and authority to choose how/when to use them; ECB prohibited from loans to EU bodies or national public-sector entities. Financial independence: own budget, capital subscribed and paid by euro-area NCBs. Legal independence: own legal personality, can apply to European Court of Justice.

Topic 04 · slides 21–26

How ordinary ECB monetary tools work

What stops an overnight bank loan rate from drifting anywhere it likes?

ECB open-market operations provide at least one-week liquidity; overnight lending and deposit facilities form an interest-rate corridor. Historically, MRO funding was allocated via auctions, with collateral, maturities and rates determining access.

Lecture details (6 slides)
  • Slide 21: Bank ↔ ECB schematic: ECB supplies euros/liquidity to bank; bank supplies interest plus eligible collateral subject to minimum collateral requirements.
  • Slide 22: ECB monetary-policy operations: open-market operations with maturities of one week or more; overnight standing facilities (marginal lending, deposit facility). Open-market operations, especially main refinancing operations (MROs), steer interest rates, manage liquidity and signal stance; standing facilities supply/absorb overnight liquidity and curb short-rate volatility.
  • Slide 23: Pre-crisis main refinancing operations (MROs): weekly auctions; ECB announced total one-week funds and minimum MRO interest rate; banks bid amounts and rates; highest interest-rate bids receive allotments first.
  • Slide 24: Marginal lending facility: banks borrow overnight from ECB at overnight lending rate. Deposit facility: banks deposit overnight at overnight deposit rate. Slide describes these as a corridor limiting EONIA interbank overnight rate: borrow from peers below ECB lending rate; lend to peers above ECB deposit rate.
  • Slide 25: ECB key rates time-series chart, Jan 1999 to roughly Jan 2027; vertical axis percentages approximately −1% to 6%; three series: overnight deposit (blue), MRO (red), overnight lending (green). Labels on slide: MRO 2.65%; overnight lending 2.90%; overnight deposit 2.50%. Rates fell near/below zero after crisis and rose sharply around 2022–23.
  • Slide 26: Interest-rate evolution comparing central banks. Left graphic (The Economist 06.09.2014) shows Federal Reserve, Bank of England, ECB, Bank of Japan policy-rate paths roughly 2000–2014. Right graphic (Financial Times 2022) shows tightening paths roughly 2007–2022, including Bank of England, US Fed, ECB and Bank of Canada.

Topic 05 · slides 27

How the financial crisis changed the toolkit

What can a central bank do when ordinary funding markets freeze?

The crisis response in the slides combines rate cuts, relaxed collateral standards, fixed-rate full allotment, longer-term refinancing (LTROs/TLTROs) and QE. The 2012 Draghi statement frames the euro-preservation commitment.

Lecture details (1 slide)
  • Slide 27: During financial crisis: lower ECB rates; looser minimum collateral requirements; QE; open-market changes to fixed-rate full allotment and longer maturities (LTROs, TLTROs). Mario Draghi, 2012: 'Within our mandate, the ECB is ready to do whatever it takes to preserve the euro. And believe me, it will be enough'.

Topic 06 · slides 28–29

Why QE exists and what the APP buys

If policy rates are already near zero, how can the ECB still loosen conditions?

The slides introduce asset purchases as QE and identify four APP components: ABSPP, CBPP3, CSPP and PSPP. Public-sector purchases are linked to ECB capital shares and a stated debt-holding limit.

Lecture details (2 slides)
  • Slide 28: When central-bank rate already at 0%, the slide argues policy cannot be further expanded by lower rates, so central banks buy government bonds, corporate bonds or asset-backed securities (ABS) to inject liquidity: quantitative easing (QE); examples ECB and FED after financial crisis.
  • Slide 29: Expanded Asset Purchase Programme (APP) listed as ECB QE since 01/2015: ABSPP (asset-backed securities), CBPP3 (covered bonds), CSPP (corporate sector), PSPP (public sector). PSPP buys by country shareholding in ECB; slide states ECB may own at most 33% of a country's debt.

Topic 07 · slides 30–34

How purchases reshape central-bank balance sheets

Does a negative monthly purchase mean the ECB never bought that bond?

Monthly NET purchase flows, APP holdings and total ECB/Fed balance-sheet assets are three different measures. The figures show large asset accumulation and later run-off, plus varied expansion paths across central banks.

Lecture details (5 slides)
  • Slide 30: Monthly NET APP purchases, horizontal axis 2015–2026, vertical EUR billions, roughly −60 to +90. Contributions PSPP (blue), CBPP3 (orange), CSPP (yellow), ABSPP (green), dotted average monthly APP target. Positive net purchases were high 2015–18, resumed 2020–22; negative net purchases dominate after 2023 (runoff). Source ECB (2026).
  • Slide 31: Cumulated APP purchases/holdings, horizontal axis 2015–2026, vertical EUR billions (0–3600); PSPP, CBPP3, CSPP and ABSPP stacked. Holdings grow strongly, reach peak around 2022–23, decline by 2026. Source ECB (2026).
  • Slide 32: ECB assets by colored components, 1999–2025 on horizontal axis; vertical axis ECB balance in EUR millions, up to 9,000,000. Sharp growth in financial crisis, post-2014 QE and 2020–21; balance-sheet peak near 2021 and contraction after. Source ECB (2026).
  • Slide 33: US FED assets, 2008–2026, vertical USD millions from 0 to about 8–9 million. Assets rise after 2008, rise again in 2020, peak near 2022 then generally decline. Source FED (2026).
  • Slide 34: The Economist chart (27.04.2017): comparative expansion of Bank of England, Federal Reserve, ECB, Bank of Japan central-bank balance sheets, indexed January 2007=100, periods 2007–2017. Some banks' balance sheets reach several times their 2007 levels.

Topic 08 · slides 35–37

What QE tries to achieve and why it is disputed

Can cheaper money help businesses and still create financial risks?

QE aims to support inflation, bank credit, investment and market stability. The lecture also raises default losses, asset bubbles, exchange-rate effects and moral hazard from anticipated secondary-market bond purchases.

Lecture details (3 slides)
  • Slide 35: ECB QE aims: stabilize inflation; improve company financing conditions; increase bank credit to real economy, stimulate investment and growth; reduce instability in risky, long-term and sovereign-bond markets.
  • Slide 36: Critiques: losses on large government-bond holdings in event of country default would ultimately concern ECB shareholders/euro-area members; cheap financing could encourage asset bubbles and instability; exchange-rate effects.
  • Slide 37: Moral hazard: ECB government-bond purchases on secondary market versus prohibited direct/primary-market financing. Initial private investor must take sovereign risk; anticipated ECB secondary buying may reduce perceived risk of buying new bonds and weaken market discipline.

Topic 09 · slides 38–40

How the ECB responded to the 2022 inflation surge

Why can a central bank raise rates while its balance sheet also shrinks?

The charts show an energy-heavy inflation spike in 2022 and easing thereafter. The policy response includes a 4.50% MRO peak in September 2023, ending APP/PEPP reinvestments, and no new long-term targeted refinancing operations.

Lecture details (3 slides)
  • Slide 38: Euro-area HICP inflation (year-on-year percent change) line chart, roughly 2019–2026; annual inflation dipped near/below zero around 2020, surged to roughly 10% in 2022, then fell toward approximately 2% by 2024–26. Source ECB.
  • Slide 39: European Commission Spring 2025 chart, 'Inflation breakdown, euro area': x-axis Jan-2021–Jan-2025; y-axis year-on-year change/contribution percentage points approximately −2 to 12. Categories energy, unprocessed food, non-energy industrial goods, processed food including alcohol & tobacco, services; thick black HICP inflation line. Energy contributes greatly to the 2022 peak; services remain positive later.
  • Slide 40: ECB tightening after 2022: MRO rose to 4.50% (09/2023); slide calls MRO 'currently 2.15%'; PEPP stops reinvesting redemptions since 12/2024; APP no reinvestment of redemptions from 07/2023; no new TLTROs or PELTROs; MROs retain fixed-rate full allotment; MRO maturity one week, LTRO maturity three months.
