1 · Understand first

The whole example, not a click marathon

A two-year bond has a €1,000 face value, pays €50 each year, and investors require a 6% annual yield. What price makes sense today?

  1. The year-one payment is €50, worth 50 / 1.06 = €47.17 today.
  2. At year two, you get the €50 coupon AND the €1,000 principal: 1,050 / (1.06)² = €934.50.
  3. Add the present values: €47.17 + €934.50 = €981.67 (rounded at the end).
  4. Because the bond's €50 coupon is below the €60 income you'd want from €1,000 at 6%, it trades below face value.

Every step stays on screen, with or without JavaScript.

2 · Finish it

Now do the meaningful bit

Same bond, but investors now require 5% annual yield. What is its new price? Calculate it before checking.

Hint: discount €50 for one year, then €1,050 for two years.

Show the full answer

50/1.05 + 1050/(1.05)² = 47.6190 + 952.3810 = €1,000.00.

A 5% coupon rate exactly equals the 5% required yield, so the bond sells at face value.

3 · Predict → explore → explain

Move the yield. Watch the price.

For a five-year bond with €50 annual coupons and €1,000 principal, what happens to the price when the required yield rises from 5% to 8%?

ILLUSTRATIVE five-year, annual-pay bond. The graph and price use the same present-value calculation.

At 5% required yield, the €50 coupon matches the required return on €1,000, so price equals face value.

Why does the curve slope downward?

When investors require a higher return, the bond's fixed future payments must be bought for less today. Every future cash flow is divided by a larger discount factor.

4 · Spot the mistake

Which calculation breaks the time-value rule?

Return to the two-year 6% bond. A student proposes these price formulas. Which is incorrect because it discounts the year-two cash flow for only one year?

See the reasoning

The second formula is wrong. The €1,050 comes in two years, so its discount factor must be (1.06)². Using just 1.06 overprices the bond by pretending year two is year one.
