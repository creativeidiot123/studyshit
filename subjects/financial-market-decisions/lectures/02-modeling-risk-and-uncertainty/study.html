Part I · Understand what risk means

Start with everyday risk, then learn when probabilities help and why definitions differ.

Identify risks in everyday decisions

A slippery staircase and a falling share price: what makes both situations risky?

1. Start outside the stock market

The lecturer begins with a question, not a ready-made formula: what is risk? Slide 3 asks you to find examples without restricting yourself to finance. Why? If your only example is losing money on shares, you might confuse risk in general with one particular financial loss. Consider a cyclist on wet pavement or a firm choosing a supplier. Both face consequences they would rather avoid, even though neither example needs a stock market.

ILLUSTRATIVE · Find the common thread
  1. Consider riding a bicycle on a wet road. Your goal is to arrive safely.
  2. An unwanted outcome would be falling and being injured.
  3. Now consider investing in a share. Your goal is to preserve or increase its value.
  4. An unwanted outcome would be the share losing value.
  5. Different activities, same starting question: what could happen that makes the outcome worse than desired?

Distinguish certainty, risk and uncertainty

Two decisions could go wrong, but only one has probabilities you can reasonably estimate. Which is risk?

1. Decisions meet external forces

A result depends on what the decision-maker does or does not do, plus external forces outside their control. Slide 10 lists sales, supplier, labor and capital markets; weather; changing legislation; central-bank decisions; and competitors. Even when you carefully choose an action, those forces can change the outcome. Start by asking both what you control and how much you know about what you cannot control.

Quick check: Which influence comes from outside the decision-maker?

Answer without JavaScript

Weather conditions. The other two are decisions or inaction by the decision-maker.

2. Frank Knight's three decision situations (1921)

Certainty means the result is fully determined by your activity with no external influence. Under risk, outside effects matter but can be modeled stochastically, using probabilities. Under uncertainty, there is no reasonable way to quantify the outside effect. Frank Knight (1885–1972) stressed the distinction between measurable risk and genuinely non-quantitative uncertainty; these are not simply different levels of danger.

ILLUSTRATIVE · Classify a company's decisions
  1. Certainty: a hypothetical action has exactly one guaranteed result and no outside effects.
  2. Risk: routine deliveries can arrive late, but reliable historical frequencies allow delay probabilities to be estimated.
  3. Uncertainty: an unprecedented law could transform the business, and its effects cannot reasonably be assigned probabilities.
  4. Test: Can external influences change the result? If yes, can you defensibly quantify their effects?

Quick check: Delivery delays have reliably estimable probabilities. The decision is under:

Answer without JavaScript

Risk. Outcomes vary, but probability modeling is reasonable.

3. See measurable risk in a probability curve

Slide 11 illustrates risk with normal probability distributions, describing how likelihood is spread across outcomes. The horizontal axis shows outcome x; the vertical axis shows probability density. The mean μ locates the center; variance σ² measures spread. More variance creates a wider, flatter curve, while changing μ shifts the curve. This illustrates a way of modeling risk, not a way to quantify every uncertainty.

Worked reading of the lecturer's four curves
  1. Blue, red and orange all have μ = 0, so they share a center.
  2. Their variances are 0.2, 1.0 and 5.0. Blue is narrowest; orange is widest.
  3. Calculated density peaks are approximately 0.892, 0.399 and 0.178, respectively. More spread lowers the peak.
  4. Green has μ = −2 and σ² = 0.5; it shifts left, with calculated peak density about 0.564.

Quick check: Which μ = 0 curve is widest?

Answer without JavaScript

Orange, because it has the largest variance.

Remember
  • Decisions depend on action, inaction and outside influences.
  • Certainty: determined outcome; risk: modelable probabilities; uncertainty: no reasonable quantification.
  • For a normal curve, μ shifts the center and σ² changes its spread.
  • Watch out: probability density is not the chance of a single exact outcome; uncertainty is not merely a tiny risk.

Compare competing definitions of risk

Can a firm lose an expected €2,000 payment without any balance-sheet asset losing value?

1. Why “loss of assets” is too narrow

Slide 17 first describes risk as potential loss through debasement of assets or enhancement of liabilities. In plain English, an asset could lose value or an amount owed could rise. That sounds measurable, but it misses future cash flows, money expected to come into or leave the business, when they are not tied to an existing asset or liability. The lecturer explicitly calls this definition too narrow: a future inflow can be threatened even before it appears on the balance sheet.

ILLUSTRATIVE · A payment not yet recorded
  1. A business expects a €2,000 service payment next month. Assume no receivable has been recognized yet.
  2. The customer might cancel; the future payment could instead be €0.
  3. Potential reduction in that cash inflow: €2,000 − €0 = €2,000.
  4. That exposure matters even though no already-recorded asset was written down.

Quick check: Why does the lecturer reject an asset-and-liability-only definition?

Answer without JavaScript

It can overlook cash flows not related to existing assets or liabilities, so it is too narrow.

2. Why “missing a target” is hard to compare

The slide also calls risk the threat of deviating from targets, such as sales or profit targets. This catches performance problems beyond balance-sheet losses. The catch: different firms choose different targets, making the benchmark subjective. If two firms report identical sales but set different goals, a target-based comparison can label one result a disappointment and the other a success. That is why the lecturer says this definition is very broad but unsuitable for straightforward comparison between entities.

Quick check: Firm A and B both sell €11,000. Which conclusion is supported?

Answer without JavaScript

Their target deviations can differ. Here A exceeds its target by €1,000; B misses by €1,000.

3. Make “unfavorable” more precise

Slide 18 says legislators and standard-setting institutions generally speak of the possibility of unfavorable future development. But what counts as “unfavorable”? The lecturer makes that vague phrase more concrete in two ways: deviations from expected values of assets, liabilities or expected cash flows; and expected negative developments in asset/liability values or future cash flows. These are the lecture's proposed specifications, not a mathematical formula. Notice that a deviation from an expectation and a negative development are not identical descriptions.

Quick check: Which addition makes “unfavorable” less vague?

Answer without JavaScript

Refer to deviations from expected asset/liability values or cash flows, and to expected negative developments in those values or flows.

Remember
  • Asset/liability loss alone misses some future cash-flow risks.
  • Target deviation captures more situations but depends on subjective goals.
  • Slide 18 specifies “unfavorable” using expected values, cash flows and negative developments.
  • Watch out: no single definition here is presented as universally precise or comparable.

Part II · Classify where risk comes from

Use the lecturer's financial-risk maps, then distinguish shocks outside a firm from failures inside it.

Classify business and financial risks

A computer outage and a currency shock can both cost money. Why do they belong to different risk categories?

1. Sort risks across the whole business

Slide 15 groups entrepreneurial risk, threats affecting the firm's activities and results, into four categories. Operational risk concerns the way work gets done. Strategic risk concerns a firm's direction and outside business environment. Financial risk concerns prices, credit and liquidity; compliance risk concerns laws, regulation and contracts. The diagram puts operational risk closer to the short term, strategic risk closer to the long term, and financial risk across much of the time horizon.

Quick check: An employee accidentally shuts down the order system. What is the main source of risk?

Answer without JavaScript

Operational risk: employee action and technology disrupt a process.

2. Unpack financial risk one level deeper

Slide 16 splits financial risk into credit risk, where an obligation or counterparty becomes less reliable or valuable; market price risk, where prices or rates move; and liquidity risk, where trading or exiting a position is difficult. Its branches are not interchangeable. In particular, the slide puts Creditworthiness Risk within its interest-rate subtree and links market-price exposure from derivatives to all four market-price categories.

Quick check: Where does FX Risk belong?

Answer without JavaScript

Market Price Risk.

3. Use the source of a problem to classify it

The entrepreneurial-risk picture also names financial markets, banks, insurance companies, external partners, politics, management and corporate culture. These are surrounding influences or actors, not extra top-level categories. A firm's exposure can cross categories, so identify the immediate source first, then consider the knock-on effects. A classification is a map of the problem, not a measurement of how likely or costly it is.

ILLUSTRATIVE · Five risks at one company
  1. A warehouse IT system fails → Operational Risk (Technology/Process Disruption).
  2. A competitor changes long-term customer demand → Strategic Risk (Sales Markets).
  3. A borrower stops paying → Financial → Credit → Credit Default Risk.
  4. An exchange-rate move changes an invoice's value → Financial → Market Price → FX Risk.
  5. A contractual requirement is broken → Compliance Risk (Contracts). One incident may have consequences in several groups.

Quick check: Which label appears specifically under Interest Rate Risk in slide 16?

Answer without JavaScript

Yield Curve Risk, one of the five illustrated interest-rate subtypes.

Remember
  • Entrepreneurial risk: Operational, Strategic, Financial and Compliance.
  • Financial risk: Credit, Market Price and Liquidity, with distinct subcategories.
  • Market Price Risk includes Equity, FX, Commodity and Interest Rate Risk, including relevant derivatives.
  • Watch out: categories can overlap; their source and subcategory labels still matter.

Separate external from internal risk sources

A storm is outside a company. But what if the company's reaction helps create the next market shock?

1. Exogenous: the source begins outside the firm

Exogenous risk means the source of a possible fluctuation in cash flows or asset values lies outside the business. Slide 23 treats all market risks and strategic risks as potentially exogenous, alongside catastrophe risk such as fire, storms and floods. A firm may prepare for an outside shock, but it cannot simply redesign the weather or the market. The classification describes where the source starts, not how severely the firm is affected.

Quick check: Which example is explicitly classed as exogenous on slide 23?

Answer without JavaScript

A storm. Its origin is outside the company.

2. Endogenous: the source begins inside the firm

Endogenous risk originates inside the firm and can often be reduced through process optimization, improving the way work is organized. Slide 24 names employees, management, machinery and IT, including cybersecurity, as operational examples. It also places all types of legal risk in this category. That last placement is the lecturer's classification; the underlying cause of a particular legal problem may be difficult to establish. Ask which internal action, system or procedure could prevent it.

Quick check: Which risk can the firm most directly reduce through its own process changes?

Answer without JavaScript

The recurring internal IT error. Better procedures or controls can address its source.

3. Managing an outside risk can change the risk itself

Here is the trap on slide 23: as firms manage market or strategic risk, their choices can influence the market. The originally exogenous exposure can develop an endogenous component through feedback. This complicates statistical models because outcomes now depend on how other decision-makers react. The lecturer calls it more of a game-theoretical question, meaning the parties' decisions interact. Slide 24 warns that identifying the real source of risk is not always easy.

ILLUSTRATIVE · A market-risk feedback loop
  1. A company notices an outside currency-market shock: initially exogenous.
  2. Many firms respond by selling the same exposed asset to reduce risk.
  3. The combined selling changes its price, affecting other firms' exposures.
  4. Future outcomes now depend partly on the firms' reactions: an endogenous feedback component, which is harder to model.

Quick check: Why can managing an external risk complicate risk models?

Answer without JavaScript

Because reactions can affect market conditions. The model must account for interacting decisions.

Remember
  • Exogenous risk begins outside the firm: markets, strategy or catastrophes.
  • Endogenous risk begins inside: staff, management, machinery, IT or legal sources as grouped by the slides.
  • Process optimization may reduce internal sources; collective risk management may create market feedback.
  • Watch out: risk origins can be ambiguous and can change as firms react.

Explain the US subprime mortgage crisis

If a borrower cannot repay, will selling the house always rescue the bank?

1. How risky mortgages got approved

Slide 5 introduces NINJA loans: No Income, No Job, No Assets. In the US mortgage bubble of 2007–2008, slide 6 contrasts banks' expected profit, borrowers' creditworthiness (capacity to repay, assessed by credit rating), and collateral (security supporting the loan). Its striking detail is that creditworthiness is crossed out. The lending terms shown are an initially low fixed interest rate followed by a rate linked to a market or reference rate, financing of 100% or more of the home's value, and immediate foreclosure when debtors struggle.

Quick check: What did the crossed-out label on slide 6 warn about?

Answer without JavaScript

The borrower's repayment ability. Creditworthiness was crossed out.

2. Why the collateral safety net failed

In the 2007/08 credit crisis, many debtors got into trouble and collateral values fell rapidly; the lecturer says some houses could not be sold at any price. If a loan covers all or more than the house value, the bank has little initial cushion. Falling prices then make foreclosure an unreliable rescue. The slides explicitly juxtapose return and risk: attractive anticipated profit does not remove the possibility of loss.

ILLUSTRATIVE · What 100%+ home financing exposes
  1. A house is worth €200,000; a loan of €210,000 finances 105% of that value (210,000 ÷ 200,000 × 100).
  2. Suppose the house price declines by 20%: 200,000 × 0.80 = €160,000.
  3. Compare the outstanding loan, assumed unchanged, with the home's value: 210,000 − 160,000 = €50,000.
  4. This €50,000 is a collateral shortfall, not automatically a realized bank loss. If the property cannot be sold, a sale-based recovery is even less certain.

Quick check: With the illustrative loan of €210,000 and home value €160,000, what is the gap in euros?

Answer without JavaScript

€50,000 = €210,000 − €160,000. This is a collateral shortfall, not necessarily the lender's realized loss.

3. What responsible lending must prioritize

Slide 8 asks whether the crisis could have been foreseen. Its warning: assess the debtor's future ability to pay before trusting collateral, whether material (real estate, residual-debt insurance) or personal (surety, guarantee). The lecturer recalls consumer-credit problems in Germany after reunification and cautions against financing short-term consumption, living standards or vacations with debt, a potential downward debt spiral. Foreclosure, enforcing security when repayments fail, should be a rare exception, not a routine lending strategy. The creditor–debtor relationship rests partly on trust and the debtor's willingness to pay (moral obligation). Slide 4 only references a Jonathan Jarvis video; no video content is assumed.

Quick check: What should a lender examine first?

Answer without JavaScript

The borrower's future ability to pay. Collateral does not substitute for credit analysis.

Remember
  • NINJA = No Income, No Job, No Assets; the slides' key omission was creditworthiness.
  • Low starting rates, benchmark-linked rates and 100%+ financing increased exposure.
  • Distressed borrowers and collapsing collateral undermined foreclosure as a fallback.
  • Watch out: future repayment ability, trust and willingness to pay matter more than routine seizure of collateral.

Map the Siemens KickStart certificate payoff

You pay €182.90 for a certificate. If Siemens shares collapse, who bears the loss?

1. Read the promised settlement, not the sales pitch

The KickStart-Zertifikat on Siemens was advertised by Sal. Oppenheim jr. & Cie. KGaA in Euro am Sonntag on 3 September 2000, WKN 703 379. Its selling price was €182.90; Siemens traded at €185.80 on that date. The certificate settles using the Siemens share price on 23 November 2001, not the price on the advertisement date. It pays in shares, shares plus cash, or cash alone depending on that final price.

Quick check: Which price is the certificate's purchase cost?

Answer without JavaScript

€182.90. The €185.80 figure is the Siemens share's historical price.

2. Translate the three payoff regions into a graph

Let S be the final Siemens share price in euros and P(S) the certificate's maturity value in euros. Below €180, you receive one share. Above €180 but below €220, you receive a share plus cash equal to S − €180. From €220 onward, you receive €260 cash instead of a share. The advertisement does not explicitly specify equality at exactly €180; both adjacent payoff expressions give €180 there.

Piecewise maturity payoff (from slide 12)

P(S) = S if S < €180; 2S − €180 if €180 < S < €220; €260 if S ≥ €220. At S = €180, the adjoining expressions both equal €180 (threshold interpretation).

Worked scenarios · Hypothetical expiry prices
  1. If S = €160, one share is delivered: P = €160, so profit = €160 − €182.90 = −€22.90.
  2. If S = €200, share value €200 plus €20 cash gives P = €220.
  3. Subtract the purchase cost: €220 − €182.90 = €37.10 profit (before any fees).
  4. If S ≥ €220, the certificate instead pays €260 cash; the maximum payoff gain over cost is €77.10.

Quick check: What is the maturity payoff in euros when S = €200?

Answer without JavaScript

€220: one €200 Siemens share plus €20 cash. The €37.10 investment gain is payoff minus purchase price.

3. Separate the contract from the historical chart

The issuer's warning on slide 13 says an unfavorable underlying move can cause total loss of invested capital, and directs investors to the relevant offering and listing prospectuses and risk documents. Here, if S fell to €0, the share delivered would be worthless: P = €0, a €182.90 loss before fees. The upside is capped at €260, yet the downside reaches zero. Slide 14's Yahoo Finance chart covers 2000–2025, plotting adjusted daily closing prices in EUR (roughly €0–€190). It shows a steep early-2000s fall followed by volatile recovery; it does not supply the unadjusted contractual expiry price for 23 November 2001.

Quick check: Can you read the exact certificate settlement directly from slide 14's adjusted price series?

Answer without JavaScript

No. Slide 14 shows adjusted historical prices, not an explicit contractual expiry settlement quote.

Remember
  • Paid €182.90; final Siemens share price S determines the payment on 23 November 2001.
  • Below €180: a share. Between €180 and €220: a share plus excess above €180. From €220: €260 cash.
  • Payoff and profit differ: profit = payoff − €182.90 before fees.
  • Watch out: investment can be lost completely, and an adjusted history chart is not a verified maturity fixing.

Part IV · Measure risk and judge the models

Choose measures, recognize the models' limits, and finally distinguish market-wide from share-specific risk.

Choose an appropriate risk measure

A potential €200 loss sounds scary. But how likely is it, and what question should you measure first?

1. Risk measurement asks two different questions

Risk measurement is part of broader risk management: understanding and responding to adverse outcomes. Slide 19 says measuring risk requires statistics, but managing it also means coping with uncertainty when useful probabilities cannot be assigned. The lecturer therefore insists on two dimensions: exposure, the amount at stake, and the probability of a negative event. A large amount with a low chance creates a different decision from a modest amount with a high chance. Measuring one dimension while ignoring the other leaves an incomplete picture.

Quick check: What two dimensions does the lecturer require?

Answer without JavaScript

Exposure (amount at stake) and event probability.

2. Pick the measure that answers your question

Slide 22 distinguishes Worst Case Analysis, a non-stochastic method requiring no probability model, from statistical approaches. Variance and standard deviation measure the variability of outcomes; for shares, the lecturer calls standard deviation volatility. Value-at-Risk (VaR) links a potential loss threshold to a fixed time horizon and a chosen probability. Shortfall Probability, especially the Probability for Loss, measures the chance of missing a target; Lower Partial Moments also focus on below-target outcomes. The last group reflects a subjective risk definition, because the chosen target matters.

Quick check: Which approach does not require probabilities?

Answer without JavaScript

Worst Case Analysis. It is the non-stochastic method listed on slide 22.

3. The same outcomes yield several risk measures

Use the following ILLUSTRATIVE one-period profits from an investment with €1,000 initially at stake. Assume five equally likely scenarios, each with 20% probability; these figures are invented for learning, not quoted from the lecture. We can calculate losses, variability and the chance of missing either a €0 break-even target or a €50 profit target. Notice that choosing a different target changes shortfall probability, even while the outcomes stay fixed.

ILLUSTRATIVE · Work through four measures
  1. Worst Case: lowest profit is −€200; largest loss among these scenarios is €200.
  2. Variability: mean = (−200 − 100 + 0 + 100 + 200) / 5 = €0; variance = (40,000 + 10,000 + 0 + 10,000 + 40,000) / 5 = 20,000 €²; standard deviation = √20,000 ≈ €141.42.
  3. Shortfall: two of five outcomes lose money, so probability of loss = 40%. Three profits fall below €50, so the shortfall probability for that target is 60%.
  4. 80% VaR: ordered losses are −200, −100, 0, 100, 200 euros. Four of five are at most €100, so this illustrative one-period 80% VaR is €100. The remaining scenario loses €200; the slide also names Lower Partial Moments for below-target deviations.
Fact check: Slide 22 describes VaR as a “maximum loss” at a given probability and horizon. VaR is a probability-based threshold, not an absolute limit: outcomes can exceed it.

Quick check: What percentage of scenarios falls below a €50 profit target?

Answer without JavaScript

60%. Three of five equally likely outcomes miss a €50 profit target.

Remember
  • Evaluate both the size of exposure and the probability of adverse events.
  • Worst case, spread, VaR and shortfall measure different aspects of risk.
  • Changing the target changes the shortfall measure.
  • Watch out: VaR is not a worst-case ceiling, and management must also handle uncertainty.

Identify three kinds of model risk

Your model looks sensible and its inputs are current. Can it still miss a market crash?

1. Even a good model cannot rule out rare events

Model risk exists because no risk measurement approach is an exact picture of reality. Slide 20 calls the first category stochastic risk: the model structure suits the decision and its parameters reflect reality, yet an extreme event can still happen with very small probability. The model need not be defective for a rare outcome to occur. Slide 21 uses a market crisis as the example. A low probability is never the same thing as impossibility.

Quick check: A market crisis occurs despite a suitable, well-calibrated model. Which risk is illustrated?

Answer without JavaScript

Stochastic risk. Rare events remain possible even when the model and its inputs fit reality.

2. Separate changing inputs from a wrong model structure

Parameter risk means the model structure is suitable but its parameters do not reflect changes in reality. Slide 20 mentions mortality trends in Western populations and changing stock-market volatility; slide 21 adds technology changes, including high-frequency trading, that alter market dynamics. Structural risk is different: the chosen model form is unsuitable from the beginning. The lecturer says grades are not normally distributed and warns that stock-price returns can have heavy tails, meaning extreme losses occur more often than a normal-distribution model predicts.

ILLUSTRATIVE · Diagnose a risk model step by step
  1. A correctly specified model assigns a very small chance to a crash, which then occurs. Diagnose stochastic risk, not necessarily a modelling error.
  2. A valid volatility model keeps yesterday's parameter while market dynamics change. Diagnose parameter risk.
  3. A model assumes normal stock returns even though the observed extreme-loss pattern has heavy tails. Diagnose structural risk.
  4. Ask the exam question: “Was it a rare outcome, a stale input, or an unsuitable model?” These are the lecturer's three distinct cases.

Quick check: A volatility model has suitable structure, but its estimates no longer match today's market. Which type?

Answer without JavaScript

Parameter risk. The model form works, but changing market conditions make its parameters stale.

3. Risk controls can amplify the danger they target

Slide 20 adds a separate warning: when many market participants manage risk uniformly, their common response can create a self-fulfilling prophecy. For example, if many firms sell after the same warning signal, their combined selling can push prices down further. Thus risk management itself may increase overall market risk. This feedback effect is not a fourth member of the three-part model-risk taxonomy; it is a caution about how models and users interact.

Quick check: Why can identical risk controls across many firms be dangerous?

Answer without JavaScript

Similar reactions can reinforce the original market movement and increase overall risk.

Remember
  • Stochastic: a rare event occurs despite a realistic model.
  • Parameter: the model is suitable, but inputs no longer match changing reality.
  • Structural: the model form itself is unsuitable, for example missing heavy tails.
  • Watch out: widespread identical risk responses can magnify losses through feedback.
